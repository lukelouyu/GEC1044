# Tong Keow Medicine and Soup Guide

## How to use this guide

This guide supports the presentation and speaker script. The “uses” and “symptoms” below describe traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) functions, product-label categories or associations reported by the shop staff. They are not biomedical efficacy claims or treatment advice.

The interview did not identify the exact brands or ingredient labels for every packaged product. For those products, the guide states that the formula is unknown rather than guessing. In Singapore, the Health Sciences Authority requires Chinese proprietary medicines and medicated oils to declare their active ingredients on the label.

## Hougang demographic context

The shop is located at Block 809 Hougang Central. Singapore’s 2020 Census recorded 227,560 residents in the wider Hougang Planning Area. About 81.2% were Chinese, 8.2% Malay, 8.2% Indian and 2.3% from other ethnic groups. About 54.0% were aged 40 or above, including 16.2% aged 65 or above.

The smaller Hougang Central subzone had 4,720 residents. About 87.5% were Chinese, 54.7% were aged 40 or above, and 16.7% were aged 65 or above. These figures help explain why a neighbourhood medicine hall can serve a substantial Chinese-speaking and middle-aged or older customer base. This is an interpretation of the demographic context, not proof of who shops at Tong Keow.

### Why ban zhi lian and other specialist herbs have lower retail demand

Ban zhi lian differs from familiar food-like herbs such as red dates, goji berries and Chinese yam. Customers do not commonly add it to routine household soup, and its traditional functions apply to narrower patterns. The staff member also explained that it normally needs to be paired with other herbs. Choosing that combination requires knowledge of the customer’s overall pattern, appropriate preparation and dosage.

Demand is therefore reduced by several practical barriers. The herb is less familiar, its purpose is harder to explain in one short retail claim, and customers may need to prepare a decoction. The absence of a clearly confirmed in-house physician also limits opportunities for individual prescription. By comparison, packaged teas, capsules and soup packs already provide a recognisable purpose, measured ingredients and simple instructions.

Strong tonic formulas (大补方) may face a different barrier. The interview suggested that many Singapore customers prefer *qing bu* (清补), or light nourishment, because of the hot and humid climate. Lower demand therefore reflects suitability and convenience as much as the medical status of the product.

## Soup slide: ingredients and combination logic

The interview did not establish five fixed or ranked soup prescriptions. It named six ingredients that customers commonly combine in soups.

| Soup ingredient | Main role in the mixture | Traditional symptom pattern or need |
|---|---|---|
| Dang shen (党参) | Gentle qi-supporting base | Fatigue, weakness, poor appetite or recovery |
| Huang qi (黄芪) | Stronger qi-supporting partner | Tiredness, spontaneous sweating or a sense of low resistance |
| Yu zhu (玉竹) | Moistening component | Dry throat, thirst or dry cough |
| Chinese yam (淮山) | Food-like digestive base | Poor appetite, loose stools or digestive weakness |
| Red dates (红枣) | Blood-nourishing and harmonising addition | Fatigue or weakness; also softens the taste of the soup |
| Goji berries (枸杞子) | Small nourishing addition | Dry or tired eyes, dizziness or general maintenance |

The combination can be explained through three functions:

- Dang shen and huang qi form the qi-supporting pair for fatigue and weakness.
- Yu zhu adds moisture when dryness, thirst or throat discomfort is emphasised.
- Chinese yam supports digestion, while red dates and goji berries make the mixture more food-like and suitable for routine household use.

The shop described the Singapore preference as *qing bu* (清补), or light nourishment. This means customers tend to favour mild, food-like and moistening combinations rather than rich “major tonic” formulas in a hot and humid climate. The presentation should describe these as traditional dietary practices, not treatments for diagnosed disease.

## Presentation wording to avoid overclaiming

Say:

- “Traditionally used in TCM for...”
- “The staff associated this product with...”
- “Customers may choose it for symptoms described as...”
- “Exact ingredients vary by brand, so the label is needed.”

Avoid saying:

- “This herb cures...”
- “This product boosts immunity” as a proven biomedical effect
- “This was the top-selling item” without sales records
- “The five most popular soup therapies,” because the interview supplied common ingredients rather than five verified recipes
