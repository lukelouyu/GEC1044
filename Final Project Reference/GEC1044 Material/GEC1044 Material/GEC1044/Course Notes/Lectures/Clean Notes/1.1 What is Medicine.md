## Core Question

The lecture begins with a basic puzzle: **if human beings are biologically similar across cultures, why are there so many different systems of medicine?** Western biomedicine exists alongside [[Chinese Medicine]], Ayurveda, Malay medicine, Jamu, African medical traditions, homeopathy, chiropractic, osteopathic medicine, and others. The purpose of studying these systems is **not to decide which medicine is “better,” but to understand how different cultures construct, interpret, and trust medical knowledge**.

## Medicine, Culture & Trust

People visit doctors because they **trust a particular interpretation of illness**. Choosing a Western clinic or a TCM clinic therefore involves more than treatment; it reflects beliefs about what counts as reliable knowledge. In early 20th-century China, some intellectuals rejected Chinese medicine because concepts such as **yin-yang and the Five Phases** appeared metaphysical or superstitious compared with Western science. This reflected the wider influence of **scientism**: the belief that modern science represented progress and that legitimate knowledge should conform to its standards.

This raises an important historical question: **why did Western medicine acquire such strong authority and credibility?**

## Foucault: The Clinic & the “Medical Gaze”

[[Michel Foucault]], in _The Birth of the Clinic_, argued that modern Western medicine emerged together with new institutions and forms of professional authority around the late 18th century. Hospitals and clinics became centres where illnesses could be systematically **observed, compared, studied, and taught**. Pathological anatomy and examination of the body strengthened the idea that disease could be discovered through scientific observation.

Foucault calls this way of seeing the patient the **medical gaze**. The doctor separates the biological body from the patient's wider personal identity and selects medically relevant information from everything the patient experiences. This creates specialised medical knowledge and language that ordinary people may not understand, increasing the authority of medical professionals.

> [!important] Medical Gaze  
> The **medical gaze** treats the patient's body as an object that can be observed, examined and interpreted by trained medical experts.

For Foucault, our trust in modern medicine is therefore connected not only to scientific discoveries but also to the **institutions, professional expertise and power structures** surrounding modern medicine.

## Medical Theory Is More Than Observation

Observation is fundamental to medicine. Doctors can examine appearance, facial colour, the tongue, smells, bodily sounds, organs and other physical signs. Modern medicine extended this principle through autopsy, pathological anatomy and increasingly detailed examination of the internal body.

However, **observation alone cannot fully explain how the body functions**. For roughly two millennia, both Chinese and Western medical traditions constructed theories that went beyond what could directly be seen. To make sense of the body, people used models derived from their **life experiences, environment, society and understanding of nature**.

Therefore, medical theories are closely connected to the social and cultural environments in which they develop. A medical theory becomes convincing when it connects observable structures of the body with a broader model explaining how life and the world function.

## The Living Person: **Body + X**

A major problem in medical thought is the distinction between a **material body** and whatever makes that body alive. Historically, many cultures assumed that a living human was more than visible physical matter.

The lecture expresses this as:

**Life = Body + X**

**X** represents an invisible or intangible component of life. Different traditions have described it using concepts such as **spirit, soul, psyche**, or in Chinese thought, ideas connected with [[Qi]]. The existence or exact nature of X is not established here; the important point is that human cultures have repeatedly used such concepts to explain the difference between a living body and a dead one.

This also allowed illness to be understood in two dimensions. The **body may be sick**, preventing normal physical activity, while **X may also be considered disordered** even when the visible body appears normal—for example when a person's behaviour or mental state prevents ordinary functioning.

## From Symptoms to Invisible Disease

Early understandings of illness often focused on whether a person could carry out normal daily activities. Medicine later developed the ability to distinguish between **visible symptoms and underlying disease**. A fever, for example, may be observable, but it is a sign of another process rather than necessarily being the disease itself.

The _[[Shiji|Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji)]]_ story discussed in the lecture illustrates this principle: a trained physician identifies an illness before the patient or surrounding people can perceive it. The physician can do this because medicine provides a **theory of bodily processes** that allows experts to interpret invisible or early changes and predict their development.

> [!important] What makes medical expertise possible?  
> A physician does not merely **see more** than an ordinary person; the physician possesses a **theoretical framework for interpreting what is seen**.

## Medicine vs Healing

[[Healing]] is much older and broader than medicine. Humans have attempted to restore health since prehistoric times through practices including prayer, exorcism, massage and substances believed to affect the body.

**Medicine is only one form of healing.** According to the framework used in this lecture, healing becomes medicine when it develops a **systematic theoretical and interpretive framework based on assumed laws of nature**. Medicine tries to explain why normal and abnormal states arise, how they develop, and how they can be prevented or treated.

> [!important] Definition of Medicine  
> **Medicine is the endeavour to understand the origins and development of normal and abnormal states of the body and “X”, in order to promote normal states, prevent abnormal states, alleviate their effects, and ultimately preserve life.**

Healing therefore existed long before medicine. The lecture places the emergence of systematic medicine around the **5th–4th centuries BCE in Europe**, with medicine developing in China approximately **two to three centuries later from older healing traditions**.

## Key Takeaway

**Medicine is not simply a collection of treatments.** It is a **theoretical system for interpreting the human body, life, health and illness**. Because these theories are shaped partly by different cultural, social and historical environments, biologically similar humans can develop very different medical systems.

**Central idea:** `Observation → Interpretation through theory → Diagnosis → Prediction → Treatment`

This provides the foundation for the next question: **how did medicine emerge from earlier healing traditions in ancient China?**
