3. Local
Products
Singapore grows few medicinal herbs because the climate is less suitable, so
local cultivation offers little advantage. Most raw materials come from China,
which provides greater variety and generally lower prices.
Locally linked examples:
• Cactus ( 仙⼈掌 ) and roselle ( 洛神花 )
• Patent medicines ( 中成药 ) 
• Five flower and Luo Han guo teas ( 五花茶∕罗汉果茶 )
Many ingredients are imported, then processed and packaged in Singapore.
Localisation therefore happens mainly through quality selection, preparation,
packaging and convenience.

3. Soup
Therapies
Common soup combinations:
• Dang shen ( 党参 ) — qi ( ⽓ ), digestion and immunity
• Huang qi ( ⻩芪 ) — qi ( ⽓ ) and daily strengthening
• Yu zhu ( ⽟⽵ ) — nourishing and moistening
• Chinese yam ( 淮⼭ ) — spleen and stomach support
• Red dates + goji ( 红枣＋枸杞 ) — nourishment and
wellness
Without distinct four seasons, Singapore’s hot, humid climate
favours qing bu ( 清补 ; light nourishment), cooling soups and
lung-moistening ingredients rather than strong tonics. Soup
makes TCM part of ordinary family meals.

4. Less-Popular
Products
The interview did not produce a verified bottom-three
ranking. The clearest examples were:
• Ban zhi lian ( 半枝莲 ) — bought less often
• Specialist herbs that require combination with other
ingredients
• Strong tonic formulas ( ⼤补⽅ ), which are less
suited to Singapore’s hot, humid climate
Low demand was linked to preparation effort,
unfamiliarity and the need for guidance.

4. Medical
Practices
An in-house physician was not clearly confirmed. Staff described commonsymptom–remedy pairings:• Colds — Yin Qiao-type remedies ( 银翘类 )• Diarrhoea — Huoxiang Zhengqi-type remedies ( 藿⾹正⽓类 )• Fatigue / qi deficiency ( ⽓虚 ) — ginseng ( ⼈参 ) or dang shen ( 党参 )• Lower immunity — Lingzhi and other supplements ( 灵芝保健品 )• Late nights, long work hours and stress — liver-care or mood-supportproducts ( 养肝∕疏肝解郁 )• Aches / injuries — red flower oil or Red Oil Essence ( 红花油∕红油精 ), often bought by older customers
Professional diagnosis should be distinguished from self-selected retailremedies.

5. Analysis: TCM in
Singapore
• Preventive care: TCM supports daily maintenance, not only illness.
• Generational pattern: customers are mainly middle-aged or older; younger buyers
choose fewer raw herbs.
• Convenience shift: younger customers prefer teas, supplements and ready-made
products.
• Local adaptation: ingredients are largely imported from China, then selected,
processed and packaged locally.
• Lifestyle pressures: long work hours, phone use, late nights and stress create demand
for liver-care, mood-support and immunity products.
• Cultural reach: Chinese customers dominate; Malay customers often buy dessert
ingredients, while Indian customers often buy nuts.
• Trust and quality: the shop prioritises sulphur-free herbs, fresh colour and visible
freshness.

PAGE 11
6. Conclusion
1. TCM remains embedded in everyday food, wellness and family care.
2. Singapore favours light tonics, soup packs and convenient products.
3. Imported ingredients are locally adapted through processing and packaging.
4.Local TCM products try to accommodate modern needs, such as safety,
taste, and side-effect concerns. It especially caters to the younger
generation and people of all races. 
Our field visit to Tong Keow shows how traditional practices can fit into daily
routines in Singapore.
