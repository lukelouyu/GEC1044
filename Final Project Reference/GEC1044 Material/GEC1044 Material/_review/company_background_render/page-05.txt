5 Localisation through products packaging and cultural contact
5.1 Imported materials and locally made products
Lecture 5 treats localisation as adaptation to climate, available materials and society. At Tong Keow, 
it appears at several production stages. Staff described imports mainly from China, citing variety and 
cost, with limited local cultivation. The presentation retained cactus (仙人掌), roselle (洛神花), 
patent medicines, Five Flower Tea (五花茶) and monk-fruit tea (罗汉果茶) (Table A3). 
Branded packs also give herbs a recognisable retail form. Staff described local processing and 
packing. Our interpretation centres on the visible selection and portioning of ingredients, named uses 
and local branding. Packaging actively shapes the product encountered by shoppers. Table A3 
distinguishes cultivation, manufacture and packaging to clarify each example’s connection to 
Singapore.
Local manufacture and assembly of finished Chinese Proprietary Medicines (CPM) occupy a formal 
place in Singapore’s supply chain. The Health Sciences Authority (HSA) requires relevant dealer 
licences and product listing, with facilities subject to licensing and Good Manufacturing Practice 
requirements (HSA, 2026c, 2026d). The capsule example connects the shelf to this framework. Raw 
herbs, foods and soup packs have their respective product categories.
5.2 Packaging and the language of trust
Packaging identifies a product’s contents and source. HSA’s CPM guidelines require full English 
labelling, corresponding information in additional languages, and product and ingredient details. 
Batch, expiry and relevant manufacturer, importer or assembler information are also required (HSA, 
2025a, section 5). These requirements shape the box shoppers encounter. Product listing is separate 
from an assessment of therapeutic efficacy (HSA, 2026b), a distinction that matters when reading 
health claims.
Quality labels offer another form of communication. Staff emphasised fresh colour and sulphur-free 
materials; our photograph shows a sulphur-free Dang Shen label and a separate American ginseng 
label (Figure 3). We interpret these claims about quality and identity as invitations to trust the shop’s 
goods. HSA cautions against treating “natural” as a safety guarantee (HSA, 2026a), a necessary limit 
on that reading. These practices will attract younger customers by reassuring them about product 
quality and addressing their concerns about potential side effects. 
5.3 Different shoppers in a shared space
At Tong Keow, staff reported that most customers were Chinese, but Malay customers came to buy 
dessert ingredients and Indian customers came to buy nuts. These everyday food purchases give 
different communities familiar reasons to enter the same medicine hall. Eu Yan Sang offers a useful 
comparison: its halal product category shows how a business can meet different consumers’ needs 
(Eu Yan Sang, n.d.). The Tong Keow account illustrates a different route to reaching different 
communities: ordinary food shopping.
