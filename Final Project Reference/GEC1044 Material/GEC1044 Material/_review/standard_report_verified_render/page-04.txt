4 Convenience and changing consumption forms
4.1 Easier preparation for the convenience of younger buyers
Staff described younger customers as buying fewer raw herbs and preferring teas, supplements and 
simpler preparations. Table A2 retains eight presentation categories, including ginseng tonics, herbal 
teas, soup packs, mood-support products and medicated oils. This account directs attention to product 
form: staff associate easier preparation with younger customers’ routines.
Soup packs make this demand for convenience visible. Because ingredients are pre-selected and 
portioned, households can bypass raw processing and begin directly with cooking. While staff 
explain the intended use of specific ingredients, buyers complete the final preparation at home. 
Finished capsules require even less effort, whereas the photographed multi-buy promotion frames 
soup packs through a familiar supermarket retail offer. Ultimately, we interpret convenience as a 
structural redistribution of labor between the seller and the household, making traditional materials 
significantly easier to choose and incorporate into modern routines. 
4.2 Comparative industry examples
Industry comparisons describe deliberate changes in how herbal goods are offered. Nanyang 
Polytechnic discusses Heng Foh Tong’s herbal-drink redesign for millennials (Nanyang Polytechnic 
[NYP], n.d.). Amacha markets TCM-inspired milk teas; a founder interview reported over 150,000 
cups sold in eight months (Amacha, n.d.; Angelynn T., 2026). Teck Soon’s owner reported a 30% 
sales increase after moving herbs from drawers to storefront displays in 1982 (Chin, 2024). These are 
promotional, journalistic or institutional public-relations accounts, not peer-reviewed studies. Their 
value lies in the specific strategies described: changing product forms and making herbs easier to 
encounter. They give context to Tong Keow’s prepared packs and staff account of convenience.
4.3 Products for daily pressures
Staff associate modern daily pressures, such as late nights, long work hours and prolonged phone use, 
with demand for liver-care or mood-support products. This commercial alignment is exemplified by 
remedies designed for soothing Liver-related constraint (疏肝解郁), effectively bridging traditional 
therapeutic vocabulary with contemporary lifestyle ailments.
A comparative survey places this retail language in a wider setting. Reporting on ADP’s 2023 survey, 
Kumari (2024) reports that 68% of Singapore workers experience work-related stress at least weekly, 
compared with 61% in the surveyed Asia-Pacific markets; 12% reported daily stress. This survey 
provides context for the staff’s descriptions of strain. We interpret the shop’s product language as 
making TCM relevant to familiar pressures and daily routines, supporting our argument that 
traditional goods acquire new everyday uses through adaptation.
