1 Introduction

[P00029 | 601:883 | NORMAL_TEXT]
At Tong Keow’s Hougang outlet, herbs and soup packs sit alongside everyday foods. Staff described purchases for nourishment, throat comfort, fatigue and daily routines. We argue that this retail setting keeps Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) visible and usable in household life.

[P00030 | 883:1066 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The report examines food therapy, convenience and local adaptation through displays, staff accounts and public sources, without assessing clinical effectiveness or sales performance.

[P00031 | 1066:1114 | HEADING_1]
2 Fieldwork and the neighbourhood medicine hall

[P00032 | 1114:1149 | HEADING_2]
2.1 How we conducted the fieldwork

[P00033 | 1149:1387 | NORMAL_TEXT]
We visited Tong Keow on 13 September 2026, photographed displays and interviewed one staff member. Transcripts, the presentation script, and photographs inform this report; we paraphrase unclear meanings where transcripts contain errors.

[P00034 | 1387:1592 | NORMAL_TEXT]
We distinguish observations, staff accounts and our interpretations. Published sources provide context, and course concepts guide analysis. Demand descriptions reflect the interviewee’s retail experience.

[P00035 | 1592:1633 | HEADING_2]
2.2 Tong Keow and its mixed retail model

[P00036 | 1633:1934 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Tong Keow’s profile describes a medicine hall founded in Toa Payoh in 1968 and now run by second-generation family owners. Promotional materials feature American ginseng, monk fruit, cooling teas, pills and plasters (Tong Keow Medical Hall, 2022; “Instagram Reel About Tong Keow Medical Hall,” n.d.).

[P00037 | 1934:2166 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The company advertises registered-physician consultations, acupuncture and cupping for indigestion, colds and sprains (“Instagram Reel About Tong Keow Medical Hall,” n.d.). We did not verify these services or observe consultations.

[P00038 | 2166:2331 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Branded soup packs beside herbs, grains and foods connect the branch to the wider business and allow shoppers to encounter herbal products during grocery purchases.

2.3 Hougang and the customers described by staff

[P00041 | 2383:2765 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff described mainly Chinese, middle-aged and older customers, including people in their forties and fifties. Hougang had 228,140 planning-area residents in June 2024; HDB reported 57,557 flats and 165,450 residents as of 31 March 2024. These totals cover different populations (Singapore Department of Statistics [DOS], 2024; Housing & Development Board [HDB], 2024, pp. 11–12).

[P00042 | 2765:3233 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Hougang Central had about 4,620 residents in June 2025, with roughly 1,070 aged 65 or above (23%) (DOS, 2025). Chua et al.’s (2025) 647 Hougang respondents averaged 69 years of age and 22 years’ residence. The area also has Teochew heritage around Tou Mu Kung, fishing, farming and rubber-growing (National Heritage Board [NHB], n.d.). This established residential setting helps explain the reported clientele, but may not represent outlets in younger neighbourhoods.

3 Everyday care and food therapy

[P00045 | 3269:3298 | HEADING_2]
3.1 Herbs in household meals

[P00046 | 3298:3594 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff named Dang Shen (党参), Huang Qi (黄芪), Yu Zhu (玉竹), Huai Shan (淮山), red dates (红枣) and goji (枸杞) as common soup ingredients. Ginseng (人参), American ginseng (西洋参) and monk fruit (罗汉果) were also selected by neighbourhood customers. Table A1 lists these nine staff-described best-selling herbs.

[P00047 | 3594:4167 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Soup gives herbs a familiar household purpose. Lecture 5 describes Chinese medicine as a cultural framework shaping everyday food and health habits (GEC1044 Lecture 5, n.d.). Nourishing life (养生) and medicine and food sharing a source (药食同源) help explain how the display connects meals with health maintenance. Lim et al. (2005) identify health maintenance as an important reason for dietary herb use. Our fieldwork illustrates this through cooking: households encounter traditional ingredients as part of preparing familiar meals, rather than only when seeking treatment.

[P00048 | 4167:4204 | HEADING_2]
3.2 Soup packs and light nourishment

[P00049 | 4204:4733 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Table A2 records five soup labels: Qing Bu Liang (清补凉), Dang Shen–Huang Qi (党参北芪补气汤), Yu Zhu–Huai Shan (玉竹淮山润燥汤), goji–red date (枸杞红枣养生汤) and Five Finger Fig (五指毛桃健脾汤). Staff linked them with aiding digestion (助消化), moistening (润燥) and light nourishment (qing bu / 清补). Light nourishment offers an everyday dietary approach alongside stronger tonification (da bu / 大补). Pre-packaged ingredients translate these ideas into household practice: the shop selects and portions the materials, while buyers still cook the soup at home.

[P00050 | 4733:5144 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Several soups, especially Five Finger Fig, emphasise Spleen/digestive support. The TCM Spleen refers to a broader functional system, rather than the anatomical organ (WHO, 2022). However, the labels also describe moistening and general nourishment, so the range should not be reduced to one organ system. Their differing purposes show how similar cooking formats can communicate distinct traditional-use ideas.

[P00051 | 5144:5198 | HEADING_2]
3.3 Singapore weather and the appeal of lighter soups

[P00052 | 5198:5596 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff described monk fruit and lighter soups as popular, linking this perceived demand to Singapore’s environment. Meteorological Service Singapore describes high temperatures, heavy rainfall and humidity throughout the year, with monsoon and inter-monsoon periods (Meteorological Service Singapore [MSS], n.d.). This differs from the four temperate seasons underlying classical seasonal guidance.

[P00053 | 5596:6250 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The Suwen links health maintenance to adapting to seasonal cycles (Unschuld & Tessenow, 2011, pp. 45–57). In that framework, Yu Zhu is associated with autumn dryness (qiu zao / 秋燥): it nourishes Yin, moistens dryness and generates fluids, particularly for Lung and Stomach Yin. Singapore has no equivalent four-season cycle, so the shop’s explanations apply these ideas through local conditions. Yu Zhu appears in the recorded Yu Zhu–Huai Shan soup, which staff described through gentle moistening and fluid replenishment. This illustrates a change in the language and setting of use, without establishing that climate determines individual suitability.

4 Convenience and changing ways of using products

[P00056 | 6303:6345 | HEADING_2]
4.1 Easier preparation for younger buyers

[P00057 | 6345:6583 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff described younger customers preferring teas, supplements and simpler preparations over raw herbs. Table A3 lists eight product categories, including ginseng tonics, herbal teas, soup packs, mood-support products and medicated oils.

[P00058 | 6583:6956 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Preselected soup packs transfer selection and portioning to sellers while households retain cooking; capsules reduce preparation further. Staff explained ingredient uses, and a multi-buy promotion presented packs through a familiar supermarket offer. Convenience therefore changes how preparation work is shared, helping traditional materials fit younger buyers’ routines.

[P00059 | 6956:6989 | HEADING_2]
4.2 Products for daily pressures

[P00060 | 6989:7545 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff linked late nights, long work hours and prolonged phone use to demand for liver-care or mood-support products. Nature’s Green Mind Soothe Capsules use the language of soothing Liver-related constraint (疏肝解郁) and are listed by HSA as a Singapore-manufactured Chinese Proprietary Medicine (CPM) (Health Sciences Authority [HSA], 2025b). Such descriptions connect TCM terminology with familiar concerns about stress and mental well-being. These are retail associations, rather than clinical diagnoses or evidence that the products treat mental illness.

[P00061 | 7545:8106 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Kumari’s (2024) report on ADP’s 2023 survey found weekly work-related stress among 68% of Singapore workers, compared with 61% across surveyed Asia-Pacific markets. This gives broader context to the shop’s product language, although it does not measure Tong Keow customers’ reasons for purchase. Together with Section 4.1, the staff account suggests that lifestyle pressures and convenience help explain demand for packaged products: familiar concerns are expressed through traditional terms, while capsules offer a preparation format that fits daily routines.

[P00062 | 8106:8171 | HEADING_2]
4.3 Preparation effort and staff-described lower-demand products

[P00063 | 8171:8839 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Lower-demand examples included Ban Zhi Lian (半枝莲), specialist herbs requiring combinations and strong tonic formulas (大补方) (Table A4). Staff associated their lower appeal with unfamiliarity and preparation effort, and considered stronger tonics less suitable for Singapore’s hot, humid climate than lighter qing bu (清补) options. Slower-selling items were stocked in smaller quantities. This comparison helps explain the appeal of prepared packs: shoppers receive selected, portioned ingredients instead of assembling combinations themselves. It also suggests that staff guidance and preparation services matter alongside the ingredients’ traditional-use descriptions.

[P00064 | 8839:8907 | HEADING_1]
5 Local adaptation through products, packaging and cultural contact

[P00065 | 8907:8956 | HEADING_2]
5.1 Imported materials and locally made products

[P00066 | 8956:9572 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff reported mainly Chinese imports for variety and cost, with limited local cultivation. Table A5 separates three meanings of “local”: grown, packaged or manufactured in Singapore. Cactus and roselle illustrate possible local cultivation, but shop-stock origins remain unconfirmed. Mind Soothe Capsules have a documented Singapore manufacturing link. Five Flower Tea and monk-fruit tea are external examples of local packaging and beverage production, not Tong Keow products. These distinctions illustrate adaptation through Singapore’s retail and production systems, even with imported ingredients (Yang, 2022).

[P00067 | 9572:10244 | NORMAL_TEXT]
HSA lists Nature’s Green Mind Soothe Capsules as manufactured by Tong Jum Chew in Singapore, without establishing ingredient origins (HSA, 2025b). Product categories also have different requirements. Finished Chinese Proprietary Medicines (CPM) require listing and dealer licences; manufacturing and assembly facilities must meet Good Manufacturing Practice requirements (HSA, 2026b, 2026c). Raw or simply processed medicinal herbs are Chinese medicinal materials, while permitted ingredients in culinary soups or drinks for non-medicinal use are food products (Singapore Food Agency, 2025). Local manufacture therefore does not determine a product’s regulatory category.

[P00068 | 10244:10284 | HEADING_2]
5.2 Packaging and the language of trust

[P00069 | 10284:10867 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Packaging makes product information visible to shoppers. CPM labels require English; Chinese may be added, while other languages must match approved English. Product names, ingredients, batch numbers, expiry dates and manufacturer, importer or assembler details help identify products and trace their supply chains (HSA, 2025a, section 5). These details can support trust, but HSA listing concerns safety and quality, rather than proving effectiveness or individual suitability (HSA, 2026b). Consumer guidance also highlights possible interactions with other medicines (HSA, 2026d).

[P00070 | 10867:11445 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Staff emphasised fresh colour and sulphur-free materials. Photographs show HSA signage, a sulphur-free Dang Shen claim and a separate American ginseng label. The Dang Shen label makes a processing claim; the ginseng label identifies the product. These messages may reassure shoppers, although photographs cannot verify the claims. HSA also warns against describing supplements as safe simply because they are natural (HSA, 2026a). Packaging therefore helps explain how trust is communicated, while the accuracy of claims and suitability for a buyer require separate assessment.

5.3 Different shoppers in a shared space

[P00073 | 11491:11926 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The staff member described most customers as Chinese, adding that some Malay customers bought dessert ingredients and some Indian customers bought nuts. These everyday foods may give different communities familiar reasons to enter a medicine hall without requiring shared knowledge of TCM. The mix of foods and medicinal products suggests that the shop can serve overlapping household purposes, rather than a single cultural practice.

[P00074 | 11926:12393 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Course material on senna shows that the same plant can have different meanings in Chinese and Malay medicine (GEC1044 Lecture 5, n.d.). This provides a way to interpret the shared space: access to similar materials need not imply identical understandings of health or use. However, purchases alone cannot establish cultural exchange or shoppers’ beliefs. Customer interviews would be needed to understand whether people regard the products as food, medicine or both.

[P00075 | 12393:12439 | HEADING_1]
6 Retail adaptation and individual assessment

[P00076 | 12439:12885 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Food gives herbs a household purpose, prepared packs reduce effort, and packaging communicates reported uses. Local branding and confirmed manufacture connect products with Singapore’s retail setting. Unschuld (2018) describes medicine adapting to social and political environments; Yang (2022) traces Singapore’s modernisation through state, professional and religious influences. Together, these accounts frame the shop’s everyday adaptations.

[P00077 | 12885:13426 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Retail access remains distinct from treatment. PolyU’s reference profile lists ginseng as neutral (Table A1) and American ginseng as cool and Yin-nourishing; staff associations between fatigue and tonics (Table A6) cannot establish suitability. Pattern differentiation (辨证论治) requires fuller assessment of signs and symptoms (WHO, 2022). Nearly 40% of TCM users in the 2022 National Population Health Survey also used Western medicine for the same condition (Ministry of Health [MOH], 2025), situating household purchases within wider care.

[P00078 | 13426:13440 | HEADING_1]
7 Limitations

[P00079 | 13440:13931 | NORMAL_TEXT]
One visit and interview cannot establish demand trends or customer interpretations. Complete recipes, sourcing and purchasing records were unavailable. Mind Soothe has a confirmed Singapore manufacturing link, but ingredient origins remain unknown; staff named the category rather than the brand. Physician presence was unclear, and no consultation, prescription or outcome was recorded. Practitioner regulation provides background, without verifying an outlet physician’s role (MOH, 2026).

[P00080 | 13931:14104 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Reference profiles and external examples are distinguished from field observations. Sales records, customer interviews and branch comparisons would strengthen the analysis.

[P00081 | 14104:14117 | HEADING_1]
8 Conclusion

[P00082 | 14117:14482 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Tong Keow keeps traditional ingredients approachable through household foods, preparation services and recognisable packaging. Soup displays connect herbs with cooking and light nourishment; portioned packs reduce household effort. Local branding and confirmed manufacture link products with Singapore’s setting, while mixed foods create access across communities.

[P00083 | 14482:14752 | NORMAL_TEXT]
These adaptations support everyday visibility and opportunities for use, rather than demonstrating clinical effectiveness. Retail access and clinical care remain distinct: treatment requires a qualified practitioner to assess the person’s pattern of signs and symptoms.
