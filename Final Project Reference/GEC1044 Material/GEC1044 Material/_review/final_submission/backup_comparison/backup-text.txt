1 Introduction
At Tong Keow’s Hougang outlet, herbs and packaged soups are sold alongside everyday foods.
Staff described purchases for nourishment, throat comfort, fatigue, and needs linked to daily
routines. These displays and staff accounts show how Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) fits into
ordinary shopping and cooking. This is the focus of our report.
We argue that Tong Keow helps keep traditional ingredients visible and usable in household
routines. We do not assess clinical effectiveness or sales performance. We describe how displays,
staff accounts and public sources help explain everyday access to TCM. Section 3 explains how
food gives herbs a familiar purpose. Section 4 looks at how prepared products make them easier to
use. Section 5 considers how the products fit Singapore’s production, rules and cultural setting.
Section 6 brings these findings together and explains why treatment needs an assessment of each
person. Section 7 summarises staff-described reasons for purchase, and Sections 8 and 9 present
the limitations and conclusion.
2 Fieldwork and the neighbourhood medicine hall
2.1 How we conducted the fieldwork
We visited Tong Keow on 13 September 2026, photographed the displays and interviewed one staff
member. This report uses the transcripts, presentation script and photographs. Where the transcripts
contain errors, we restate the clear meaning in our own words. All staff accounts in this report come
from this interview.
We distinguish what the staff member said from what our photographs show. We also make clear
when we offer our own interpretation. Published sources provide historical and industry context, and
course concepts guide our discussion. Descriptions of popular and lower-demand products reflect
the staff member’s retail experience.
2.2 Tong Keow and its mixed retail model
According to its company profile, Tong Keow Medical Hall is a TCM shop and clinic with roots in Toa
Payoh. The profile states that it was founded in 1968 and is now run by the second generation of
family owners. Its promotional materials describe raw herbs, such as American ginseng and monk
fruit, alongside cooling teas, pills and medicinal plasters (Tong Keow Medical Hall, 2022; “Instagram
Reel About Tong Keow Medical Hall,” n.d.).
The company advertises consultations with registered TCM physicians, acupuncture and cupping for
concerns such as indigestion, common colds and joint sprains (“Instagram Reel About Tong Keow
Medical Hall,” n.d.). We did not independently verify these advertised services. Our Hougang visit
focused on retail displays and staff accounts. We did not record a consultation or clinical
prescription.
Our photographs show branded soup packs alongside herbs, grains and other foods. The branding
links the branch to the wider business. We see this mixed range as an important part of the shop’s
everyday role because shoppers can come across herbal products during an ordinary trip to buy
food.

2.3 Hougang and the customers described by staff
Staff described customers as mainly Chinese, middle-aged and older, including people in their forties
and fifties. Hougang planning area had 228,140 residents in June 2024. HDB separately reported
57,557 dwelling units and 165,450 residents in its Hougang flats as of 31 March 2024. These totals
cover different housing populations (Singapore Department of Statistics [DOS], 2024; Housing &
Development Board [HDB], 2024, pp. 11–12).
Hougang Central, the subzone around the outlet, had about 4,620 residents in June 2025. Official
rounded age-group totals show that around 1,070 residents were aged 65 or above, or roughly 23%
(DOS, 2025). The 647 Hougang respondents in Chua et al.’s (2025) study had an average age of 69
and had lived in the area for an average of 22 years. At the same time, the Hougang region has a
rich Teochew history. The National Heritage Board (NHB) describes the community around Tou Mu
Kung and its fishing, farming and rubber-growing activities (National Heritage Board [NHB], n.d.).
The residential setting in Figure 2.3a and heritage image in Figure 2.3b provide context for the
shop’s mixed range of medicinal products and foods. This relatively older local profile may help
explain why staff described mainly middle-aged and older customers, so our observations may not
fully represent outlets in younger neighbourhoods.

3 Everyday care and food therapy
3.1 Herbs in household meals
Staff named Dang Shen (党参), Huang Qi (黄芪), Yu Zhu (玉竹), Huai Shan (淮山), red dates (红枣),
and goji (枸杞) as common soup ingredients. Ginseng (人参), American ginseng (西洋参) and monk
fruit (罗汉果) are common herbs selected by neighbourhood customers. Table A1 lists these nine
best-selling herbs.
Soup gives herbs a familiar household purpose. Lecture 5 describes Chinese medicine as a cultural
framework that shapes everyday food and health habits (GEC1044 Lecture 5, n.d.). This helps us
understand the display through the ideas of nourishing life (养生) and medicine and food sharing a
source (药食同源). Lim et al. (2005) identify health maintenance as an important reason for the use
of herbs in daily food intake. Our fieldwork shows one way this happens: herbs enter the household
through cooking.

3.2 Soup packs and light nourishment
Our field survey grouped the soup products under five labels: Qing Bu Liang (清补凉), Dang
Shen–Huang Qi soup (党参北芪补气汤), Yu Zhu–Huai Shan soup (玉竹淮山润燥汤), goji–red date
soup (枸杞红枣养生汤), and Five Finger Fig soup (五指毛桃健脾汤). As recorded in Table A2, staff
linked these preparations to specific reported purposes, such as aiding digestion (助消化),
moistening (润燥), and light nourishment (qing bu / 清补). In TCM, light nourishment can be
presented as an everyday dietary option alongside stronger tonification (da bu / 大补). Pre-packaged
ingredient packs make these ideas easier to use because households receive preselected, portioned
ingredients and still complete the cooking at home.
Several of these soups, especially Five Finger Fig soup, are framed around Spleen/digestive
support. The TCM Spleen refers to a broader functional system rather than the anatomical organ
(WHO, 2022). However, the five labels also include moistening and general nourishment purposes,
so they should not all be reduced to a single organ system.

3.3 Singapore weather and the appeal of lighter soups
Staff described monk fruit and lighter soups as popular and linked this perceived popularity to
Singapore’s environment. Meteorological Service Singapore describes high temperatures, heavy
rainfall and humidity throughout the year, with monsoon and inter-monsoon periods (Meteorological
Service Singapore [MSS], n.d.), and no distinct four seasons like China, where TCM originates.
Classical texts such as the Suwen (素问) link health maintenance to adapting to seasonal cycles
(Unschuld & Tessenow, 2011, pp. 45–57). Singapore does not have the four temperate seasons, so
these principles are applied differently. In a traditional seasonal framework, Yu Zhu (玉竹) is
associated with autumn dryness (qiu zao / 秋燥): it nourishes Yin, moistens dryness and generates
fluids, and is used to protect Lung and Stomach Yin. In the shop’s Singapore setting, Yu Zhu
appears in light-nourishment soups such as the recorded Yu Zhu–Huai Shan soup. Staff described
its use in terms of gentle moistening and fluid replenishment.

4 Convenience and changing ways of using products
4.1 Easier preparation for younger buyers
Staff described younger customers as buying fewer raw herbs and preferring teas, supplements and
simpler preparations. Table A3 lists the top eight selling products, including ginseng tonics, herbal
teas, soup packs, mood-support products and medicated oils. This account highlights product form
because staff link easier preparation to younger customers’ routines.
Soup packs show how products meet this preference for convenience. The ingredients are already
preselected and portioned, so households can skip the initial preparation of raw herbs and start
cooking. Staff explained the intended use of specific ingredients, and buyers finish preparing the
soup at home. Finished capsules need even less effort. The photographed multi-buy promotion also
presents soup packs through a familiar supermarket offer. We see convenience as a change in how
the work is shared between the seller and the household. The shop takes on more of the selection
and preparation, making traditional ingredients easier to choose and use in daily routines.

4.2 Comparative industry examples
Industry examples describe changes in how herbal products are sold in Singapore. Nanyang
Polytechnic describes Heng Foh Tong’s redesign of herbal drinks for millennials (Nanyang
Polytechnic [NYP], n.d.). Amacha markets TCM-inspired milk teas, and a founder interview reported
more than 150,000 cups sold in eight months (Amacha, n.d.; Angelynn T., 2026). Teck Soon’s owner
reported a 30% increase in sales after moving herbs from drawers to storefront displays in 1982
(Chin, 2024). The Teck Soon example is a historical analogy only; its setting differs from current
prepared packs and online retail. Figures 4.2a–c illustrate these different retail formats. Overall,
these examples suggest that some of Singapore’s local Chinese medicine shops have adapted

4.3 Products for daily pressures
Staff linked daily pressures, such as late nights, long work hours and prolonged phone use, to
staff-reported demand for liver-care or mood-support products. Products marketed with the language
of soothing Liver-related constraint (疏肝解郁)—such as the pictured Nature’s Green Mind Soothe
Capsules, listed by HSA as a Singapore-manufactured Chinese Proprietary Medicine (HSA,
2025b)—show how retail TCM responds to everyday concerns about stress and mental well-being.
These product descriptions connect TCM terms with everyday concerns about stress. They should
not be read as a clinical diagnosis or evidence that the products treat mental illness.
A survey provides broader context for this retail language. In a report on ADP’s 2023 survey, Kumari
(2024) states that 68% of Singapore workers experienced work-related stress at least weekly,
compared with 61% across the surveyed Asia-Pacific markets. We see the shop’s product language
as connecting TCM to familiar concerns and routines. Together with Section 4.1, this suggests that
lifestyle pressures, as well as convenience, help explain the staff-described shift towards packaged
products.
4.4 Preparation effort and staff-described lower-demand products
Staff-described lower-demand examples also help explain the appeal of simpler product forms.
These include Ban Zhi Lian (半枝莲), specialist herbs requiring combinations, and strong tonic
formulas (大补方) (Table A4). Staff linked their lower appeal to unfamiliarity and preparation effort,
and noted that stronger tonics may be less suitable for Singapore’s hot and humid climate than
lighter qing bu (清补) options. Slower-selling items were stocked in smaller quantities. These

5.1 Imported materials and locally made products
Yang (2022) examines how Chinese medicine has developed within Singapore’s social and
institutional setting. Table A5 considers local adaptation through cultivation, packaging and

manufacture. Staff at Tong Keow reported that most imports came from China because of variety
and cost, while local cultivation was limited. The five examples are cactus (仙人掌), roselle (洛神花),
Mind Soothe Capsules (舒肝解郁胶囊), Five Flower Tea (五花茶) and monk-fruit tea (罗汉果茶).
Cactus and roselle illustrate possible local cultivation, although the origin of the shop’s stock remains
unverified. Mind Soothe Capsules provide a documented Singapore manufacturing link. The
branded Five Flower Tea and monk-fruit drink examples are external industry comparisons
illustrating local packing and beverage manufacture; they are not products documented at Tong
Keow. This distinction allows the table to compare different forms of local involvement without
treating ingredient origin, packing and manufacture as interchangeable.
Finished Chinese Proprietary Medicines (CPM), including capsules and tablets, require the relevant
dealer licences and product listing for sale and supply in Singapore (HSA, 2026b). Local
manufacturing and assembly facilities must also meet HSA’s Good Manufacturing Practice
requirements (HSA, 2026c). HSA’s dataset lists Mind Soothe Capsules (舒肝解郁胶囊), under the
Nature’s Green (绿叶) brand, with Tong Jum Chew Pte Ltd as manufacturer and Singapore as the
manufacturing country (HSA, 2025b). This establishes a manufacturing link, not the origin of every
ingredient. Under Singapore’s regulatory classification, raw or simply processed herbs intended for
traditional medicinal use fall within Chinese medicinal materials; when permitted materials are
incorporated into culinary soup packs or herbal drinks for non-medicinal use, the finished items are
classified as food products. Their classification therefore depends on their form and intended use
(Singapore Food Agency, 2025).

5.2 Packaging and the language of trust
Packaging identifies a product’s contents and source. HSA’s Chinese Proprietary Medicines
guidelines require full English labelling. Chinese may be added; information in other languages must
match the approved English labelling. Labels must provide product and ingredient details, batch and
expiry information, and the relevant manufacturer, importer or assembler details (HSA, 2025a,
section 5). These requirements make the product and its supply chain more traceable. Product
listing addresses safety and quality requirements (HSA, 2026b), while HSA’s consumer guidance
also notes possible interactions with other medicines (HSA, 2026d). For this report, traceable
product information supports an analysis of how trust is communicated; it does not establish
suitability or treatment effectiveness for an individual shopper.
Quality labels communicate a further kind of assurance. The staff we interviewed stressed fresh
colour and sulphur-free materials. Figure 5.2a shows HSA signage, while Figure 5.2b shows a
sulphur-free Dang Shen label and a separate American ginseng label. The Dang Shen label
presents a processing claim, while the American ginseng label identifies the product; together, these
labels show how specific wording may reassure shoppers. The photograph records these claims
without verifying them. In its separate guidance on health supplements, HSA also prohibits
suggesting that a product is safe simply because it is natural (HSA, 2026a). The distinction between

5.3 Different shoppers in a shared space
The staff member described most customers as Chinese, adding that some Malay customers bought
dessert ingredients and some Indian customers bought nuts. This account suggests that everyday
food purchases may give different communities familiar reasons to enter the shop. Course materials
on senna also show that the same plant can have different meanings in Chinese and Malay
medicine (GEC1044 Lecture 5, n.d.). This offers one way to interpret the shared shopping space, but
customer interviews would be needed to understand how shoppers themselves view the products.
6 Bringing the findings together through pattern differentiation and adaptation
6.1 Retail advice and pattern differentiation
Table A1 presents ginseng as neutral and American ginseng as cool and Yin-nourishing. These
different profiles show why retail suggestions cannot replace individual assessment. Staff linked
fatigue with tonic use (Table A6), but this is a retail association rather than a clinical
recommendation. Choosing between these herbs for treatment requires a fuller assessment of signs
and symptoms through pattern differentiation and treatment (辨证论治) (WHO, 2022). The ginseng
comparison makes the principle clear: the appropriate choice depends on the person’s full pattern.
TCM use also often sits alongside other care. MOH reported that nearly 40% of TCM users in the
2022 National Population Health Survey used TCM and Western medicine for the same condition
(Ministry of Health [MOH], 2025). This gives context to the medicine hall’s everyday role: a purchase
for nourishment or household care may be one part of a wider health routine.

6.2 How the shop brings TCM into everyday life
The findings suggest how several retail features can make traditional ingredients visible and easier
to use. Food gives herbs a household purpose, prepared packs reduce selection and preparation
effort, and packaging explains reported uses. Local branding, and in one confirmed case local
manufacture, link the examples to Singapore’s retail setting. These features support opportunities for
everyday use.
Unschuld’s (2018) account of adaptation and Yang’s (2022) history help explain these links.
Unschuld argues that Chinese medicine has changed with its social and political environment. Yang
shows that TCM in Singapore became more modern, more formally organised and more locally
adapted through the influence of the state, medical professionals and religious organisations. These
histories provide a lens for interpreting the shop’s retail practices. Our fieldwork illustrates how
traditional ingredients are presented through familiar foods and prepared products.

8 Limitations
This report draws on one visit and one staff interview. Accounts of demand, customer preferences
and cross-community purchases come from staff. Sales records, customer interviews and branch
comparisons would allow fuller assessment. Complete soup recipes, sourcing and purchasing
records were unavailable. The pictured Nature’s Green capsules have a confirmed Singapore
manufacturing link, but ingredient origins remain unknown; staff named the product category rather
than the brand. It was unclear whether a physician was present, and we recorded no consultation,
prescription or patient outcome. MOH (2026) provides background on the registration framework for
TCM practitioners; it does not confirm a physician’s presence or role at this outlet.
The reference tables and captions distinguish reference profiles and external images from field
photographs. Customer interviews and further visits would help develop this account.
9 Conclusion
Tong Keow presents traditional ingredients and prepared products within a medicine hall that also
sells everyday foods. Soup displays link herbs to cooking and staff-described light nourishment.
Prepared packs leave selection and portioning to the seller, so households can begin with cooking.
Packaging communicates reported uses, while local branding—and in one confirmed case local
manufacture—connects products to Singapore’s retail setting. The mixed food range also gives
different communities familiar reasons to enter. Together, these features support an account of
everyday visibility and opportunity for use.
The Hougang shop illustrates how retail adaptation can keep traditional ingredients visible and
usable in household routines. Food provides a household purpose, convenience reduces
preparation effort, and local adaptation gives products a recognisable form. These practices create
opportunities for different ethnic communities and age groups to encounter TCM. Retail access,
however, is not treatment advice. Clinical treatment requires a qualified practitioner to assess the
person’s pattern of signs and symptoms. Making products approachable and providing clinical care
are therefore separate but connected parts of the wider practice of care.
