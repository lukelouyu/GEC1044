3.2 Soup packs and light nourishment
The presentation grouped soups under five labels: Qing Bu Liang 清补凉, Dang Shen–Huang Qi 
soup 党参北芪补气汤, Yu Zhu–Huai Shan soup 玉竹淮山润燥汤, goji–red date soup 枸杞红枣养
生汤 and Five Finger Fig soup 五指毛桃健脾汤. Table A4 records their named ingredients and 
connects familiar preparations to specific forms of care.
Staff associated these soups with digestion, moistening and light nourishment, 清补. Light 
replenishment offers an everyday approach alongside stronger tonification, 大补. Named packs make 
this vocabulary practical: buyers can take a preparation home to cook. In the context of these pre￾selected soup packs, the Spleen and Liver do not merely represent anatomical structures, but specify 
broader TCM functional systems that dictate how light nourishment formulations support digestion 
and metabolic balance (World Health Organization [WHO], 2022). We interpret the packs as 
bringing these ideas into meals.
3.3 Singapore weather and the appeal of lighter soups
Staff linked monk fruit’s popularity and lighter soups to Singapore’s environment. Meteorological 
Service Singapore describes high temperatures, abundant rainfall and humidity throughout the year, 
with monsoon and inter-monsoon periods (Meteorological Service Singapore [MSS], n.d.). This gives 
climatic context to the presentation’s phrase “without four distinct seasons” and the staff account of 
local demand.
The Suwen (素问) reading relates seasonal habits to maintaining health (Unschuld & Tessenow, 
2011, pp. 45–57). We interpret the staff’s discussion of lighter soups as environmental reasoning 
expressed in retail advice. Singapore’s weather supplies a local context for explaining their appeal. 
The ingredient profiles make this account more specific: Yu Zhu （玉竹）, for example, is 
associated with moistening dryness. Climate provides context for the staff account, while the named 
ingredients retain their particular TCM functions.
