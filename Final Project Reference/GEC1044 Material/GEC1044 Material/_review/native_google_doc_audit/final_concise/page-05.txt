5 Local adaptation through products, packaging and cultural contact
5.1 Imported materials and locally made products
Yang (2022) situates Chinese medicine within Singapore’s social and institutional development. Staff
reported mainly Chinese imports for variety and cost, with limited local cultivation. Table A5
distinguishes cultivation, packing and manufacture through cactus, roselle, Mind Soothe Capsules,
Five Flower Tea and monk-fruit tea. Cactus and roselle illustrate possible local cultivation, although
shop-stock origins remain unverified. Mind Soothe has a documented manufacturing link; the tea
examples illustrate external industry packing and beverage manufacture, rather than Tong Keow
products.
HSA lists Nature’s Green Mind Soothe Capsules, made by Tong Jum Chew in Singapore, without
establishing ingredient origins (HSA, 2025b). Finished CPM require dealer licences and listing, with
manufacturing/assembly facilities meeting Good Manufacturing Practice requirements (HSA, 2026b,
2026c). Regulatory classification depends on form and intended use: raw or simply processed
medicinal herbs are Chinese medicinal materials, while permitted ingredients in non-medicinal
culinary soups or drinks form food products (Singapore Food Agency, 2025).
5.2 Packaging and the language of trust
CPM require English labelling; Chinese may be added, while other languages must match approved
English. Ingredient, product, batch, expiry and manufacturer/importer/assembler details make supply
chains traceable (HSA, 2025a, section 5). Listing addresses safety and quality, and consumer
guidance notes medicine interactions (HSA, 2026b, 2026d). Traceability helps explain
communicated trust, without establishing individual suitability or effectiveness.
Staff emphasised fresh colour and sulphur-free materials. Photographs show HSA signage, a
sulphur-free Dang Shen claim and a separate American ginseng label. The first label communicates
processing; the second identifies the product. These messages may reassure shoppers, but
photographs cannot verify them. HSA also warns against presenting supplements as safe simply
because they are natural (HSA, 2026a). Label messages and established product properties
therefore need distinction.
