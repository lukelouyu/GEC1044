3 Everyday care and food therapy
3.1 Herbs in household meals
Table A1 records nine staff-described popular herbs: Dang Shen, Huang Qi, Yu Zhu, Huai Shan, red
dates, goji, ginseng, American ginseng and monk fruit. The first six commonly enter household
soups.
Soup gives herbs a familiar purpose. Lecture 5 frames Chinese medicine through everyday food and
health habits, including nourishing life (养生) and medicine–food continuity (药食同源) (GEC1044
Lecture 5, n.d.). Lim et al. (2005) identify health maintenance as a reason for herbs’ dietary use; our
observations illustrate this through cooking.
3.2 Soup packs and light nourishment
Table A2 records Qing Bu Liang (清补凉), Dang Shen–Huang Qi, Yu Zhu–Huai Shan, goji–red date
and Five Finger Fig soups. Staff associated them with digestion, moistening and light nourishment
(清补), alongside stronger tonification (大补). Preselected, portioned packs make these ideas
practical while households complete the cooking.
Five Finger Fig particularly emphasises Spleen/digestive support. The TCM Spleen is a functional
system, rather than the anatomical organ (WHO, 2022). Other soups emphasise moistening or
general nourishment, so the range should not be reduced to one organ system.
3.3 Singapore weather and the appeal of lighter soups
Staff linked monk fruit and lighter soups’ popularity to Singapore’s environment. High temperatures,
humidity and rainfall persist through monsoon and inter-monsoon periods, rather than four temperate
seasons (Meteorological Service Singapore [MSS], n.d.).
