8 Limitations
This report draws on one visit and one staff interview. Accounts of demand, customer preferences
and cross-community purchases come from staff. Sales records, customer interviews and branch
comparisons would allow fuller assessment. Complete soup recipes, sourcing and purchasing
records were unavailable. The pictured Nature’s Green capsules have a confirmed Singapore
manufacturing link, but ingredient origins remain unknown; staff named the product category rather
than the brand. It was unclear whether a physician was present, and we recorded no consultation,
prescription or patient outcome. MOH (2026) provides background on the registration framework for
TCM practitioners; it does not confirm a physician’s presence or role at this outlet.
The appendix separates reference profiles and external images from field photographs. Customer
interviews and further visits would help develop this account.
9 Conclusion
Tong Keow presents traditional ingredients and prepared products within a medicine hall that also
sells everyday foods. Soup displays link herbs to cooking and staff-described light nourishment.
Prepared packs leave selection and portioning to the seller, so households can begin with cooking.
Packaging communicates reported uses, while local branding—and in one confirmed case local
manufacture—connects products to Singapore’s retail setting. The mixed food range also gives
different communities familiar reasons to enter. Together, these features support an account of
everyday visibility and opportunity for use.
The Hougang shop illustrates how retail adaptation can keep traditional ingredients visible and
usable in household routines. Food provides a household purpose, convenience reduces
preparation effort, and local adaptation gives products a recognisable form. These practices create
opportunities for different ethnic communities and age groups to encounter TCM. Retail access,
however, is not treatment advice. Clinical treatment requires a qualified practitioner to assess the
person’s pattern of signs and symptoms. Making products approachable and providing clinical care
are therefore separate but connected parts of the wider practice of care.
