3.3 Singapore weather and the appeal of lighter soups
Staff described monk fruit and lighter soups as popular, linking this perceived demand to Singapore’s
environment. Meteorological Service Singapore describes high temperatures, heavy rainfall and
humidity throughout the year, with monsoon and inter-monsoon periods (Meteorological Service
Singapore [MSS], n.d.). This differs from the four temperate seasons underlying classical seasonal
guidance.
The Suwen links health maintenance to adapting to seasonal cycles (Unschuld & Tessenow, 2011,
pp. 45–57). In that framework, Yu Zhu is associated with autumn dryness (qiu zao / 秋燥): it
nourishes Yin, moistens dryness and generates fluids, particularly for Lung and Stomach Yin.
Singapore has no equivalent four-season cycle, so the shop’s explanations apply these ideas
through local conditions. Yu Zhu appears in the recorded Yu Zhu–Huai Shan soup, which staff
described through gentle moistening and fluid replenishment. This illustrates a change in the
language and setting of use, without establishing that climate determines individual suitability.
4 Convenience and changing ways of using products
4.1 Easier preparation for younger buyers
Staff described younger customers preferring teas, supplements and simpler preparations over raw
herbs. Table A3 lists eight product categories, including ginseng tonics, herbal teas, soup packs,
mood-support products and medicated oils.
Preselected soup packs transfer selection and portioning to sellers while households retain cooking;
capsules reduce preparation further. Staff explained ingredient uses, and a multi-buy promotion
presented packs through a familiar supermarket offer. Convenience therefore changes how
preparation work is shared, helping traditional materials fit younger buyers’ routines.
4.2 Products for daily pressures
Staff linked late nights, long work hours and prolonged phone use to demand for liver-care or
mood-support products. Nature’s Green Mind Soothe Capsules use the language of soothing
Liver-related constraint (疏肝解郁) and are listed by HSA as a Singapore-manufactured Chinese
Proprietary Medicine (CPM) (Health Sciences Authority [HSA], 2025b). Such descriptions connect
TCM terminology with familiar concerns about stress and mental well-being. These are retail
associations, rather than clinical diagnoses or evidence that the products treat mental illness.
