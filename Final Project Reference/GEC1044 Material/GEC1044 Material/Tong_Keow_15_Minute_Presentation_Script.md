# Traditional Chinese Medicine in Singapore
## 15-Minute Presentation Script: Tong Keow Healthcare Fieldwork

**Target length:** about 15 minutes  
**Evidence note:** Product uses below are presented as staff-reported uses or TCM concepts, not as biomedical efficacy claims. The interview did not provide documentary sales records or a verified ranking, so “best-selling” items are described as commonly mentioned by the staff member.

---

## Slide 1 — Title
### Person 1 | About 30 seconds

Good afternoon. Our presentation examines Traditional Chinese Medicine, or TCM (中医药), in contemporary Singapore through our field visit to Tong Keow Healthcare in Hougang.

Rather than asking whether TCM is simply effective or ineffective, we approached the medicine hall as a cultural and commercial space. We wanted to understand how inherited medical knowledge is translated into products, food practices and everyday choices—and how those practices are adapted to Singapore’s climate, population and modern lifestyle.

Our main argument is that TCM in Singapore survives not by remaining unchanged, but by being continuously localised.

---

## Slide 2 — Introduction, Hougang Demographics and Fieldwork Context
### Person 1 | About 1 minute 35 seconds

We visited Tong Keow Healthcare at Block 809 Hougang Central on 13 September 2026. The outlet is a neighbourhood Chinese medicine hall (中药行) selling dried herbs, soup ingredients, packaged remedies, health supplements and other dried goods.

The local population helps explain the setting. In the 2020 Census, the wider Hougang Planning Area had about 227,560 residents. Around 81 per cent were Chinese, 54 per cent were aged 40 or above, and 16 per cent were aged 65 or above. Hougang Central itself was about 88 per cent Chinese. These figures do not identify Tong Keow’s customers, but they show the neighbourhood context in which the shop operates.

Our method combined direct observation, photographs of the displays and a semi-structured interview with a shop staff member. We asked about commonly purchased herbs and products, soup therapies, customer groups, sourcing and quality standards.

One boundary must be stated: this is a retail medicine hall, and we did not confirm an in-house registered TCM physician (中医师). We therefore treat the interview as retail knowledge rather than clinical diagnosis or prescription.

---

## Slide 3 — Best-Selling Herbs
### Person 2 | About 1 minute 35 seconds

The interview did not give us audited sales figures or a reliable ranking from one to eight. What we have is staff recollection, so these should be treated as commonly mentioned popular herbs rather than a statistically verified list.

The staff member mentioned ginseng (人参) and American ginseng (西洋参／花旗参), dang shen (党参), huang qi (黄芪), yu zhu (玉竹), Chinese yam (淮山), red dates (红枣), goji berries (枸杞子) and monk fruit (罗汉果).

Each herb has a more specific role. Ginseng root strongly replenishes qi and is traditionally used for marked weakness or exhaustion. American ginseng also replenishes qi, but it nourishes yin and generates fluids, so it is associated with tiredness accompanied by thirst, dryness or heat. Dang shen is a gentler qi tonic linked to fatigue and poor appetite, while huang qi is associated with tiredness, spontaneous sweating and support for the body’s exterior.

Yu zhu nourishes yin and moistens dryness, so the traditional symptom examples are dry throat, thirst and dry cough. Chinese yam supports digestion and is associated with poor appetite or loose stools. Red dates nourish qi and blood, while goji berries nourish the liver and kidneys and are linked with dry or tired eyes. Monk fruit clears heat, moistens the lungs and soothes the throat, making it relevant to dry throat, hoarseness and dry cough.

So, the popularity of these herbs reflects a preventive orientation: people buy them not only when seriously ill, but to manage fatigue, digestion, heat and general wellbeing.

## Slide 4 — Popular Products
### Person 2 | About 1 minute 35 seconds

Popular products showed how the shop makes traditional knowledge convenient. Categories included ginseng tonics (人参／西洋参), monk-fruit products (罗汉果), teas (花茶／凉茶), soup packs (药材汤包), Lingzhi supplements (灵芝保健品), liver-care and mood-support products (养肝／疏肝解郁), Yin Qiao-type cold remedies (银翘类), red flower oil (红花油) and Red Oil Essence (红油精).

Their ingredients and intended uses are not identical. Ginseng tonics centre on ginseng or American ginseng and target fatigue or weakness. Monk-fruit drinks target dry throat and hoarseness. Lingzhi products contain Ganoderma extract or powder and are traditionally associated with qi support, poor sleep, cough or shortness of breath.

Yin Qiao formulas centre on honeysuckle flower (金银花) and forsythia fruit (连翘). TCM uses them for early wind-heat symptoms such as sore throat, feverishness, headache and mild cough. Teas, soup packs, liver-care products and mood-support medicines have no single standard formula, so their labels must supply the ingredients.

The interview did not record the formulas of the two medicated oils. The staff associated them with external use for bruises, sprains and muscular or joint aches, especially among older customers.

Packaging reduces preparation and attracts younger customers who prefer teas and supplements. It also creates a tension: pattern differentiation (辨证论治) considers the individual, while a packaged product gives many customers the same fixed formula.

## Slide 5 — Local Products and Supply Chains
### Person 3 | About 1 minute 25 seconds

When we asked about local production, the staff member emphasised that Singapore grows very few medicinal herbs. The main reason given was climate, together with the limited advantage of cultivating herbs locally. China offers a much larger supply and generally lower prices.

This means that “local TCM” does not necessarily mean herbs grown in Singapore. Many raw ingredients are imported from China and then selected, processed, formulated or packaged locally. Examples connected to Singapore included patent medicines (中成药), supplements, flower teas and herbal teas. Cactus (仙人掌) and roselle (洛神花) were also mentioned as possible locally linked plants, although the exact production chain was not independently verified.

The local contribution therefore lies mainly in quality control, formulation and packaging. The staff member emphasised colour, freshness and herbs without sulphur treatment. Imported materia medica (中药材) becomes Singapore retail TCM through local selection, explanation and convenience.

## Slide 6 — Soup Ingredients and Light Nourishment
### Person 3 | About 2 minutes 10 seconds

Soup therapy was one of the clearest examples of TCM entering everyday family life. An important correction is that the interview named six common ingredients, not five fixed or ranked soup prescriptions.

The first pair is dang shen (党参) and huang qi (黄芪). Both are roots used to replenish qi. Dang shen is the gentler, food-like base associated with fatigue, weakness and poor appetite. Huang qi adds a stronger qi-supporting function and is traditionally associated with tiredness and spontaneous sweating.

Yu zhu (玉竹) is a rhizome that nourishes yin and moistens dryness. It fits symptom patterns such as dry throat, thirst or dry cough. Chinese yam, or huai shan (淮山), is the digestive component. It supports the spleen and stomach and is traditionally linked with poor appetite, loose stools and digestive weakness.

Red dates (红枣) and goji berries (枸杞子) are smaller additions. Red dates replenish qi and nourish blood while also softening the taste of the soup. Goji berries nourish the liver and kidneys and are traditionally associated with dry or tired eyes and general maintenance.

Together, dang shen and huang qi support qi, yu zhu moistens, and Chinese yam supports digestion. Red dates and goji berries make the mixture more food-like for regular household cooking.

The important concept here is qing bu (清补), or “light nourishment.” The staff member explained that Singapore does not have the four sharply distinct seasons found in parts of China. Because the local climate is consistently hot and humid, customers tend to prefer mild, food-like and moistening soups rather than very rich tonic formulas (大补方).

The practice adapts Chinese medical ideas about climate and diet to tropical conditions. It also connects food therapy (食疗) with yangsheng (养生), or cultivating health through ordinary habits. Prepacked soup bags simplify the knowledge while keeping it within family cooking.

## Slide 7 — Less-Popular Products
### Person 4 | About 1 minute 55 seconds

The interview did not provide a reliable bottom-three sales ranking, so we should not claim that we identified the three objectively least popular products.

The clearest named example was ban zhi lian (半枝莲), the dried aerial parts of *Scutellaria barbata*. In TCM, practitioners classify it as a herb that clears heat and removes toxin (清热解毒), resolves blood stasis (化瘀) and promotes urination (利尿). These are technical functions within a pattern-based system. They do not mean that the herb cures a particular biomedical disease, and it is not an ordinary daily tonic like red dates or goji berries.

This helps explain its lower demand. First, ban zhi lian has a specialised purpose. Customers cannot easily match it to a familiar everyday need such as fatigue, throat discomfort or soup preparation. Second, the staff member said it normally needs to be combined with other herbs. The correct combination depends on the person’s overall pattern, so customers may need guidance from a trained practitioner. Third, it is less convenient. A customer must recognise the herb, understand the preparation method and usually make a decoction, while a tea bag, capsule or soup pack already provides a clear format and simple instructions.

The shop also reported less demand for specialist herbs in general and for very strong tonic formulas (大补方). Strong tonics may feel unsuitable for customers seeking light nourishment in Singapore’s hot and humid climate. Therefore, lower sales do not necessarily mean that a medicine is ineffective or unimportant. They often mean that it has a narrower use, requires more knowledge and is harder to prepare without clinical guidance. This reflects a wider commercial shift from specialised materia medica (中药材) towards products that are standardised, familiar and convenient.

## Slide 8 — Medical Practices and the Retail–Clinical Boundary
### Person 4 | About 3 minutes

Because we could not confirm an in-house physician, we cannot present the staff member’s comments as prescription data. They are retail associations between common symptoms and product categories.

The first example was Yin Qiao-type remedies (银翘类). The name refers to the two principal herbs: honeysuckle flower, jin yin hua (金银花), and forsythia fruit, lian qiao (连翘). Classical versions also include herbs such as peppermint, burdock fruit, balloon flower and licorice. In TCM, this formula corresponds to an early wind-heat pattern (风热), with symptoms such as a sore throat, feverishness, headache, thirst and a mild cough. It should not be described as suitable for every type of cold because TCM distinguishes wind-heat from other patterns such as wind-cold.

The second example was Huoxiang Zhengqi (藿香正气). Its main herb is huo xiang (藿香), commonly translated as patchouli. Traditional formulas combine it with ingredients such as perilla leaf, citrus peel, pinellia, magnolia bark, poria and licorice. The combination is intended to transform dampness and regulate digestion. The relevant symptom cluster includes nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, poor appetite and abdominal fullness. Again, this is a specific TCM dampness pattern rather than a general treatment for every cause of diarrhoea.

For fatigue or qi deficiency (气虚), the staff mentioned ginseng (人参) and dang shen (党参). Ginseng is viewed as the stronger qi tonic, while dang shen provides gentler support for fatigue, weakness and poor appetite. Lingzhi (灵芝), made from a Ganoderma fungus, was mentioned as a supplement for tiredness, poor sleep and lower perceived immunity.

The staff also connected late nights, long working hours and heavy phone use with liver-care (养肝) or mood-support products described as soothing liver constraint (疏肝解郁). In TCM, “liver constraint” can describe stress, irritability, poor sleep or a feeling of tension. It does not automatically mean that the customer has biomedical liver disease. The interview did not record the exact products, so we cannot state their ingredients.

Finally, red flower oil (红花油) and Red Oil Essence (红油精) were associated with bruises, sprains and muscular or joint aches among older customers. These are topical products. Their exact formulas vary by brand, and the interview did not photograph their ingredient labels, so we should not assume that red flower oil contains safflower as its main ingredient simply because of its name.

These examples show the retail-clinical boundary. A shop can help customers recognise familiar product categories, but a practitioner uses pattern differentiation (辨证论治). The practitioner considers the person’s age, constitution, diet and combination of symptoms before selecting and adjusting a formula. A fixed retail product cannot reproduce that individual assessment, so the medicine hall provides accessible knowledge without functioning as a substitute for a clinic.

## Slide 9 — Analysis: What Does This Tell Us About TCM in Singapore?
### Person 5 | About 2 minutes 15 seconds

Our findings point to five patterns. First, TCM operates as everyday preventive care. Herbs and soups address tiredness, digestion, heat and general maintenance. This reflects yangsheng (养生), where health is cultivated through repeated habits.

Second, convenience is reshaping knowledge. Younger customers prefer teas, supplements and ready-made products. Packaging makes TCM easier to buy, but may reduce individual explanation.

Third, Singapore TCM is locally adapted. Imported ingredients are selected, packaged and explained in relation to local quality expectations, climate and lifestyles.

Fourth, the shop combines cultural continuity with wider commercial reach. Chinese customers formed the largest reported group, while Malay customers bought ingredients for desserts and Indian customers bought nuts. These are staff observations, not fixed ethnic behaviours.

Fifth, trust now combines inherited uses with freshness, packaging, sulphur-free processing and regulation. Tong Keow remains relevant because it translates tradition into forms suited to contemporary Singapore.

---

## Slide 10 — Conclusion
### Person 5 | About 50 seconds

To conclude, our fieldwork produced three main takeaways.

First, TCM remains embedded in food, wellness and family care. Soup therapy and everyday herbs blur a strict boundary between medicine and ordinary nourishment.

Second, Singapore’s tropical climate and fast-paced lifestyle shape demand. Light nourishment, cooling products, convenient teas, supplements and packaged soup ingredients are more accessible than strong tonics or specialist herbs.

Third, Singapore TCM is import-dependent but locally adapted. Its distinctiveness comes from how ingredients are selected, processed, packaged, explained and trusted within the local setting.

Tong Keow therefore illustrates our main argument: tradition survives through adaptation. The medicine hall preserves inherited ideas, but it also responds to modern work stress, generational change, multi-ethnic customers, regulation and consumer convenience.

Thank you.
