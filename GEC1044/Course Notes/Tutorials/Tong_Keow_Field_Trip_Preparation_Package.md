# 1. Research Purpose and Scope

## 1.1 Purpose

This project investigates Tong Keow Healthcare as a contemporary Singapore Chinese medicine hall: a neighbourhood retail site where materia medica, proprietary products, dried foods, soup therapies, health knowledge and digital delivery may intersect. It examines the place and business rather than evaluating therapeutic efficacy.

## 1.2 Research Question

> How does Tong Keow Healthcare combine inherited Chinese medical knowledge, neighbourhood relationships, retail commerce and digital delivery, and what does this combination reveal about the localisation and continuing adaptation of TCM in Singapore?

## 1.3 Objectives

1. Document the layout, product organisation and boundary between retail advice and clinical care.
2. Collect the required Top 8, Top 5 and Top 3 rankings using a declared metric and evidence-strength code.
3. Examine sourcing, Singapore production/manufacturing, standardisation and supply-chain practices.
4. Compare face-to-face retail knowledge with GrabMart, Foodpanda or other digital delivery listings.
5. Investigate changes in customer demographics, household use, trust and neighbourhood relationships.
6. Interpret the evidence through three core lenses: pattern differentiation versus standardisation; yangsheng/food therapy/preventive care; and localisation/digital adaptation.

## 1.4 Absolute Scope Boundary

Tong Keow must be treated as a **medicine hall, not a clinic**, unless the outlet explicitly confirms that an in-house registered physician practises at this branch. Retail recommendation, weighing, packing and the sale of medicines do not by themselves establish clinical diagnosis or treatment. If no in-house registered physician exists, write **“Not applicable — retail medicine hall without an in-house registered physician”** in both clinical tables.

# 3. Semi-Structured Interview Guide

Use the questions flexibly. Ask the eight Must-ask questions first; they are designed for a 20-30 minute interview. Use Optional questions to explain the rankings and use Backup questions only when time permits or the participant is especially forthcoming. Pause whenever customers need service.

## 3.1 Must-Ask Tier

1. **[Must-ask] Site type and physician boundary.** How would you describe Tong Keow Healthcare and its main services? Does an in-house registered physician provide consultations or prescriptions at this outlet?

   *If yes: Is the in-house registered physician regularly based here or visiting, and may we ask for aggregate Top 8 prescription and symptom rankings? If no: mark both clinical tables “Not applicable — retail medicine hall without an in-house registered physician”.*

2. **[Must-ask] Top 8 herbs.** Looking at the past 12 months, which eight raw herbs had the highest physical sales volume or transaction count?

   *Probes: What unit and channel scope are used? Is this based on records or experience? If unavailable, which permitted fallback metric can be used?*

3. **[Must-ask] Top 8 medical products.** Which eight packaged medical products were most popular, and how would you describe their principal uses?

   *Probes: Are uses taken from the label, supplier information or staff knowledge? Are online and in-store rankings different?*

4. **[Must-ask] Top 5 soup therapies.** Which five soup therapies were most popular, what are their main uses, and are their rankings seasonal?

   *Probes: Who commonly buys each one and for whom? Are they used for daily maintenance, recovery, postpartum care or seasonal adjustment?*

5. **[Must-ask] Three least-used products.** Which three products sell least often, and what explains the low use?

   *Probes: Price, taste, inconvenience, unfamiliarity, seasonality or changing demand? Were discontinued and unavailable products excluded?*

6. **[Must-ask] Singapore production.** Which five commonly used products are genuinely grown, manufactured, formulated or packed in Singapore?

   *Probes: What part of production occurs locally? How do you distinguish a local producer from a Singapore seller of imported finished goods?*

7. **[Must-ask] E-commerce and delivery.** How and why did the shop begin using GrabMart, Foodpanda or other delivery channels, and what has changed in product range, descriptions, pricing, packing or customer interaction?

   *Probes: Which products work well online? What is lost or gained when the counter conversation becomes an app listing?*

8. **[Must-ask] Customer change.** How have the age groups, languages, cultural backgrounds, household roles or buying habits of customers changed?

   *Probes: How do older and younger customers differ? Who uses delivery, and who still prefers face-to-face advice?*

## 3.2 Optional Tier: Thematic Follow-Ups

1. **[Optional - sourcing]** Where do most raw herbs and packaged products come from, and how have those sources changed?
2. **[Optional - sourcing]** How do you select suppliers and judge herb quality, authenticity, grade and processing?
3. **[Optional - sourcing]** How do shortages, import delays, costs, storage requirements or regulatory expectations affect what the shop stocks?
4. **[Optional - standardisation]** How do loose herbs, shop-assembled combinations, proprietary medicines and fixed soup packs differ in convenience and the advice customers need?
5. **[Optional - standardisation]** How do fixed packaged products fit with the TCM idea that people with the same symptom may require different approaches?
6. **[Optional - translation]** How do staff explain heat/cold, dampness, deficiency, nourishment or constitution to customers with little TCM background?
7. **[Optional - translation]** Are terms such as “energy,” “detox,” “immunity” or “heatiness” accurate translations, useful shortcuts or marketing language in this setting?
8. **[Optional - trust]** What makes customers trust a product or recommendation: staff relationships, shop history, brands, certification, ingredient information or something else?
9. **[Optional - continuity]** Which practices feel continuous with older medicine-hall traditions, and which have changed most for contemporary Singapore?
10. **[Optional - continuity]** Do prepacked soups preserve food-therapy and yangsheng knowledge, simplify it, or do both at once?
11. **[Optional - medical pluralism]** How do staff respond when customers use a biomedical diagnosis or ask about combining products with other medicines?
12. **[Optional - local role]** What role does a neighbourhood medicine hall still play when similar goods can be bought from supermarkets and online sellers?

## 3.3 Backup Tier

1. **[Backup]** Could you tell us how this outlet developed and what has changed most during your time here?
2. **[Backup]** What responsibilities do staff members have for selling, preparing herbs, recommending soup packs or providing consultation?
3. **[Backup]** How are loose herbs stored, checked and rotated compared with packaged products?
4. **[Backup]** Are traditional supplier relationships still important, or have formal documentation and standard specifications become more important?
5. **[Backup]** Singapore has limited commercial cultivation space. How does this affect imported ingredients and locally manufactured products?
6. **[Backup]** How are online descriptions kept brief without oversimplifying a product's use, and how are substitutions handled when stock is unavailable?
7. **[Backup]** Do platform commissions, ratings, searches or promotions influence pricing and purchasing more than staff recommendations do?
8. **[Backup]** How is knowledge passed to newer staff: family teaching, apprenticeship, formal courses, supplier training or written materials?
9. **[Backup]** What knowledge or products are becoming less familiar to younger customers, and have social media or wellness trends revived any items?
10. **[Backup]** How does the hall balance cultural responsibility with commercial survival, and what would be lost if neighbourhood medicine halls disappeared?
11. **[Backup]** What do you think this shop will look like in ten years?
12. **[Backup]** Is there anything important our questions missed, or any display/product we should observe to understand the business properly?

### Conditional Physician Questions

Ask only if an in-house registered physician is explicitly confirmed.

13. **[Backup - conditional]** Over the past 12 months, which eight herbs or prescriptions were prescribed most frequently, based on aggregate data?
14. **[Backup - conditional]** Which eight symptoms or complaints were treated most often, and what remedies were commonly associated with them in aggregate?
15. **[Backup - conditional]** How do constitution, age, diet, season, heat/cold and deficiency/excess modify a prescription for people presenting with a similar symptom?

## 5.4 Detailed Fieldwork and Observation Templates

### 5.4.1 Operational Definitions

Before collecting rankings, confirm these definitions with the staff member:

- **Herb:** A raw or minimally processed medicinal ingredient, not a proprietary packaged medicine.
- **Medical product:** A packaged proprietary medicinal or healthcare product. Record whether it is herbal, non-herbal or mixed.
- **Popular:** Prefer units sold or number of transactions over the past 12 months. Record another basis if the shop uses one.
- **Sales volume:** Physical quantity sold, such as kilograms, packets, boxes or bottles—not revenue.
- **Locally produced:** Either grown in Singapore or manufactured, packed or formulated in Singapore. Record which criterion applies.
- **Soup therapy:** A prepacked or shop-assembled combination intended for home cooking and described as supporting health maintenance, recovery or seasonal wellness.
- **Least used:** Products with the lowest or slowest sales according to records or staff experience. Clarify whether seasonal items are being excluded.
- **In-house registered physician:** A registered practitioner who provides consultation or prescriptions at this specific outlet. Do not assume one is present.

### 5.4.4 Recommended Visit Sequence

#### Before the Visit (before arrival)

1. **Confirm scope.** Remind the group that the subject is a medicine hall as a place and business. Clinical examination is outside scope unless the outlet independently has an in-house registered physician.

2. **Check practical details.** Verify opening hours and current GrabMart/Foodpanda availability shortly before the visit.

3. **Choose the approach.** A walk-in request during a quieter period may reduce the chance of an advance refusal. If the interview is essential, make a short advance enquiry without demanding a commitment.

4. **Prepare a backup.** Identify another medicine hall in the assigned area. Use it only if access to Tong Keow fails completely or if the course instructor permits supplementary comparison.

5. **Conduct a preliminary digital audit.** Capture dated screenshots of public product categories, descriptions, prices, promotions, ratings and delivery information. Do not treat these as proof of sales popularity.

6. **Assign roles and rehearse.** Practise the introduction, the ranking definitions and a five-minute abbreviated interview.

#### On Arrival (0–10 min)

7. **Observe before approaching staff.** Spend approximately five minutes noting the façade, signage, immediate surroundings, customer flow and the visible distinction between retail and consultation space.

8. **Request permission politely.** Ask for a brief interview and separate permission for photographs and audio recording. Consent to answer questions does not automatically include consent to photographs or recording.

9. **Start with low-burden questions.** Ask about the history and nature of the business before requesting rankings or business-sensitive information.

#### Required Rankings (10–30 min)

10. **Complete the required rankings.** Use the tables below. Confirm the time period, ranking basis, units and whether answers come from records or recollection.

#### Thematic Follow-Up (30–45 min)

11. **Conduct thematic follow-up questions.** Prioritise questions that explain why the rankings and store practices look the way they do.

#### Interior Observation and Permitted Photographs (45–60 min)

12. **Observe without obstructing business.** Pause the interview whenever customers require service. Do not record customers’ names, health conditions or identifiable faces.

#### Before Leaving (60–70 min)

13. **Read back key facts.** Confirm the business name, site type, rankings, local-product definitions and whether an in-house registered physician operates there.

14. **Ask permission for attribution.** Establish whether the staff member may be described by name, role only or anonymously.

15. **Thank the participant.** Offer to send the completed presentation or report if appropriate, without promising editorial control over the analysis.

#### Immediately After Leaving (debrief and evidence labelling)

16. **Hold a ten-minute debrief.** Each member separately states three striking observations, one contradiction and one unanswered question before group discussion influences recollection.

17. **Label all evidence.** Record date, time, observer, source and consent status for every note, quotation, photograph and screenshot.

18. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Use the analytical memo format below.

19. **Identify gaps.** If necessary, send one concise follow-up question rather than reopening the whole interview.

Adapt this schedule to the shop’s workload. A shorter, respectful interview is preferable to disrupting customers.

### 5.4.5 Evidence-Strength Code

- **A — Documentary:** Derived from a 12-month sales report, transaction record or other record shown or described in detail.
- **B — Record-informed estimate:** Staff consulted records but provided approximate or partial figures.
- **C — Experienced recollection:** Ranking based on staff experience without records.
- **D — Observational proxy:** Based only on shelf position, stock or platform visibility. This is not sufficient on its own to claim “best-selling.”

## 6.1 Introduction

### 6.1.1 Purpose

This project investigates Tong Keow Healthcare as a contemporary Chinese medicine hall: a neighbourhood retail site where materia medica, proprietary products, dried foods, soup therapies, health knowledge and digital delivery may intersect. The study asks what the organisation of products, staff-customer interactions, sales patterns and platform practices reveals about the cultural, commercial and social meanings of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in Singapore.

The two supplied student papers are used only as academic benchmarks. The individual essay demonstrates how a writer can move from bodily experience and constitution theory to a documented herbal recipe and reflective evaluation (Bi 2024). The field report demonstrates how historical context, observed practices, herb tables, numbered figures and personal reflection can be arranged into a coherent field narrative (Lim 2024). Their specific diagnoses, therapies and claims are not transferred to Tong Keow.

### 6.1.2 Research Question

> How does Tong Keow Healthcare combine inherited Chinese medical knowledge, neighbourhood relationships, retail commerce and digital delivery, and what does this combination reveal about the localisation and continuing adaptation of TCM in Singapore?

### 6.1.3 Objectives

1. Document the material and spatial organisation of a medicine hall rather than a clinical treatment centre.
2. Obtain the required Top 8, Top 5 and Top 3 rankings using a stated 12-month metric and evidence-strength code.
3. Examine sourcing, Singapore production, product standardisation and supply-chain constraints.
4. Compare face-to-face retail knowledge with product descriptions and transactions on GrabMart, Foodpanda or other public digital channels.
5. Analyse how customers, staff, packaging and displays translate course concepts such as heat/cold, constitution, pattern, nourishment and prevention into everyday practice.
6. Develop a cautious, evidence-based account of the hall's cultural, commercial and social significance.

## 6.2 Background and Significance

### 6.2.1 Site and Scope

Tong Keow Healthcare is located at Blk 809 Hougang Central, #01-170, Singapore 530809. On the information presently available, it is a medicine hall selling herbal ingredients, dried goods and medicinal products, with modern delivery channels including GrabMart and Foodpanda. These facts establish the field site but do not establish current opening hours, platform availability, ownership history, customer profile, sales volume or the presence of an in-house registered physician; those items require verification.

The medicine hall/clinic distinction is fundamental. Retail recommendation, weighing and packing do not by themselves constitute clinical diagnosis. Clinical sections apply only if the outlet confirms that an in-house registered physician practises at this branch. Until then, in-house registered physician data are marked **[data not obtained]**, not presumed absent or present.

### 6.2.2 Singapore Historical, Cultural and Social Context

GEC1044 course materials present Chinese medicine in Singapore as a localised and historically changing field rather than a sealed inheritance from China. Medicine halls have operated simultaneously as commercial distributors of imported materia medica, neighbourhood sources of practical health knowledge, and sites where household food therapy and formal medicine meet. Their development has also occurred within a biomedical-dominant environment shaped by professionalisation, associations, regulation, standardisation and public expectations of safety and evidence (GEC1044 Week 5 course materials 2026).

This context makes a neighbourhood hall analytically significant. Bilingual labels may translate specialist concepts into consumer language; packaged products may standardise knowledge previously conveyed through personal interaction; locally manufactured goods may coexist with imported raw ingredients; and delivery platforms may extend access while reducing opportunities for staff to ask person-specific questions. These are propositions for investigation, not conclusions.

### 6.2.3 Why Tong Keow Is a Useful Case

Tong Keow offers a bounded case through which the group can examine four relationships:

- **Culture and practice:** how classical concepts are retained, simplified or reworded in daily retail encounters;
- **Commerce and care:** how commercial sale can coexist with advice, trust and household responsibility;
- **Local and transnational:** how imported ingredients connect with Singapore manufacturing, packing, regulation and neighbourhood demand;
- **Physical and digital:** how the counter, shelves and herb drawers compare with the fixed categories and short descriptions of delivery platforms.

It does not, by itself, show that buyers understand constitution theory or that the soup is clinically effective. ## 6.4 Methodology

### 6.4.1 Research Design and Case Selection

This is a qualitative, single-site case study using purposive selection. Tong Keow is selected because it is a neighbourhood medicine hall with both physical retail and reported platform delivery. The unit of analysis is the outlet as a place and business. Relevant sub-units are its product categories, displays, staff explanations, customer-service interactions, delivery listings and documentary materials.

The study does not aim to represent every Singapore medicine hall or assess clinical effectiveness. Its purpose is analytic: to show how several course concepts become visible, contested or transformed within one contemporary setting.

### 6.4.2 Data-Collection Methods

| Method | Data produced | Minimum documentation | Main limitation |

### 6.4.4 Ethics and Privacy

- Obtain voluntary consent for the interview; request separate permission for audio and photography.
- Use participant codes such as **S1 (staff member)** or **O1 (owner/manager)**. Record a name only when explicit attribution consent is given.
- Do not photograph identifiable customers, prescriptions, payment screens or private records.

- If clinical questions arise, state that the research group is documenting practice and cannot recommend treatment.

3. Transcribe audio verbatim where intelligible. Mark omissions as `[inaudible]`, translations as `[translated from Mandarin]`, and paraphrases as `[fieldnote paraphrase]`.
4. Preserve Chinese technical terms on first use, followed by pinyin and a cautious English gloss where helpful.
5. Enter ranking data exactly as supplied. Keep original units and create converted units only in a separate derived column.
6. Record who supplied each claim, whether it came from records or recollection, and whether it concerns in-store, online or combined sales.
7. Maintain a read-only master evidence folder and a separate working folder for cropped or presentation-ready copies.

### 6.4.7 Triangulation and Analytic Validity

Claims should be checked across staff accounts, physical observation, labels and digital evidence. Popularity requires a ranking source; shelf prominence is only a supporting proxy. Singapore production requires manufacturing or packing evidence; a Singapore brand address alone is insufficient. Deviant cases and conflicting staff accounts should be retained rather than forced into a single narrative. Section 7.4 provides the claim-by-source matrix.

### 6.4.8 Researcher Reflexivity

Before the visit, each member should note prior experiences with herbal soups, “heaty/cooling” language, biomedical care or family medicine-hall use. After the visit, write a short first-person reflection under four headings: **what I noticed; why it stood out to me; how my background may have shaped that response; what independent evidence supports or challenges it**. Sensory details such as smell, packaging texture or the rhythm of counter work can enrich description, but they cannot establish medical effect or customer motivation.

## 6.7 Discussion

### 6.7.1 Continuity Through Adaptation

The central discussion should test whether Tong Keow illustrates continuity through adaptation: not an unchanged survival of the past, but the maintenance of recognisable Chinese medical categories through packaging, language, regulation, retail routines and digital channels. Use counter-evidence. For example, an app may broaden access while narrowing person-specific explanation.

### 6.7.2 Individualisation and Standardisation

Pattern differentiation and constitution theory emphasise context and individual variation, whereas proprietary products, fixed soup packs and platform listings favour stable units and brief indications. The report should show how staff manage this tension in practice. A fixed product is not necessarily “untraditional,” and a loose herb is not automatically individualised.

### 6.7.3 Medical Pluralism and Boundary Work

Discuss how the outlet positions TCM alongside biomedicine and general wellness without assuming conflict or equivalence. Evidence may include safety questions, label language, referrals, customer vocabulary and staff distinctions between retail advice and diagnosis. Preserve the site's status as a medicine hall unless a clinical service is verified.

### 6.7.4 Reflective Evaluation

Following the useful reflective dimension of the benchmark PDFs, include a short group reflection on what became tangible at the site and which assumptions changed. Unlike a therapeutic self-experiment, this reflection must evaluate the research encounter, not the efficacy of a product. A strong reflection identifies an initial expectation, a surprising observation, a course concept that clarified it, and a limitation or alternative interpretation.

## 6.8 Limitations

The final report should address, as applicable:

1. **Single-site scope:** one Hougang outlet cannot represent all medicine halls or TCM in Singapore.
2. **Time and season:** one visit captures a limited period; festivals, weather and illness cycles may alter demand.
3. **Access and recall:** staff may decline commercially sensitive questions or estimate rankings from memory.
4. **Metric comparability:** kilograms, packets, bottles and transactions cannot be combined without a clear method.
5. **Observer effect:** staff and customers may act differently while being observed.
6. **Demographic uncertainty:** visible age bands and languages are approximate; identity and motivation cannot be inferred.
7. **Platform instability:** prices, stock and listings can change rapidly; every screenshot needs a date and time.
8. **Translation:** English glosses such as “energy,” “organ” or “dampness” may not reproduce the full Chinese concept.
9. **Source quality:** label and supplier claims differ from course scholarship, sales records and clinical evidence.
10. **Clinical boundary:** if no in-house registered physician is present, the project cannot draw conclusions about diagnosis or prescribing at Tong Keow.
