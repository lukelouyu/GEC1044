# Lesson 1 Part II — Beginnings of Chinese Medicine

## 1. From Survival to a Culture of Hygiene

The history of Chinese medicine begins with the earliest history of Chinese culture. In the **Paleolithic and Neolithic periods**, survival was difficult: prehistoric people faced harsh environments, warfare, wild animals, injuries and diseases, while average lifespans were short. Archaeological remains show fractures, trauma, bone diseases and other illnesses. These experiences encouraged early humans to identify threats to health and develop ways to protect themselves.

Early Chinese communities gradually developed a **primitive culture of hygiene**, which became an important precursor to medicine. Settlements were designed partly around health and environmental protection. At **Banpo**, for example, a surrounding ditch protected the village from enemies and animals while also draining water. People of the **Hemudu culture** built elevated houses that improved ventilation and kept living spaces dry in humid environments. Ancient people therefore recognized that environmental conditions could contribute to illness and attempted to modify or leave unhealthy environments.

> [!important]  
> **The earliest foundation of Chinese medicine was not formal medical theory, but practical attempts to survive, prevent illness and create healthier living environments.**

## 2. Early Healing and the Supernatural

Prehistoric people also developed spiritual explanations for life, death and disease. Observing the difference between a living body and a corpse encouraged belief in an invisible **soul or spirit** that gave life to the physical body. This developed alongside **animism**, the belief that supernatural forces existed throughout the natural world.

Because disease was often unpredictable and uncontrollable, illness could be interpreted as the action of invisible supernatural forces. Healing therefore originally contained strong **religious and magical elements**. Other prehistoric cultures practised procedures such as **trepanation**, possibly to release harmful spirits associated with headaches, epilepsy or mental disturbance, although the lecture notes that there is no archaeological evidence of trepanation among prehistoric Chinese remains.

## 3. Shang Dynasty: Ancestors as Causes of Illness

The **Shang dynasty** marks the beginning of historically documented China because it left written evidence, especially **oracle-bone inscriptions**. Shang society was hierarchical, with the king serving not only as ruler but also as **high priest**, acting as an intermediary between the living and the dead.

The Shang worldview treated ancestors as active members of society even after death. Ancestors depended on descendants for offerings of food and wine, but could also punish descendants when displeased. One important punishment was **illness**. Oracle-bone inscriptions record questions about sickness and whether particular illnesses were caused by curses from deceased ancestors.

> [!important]  
> In Shang thought, people recognised many **symptoms of illness**, but the most important explanation for their underlying cause was often an **ancestor's curse**.

Healing therefore usually involved priests communicating and negotiating with ancestors through **divination and offerings**.

## 4. From Illness to Disease Concepts

Oracle-bone texts repeatedly contain early characters representing illness. Over time, Chinese writing increasingly distinguished observable sickness from an underlying **disease or pathological cause**. This distinction eventually contributed to terms such as **疾病 (jíbìng)**, referring broadly to illness and disease.

The Shang also recognised causes other than ancestral curses. One important example was **harmful or "evil" wind**. Wind could originally be imagined as a supernatural agent or a tool of higher powers, but it later became one of the major **pathogenic factors** in Chinese medicine.

This concept survives in terms such as **中風 (zhòngfēng, "struck by wind")**, the traditional Chinese term associated with stroke. Ancient physicians correctly recognised characteristic neurological symptoms, although they explained their cause through the medical concepts available at the time.

## 5. From Priest to Shaman-Healer

During the Shang, healing was primarily conducted by **priests**, including the king. Under the **Zhou dynasty**, the role increasingly shifted toward **巫 (wū)** — shamans, magicians or ritual healers.

The early Chinese concept of a healer therefore emerged from **religion, ritual and magic**, rather than from a separate scientific medical profession. The historical relationship is even reflected in the development of terminology surrounding the physician or healer.

> [!key]  
> **Chinese medicine did not appear suddenly. It gradually developed out of an older system of supernatural and ritual healing.**

## 6. Zhou and Warring States: Ancestors, Heaven and Demons

The Zhou continued ancestor worship but introduced **天 (Tiān, Heaven)** as a greater cosmological force. Heaven gradually became an impersonal and later moral principle, particularly under Confucian influence. Ancestors remained important, but they now shared the supernatural world with other spirits.

During the **Warring States period**, beliefs about harmful **demons or ghosts (鬼)** became increasingly prominent. Unlike ancestors, demons had no family relationship with their victims and were therefore harder to negotiate with. They might be understood as resentful beings resulting from violent or unnatural death.

Healing consequently adopted the language of warfare: harmful spirits were enemies that needed to be **expelled, attacked or defeated**. People used rituals, deities, spells, objects and substances to combat them.

## 7. Demonic Medicine and the Influence of Warfare

The violent political environment of the Warring States strongly influenced medical thinking. Healing increasingly resembled a military struggle between the human body and hostile external forces.

The lecture describes this as **demonic medicine**. Certain treatments may have developed from this worldview: poisonous herbs could be imagined as weapons against harmful entities, while the insertion of needles into the body eventually contributed to the development of **acupuncture**.

> [!important]  
> **Medical ideas reflected contemporary social experience: a society dominated by warfare naturally produced medical models involving attack, defence, invasion and expulsion.**

This supernatural style of healing continued to influence Chinese medicine even into the Qin and Han imperial periods.

## 8. The Yin-Yang Thinkers: Toward Natural Explanations

During the Warring States period, another intellectual development occurred. Thinkers associated with the **Yin-Yang school (陰陽家)** attempted to explain the world increasingly through **natural processes rather than spirits and demons**. Their thought overlapped with developing Daoist ideas.

These thinkers did not simply eliminate older spiritual concepts. Instead, they **reinterpreted them**. Spirits were gradually internalised into the human body and connected with substances and processes such as **blood (血), Qi (氣) and Shen (神)**.

Qi was understood in multiple ways, including breath, subtle material influence, force or energy. Together with blood, it circulated through the organism and supported life. **Shen (神)** became increasingly connected with consciousness and mental activity.

This represented an important transition:

**supernatural explanation → naturalised bodily theory**

## 9. Zang and Fu: The Body as a System of Storage

Chinese medicine divided internal organs into two broad categories: **Zang (臟)** and **Fu (腑)**.

**Zang organs** were understood as long-term repositories that stored important substances and supported the body's vital and spiritual functions. **Fu organs** were more like temporary repositories that received, processed and discharged substances rather than storing them permanently.

The metaphor reflected ordinary social and economic experience: Zang organs were like **long-term storehouses**, while Fu organs resembled **temporary depots**.

Shen or the body's spirits could remain securely within the organism only if the Zang possessed sufficient **Qi and resources**.

> [!important]  
> Ancient medical texts therefore emphasised that **retaining the spirit meant life and prosperity, while losing the spirit meant decline or death**.

## 10. Emotions as Internal Causes of Disease

The new medical theory also placed greater responsibility on the individual. Instead of illness being caused only by external spirits, people's own behaviour could weaken their internal resources.

Chinese medicine identified **seven emotions (七情)**: joy, anger, worry, grief, fear, fright and pensiveness. Excessive emotion was believed to consume the resources of particular Zang organs, weaken Qi and disturb Shen. The **heart** became especially closely associated with Shen and mental activity.

Moderate emotion was therefore desirable, while emotional extremes were dangerous.

> [!important]  
> **Excessive emotions → depletion of internal resources → weakened organs/Shen → greater vulnerability to disease.**

This framework strongly influenced later Chinese ideas about the relationship between **physical health, mental states and emotional self-control**.

## 11. Deficiency, Invasion and Military Metaphors

An organ could become **depleted or deficient** when its internal resources were exhausted. Once weakened, it could no longer perform its functions effectively. Different organs were also associated with different bodily functions; for example, sufficient resources in the **liver** were linked to healthy vision.

A depleted organ was compared to a **breach in a city's defensive walls**. Just as enemies could exploit weak city defences, harmful external influences could invade a weakened body.

This is why classical Chinese medical vocabulary frequently resembles military language:

**body = state/city**  
**organs = internal institutions/storage**  
**Qi = defensive resources**  
**pathogens = invading enemies**  
**treatment = defence and expulsion**

## 12. Zheng Qi and Xie Qi: Correct vs Evil

By the Qin-Han imperial period, Chinese thought increasingly organised the world through the contrast between what was **proper/correct** and what was **evil/improper**.

Natural phenomena such as wind, cold and dampness were not inherently harmful. They became pathogenic when they took advantage of bodily weakness and entered the organism. A normally natural influence could therefore become **邪 (xié, evil/pathogenic)** when it caused disorder.

This produced the fundamental medical opposition between the body's proper or healthy forces and harmful external influences, later expressed through ideas such as **正氣 (zhèngqì)** and **邪氣 (xiéqì)**.

> [!important]  
> Disease was increasingly explained as a **natural interaction between the body's internal condition and external pathogenic influences**, rather than simply as supernatural punishment.

## 13. From Healing to Chinese Medicine

The development of Chinese medicine was therefore a gradual transformation:

**survival and hygiene → supernatural healing → ancestor medicine → demonic medicine → naturalistic theories of Qi, blood, organs, emotions and pathogens**

The social environment repeatedly shaped medical theory. Harsh prehistoric environments encouraged hygiene; Shang ancestor worship shaped explanations of illness; Warring States warfare contributed military metaphors; and new philosophical movements encouraged natural explanations involving **Yin-Yang, Qi, Shen, Zang-Fu and pathogenic influences**.

> [!summary] Core Takeaway  
> **Chinese medicine developed out of earlier religious and magical healing traditions. During the late Zhou, Warring States, Qin and Han periods, supernatural explanations were gradually reinterpreted into systematic theories of natural bodily processes. By the end of the classical formative period, the basic conceptual framework of Chinese medicine was largely in place.**
