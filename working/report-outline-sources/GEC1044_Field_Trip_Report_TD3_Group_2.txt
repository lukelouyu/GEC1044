1 Introduction 
At Tong Keow’s Hougang outlet, herbs and soup packs sit beside everyday foods. Staff described 
purchases for nourishment, throat comfort and needs linked to daily routines. These displays show 
how Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) enters ordinary shopping and cooking. 
We argue that the medicine hall keeps traditional ingredients usable in household life by linking them 
with food, convenient preparations and local retail practices. Our question is how these practices 
sustain TCM while changing how people encounter it. We examine this through displays, one staff 
interview and published sources, without assessing clinical effectiveness or sales performance. 
2 Fieldwork and the neighbourhood medicine hall 
2.1 How we conducted the fieldwork 
We visited Tong Keow on 13 September 2026, photographed displays and interviewed one staff 
member. The transcripts, presentation script and photographs form the basis of this report. When a 
transcript is unclear, we use only meanings supported by the other records. 
We separate field observations, staff comments, published references and our interpretations. 
Demand descriptions reflect one employee’s experience and may favour products the shop sells. 
They are not independent evidence of customer preferences. 
2.2 Tong Keow and its mixed retail model 
Tong Keow’s profile states that the medicine hall was founded in Toa Payoh in 1968 and is now run 
by second-generation family owners. Its promotional materials feature American ginseng, monk fruit, 
cooling teas, pills and plasters (Tong Keow Medical Hall 2022; “Instagram Reel about Tong Keow 
Medical Hall” n.d.). 
The company advertises physician consultations, acupuncture and cupping for indigestion, colds 
and sprains (“Instagram Reel about Tong Keow Medical Hall” n.d.). We did not verify these services 
or observe consultations. Our visit focused on the retail space. Branded soup packs beside herbs, 
grains and foods connect the branch to the wider business. This mixed range lets shoppers 
encounter herbal products while buying groceries.

2.3 Hougang and the customers described by staff 
Staff described mainly Chinese, middle-aged and older customers, including people in their forties 
and fifties. Hougang Central had about 4,620 residents in June 2025. Around 1,070 were aged 65 or 
above (23%) (Singapore Department of Statistics 2025). These figures give neighbourhood context, 
but do not identify actual shoppers. 
Hougang also has a rich Teochew history. The National Heritage Board describes the community 
around Tou Mu Kung and its fishing, farming and rubber-growing activities (National Heritage Board 
n.d.). This provides cultural context, but heritage alone cannot explain current buying habits or 
establish customers’ dialect identities.

3 Food and everyday care 
3.1 Household nourishment 
Staff named Dang Shen (党参), Huang Qi (黄芪), Yu Zhu (玉竹), Huai Shan (淮山), red dates (红枣) 
and goji (枸杞) as common soup ingredients. They also described ginseng (人参), American ginseng 
(西洋参) and monk fruit (罗汉果) as common purchases. Table A1 records nine profiles.

Soup gives herbs a familiar household purpose. Nourishing life (养生) and medicine and food 
sharing a source (药食同源) help explain this connection. Lim et al. (2005) identify health 
maintenance as an important reason for complementary and alternative medicine use in Singapore. 
Cooking offers one route for household care: traditional ingredients enter familiar meals, so their use 
need not begin with a specific illness. 
3.2 Soups and light nourishment 
Table A2 retains five presentation labels: Qing Bu Liang (清补凉), Dang Shen–Huang Qi (党参北芪
补气汤), Yu Zhu–Huai Shan (玉竹淮山润燥汤), goji–red date (枸杞红枣养生汤) and Five Finger Fig 
(五指毛桃健脾汤). Staff described uses such as aiding digestion (助消化), moistening (润燥) and 
light nourishment (qing bu / 清补). Light nourishment offers an everyday dietary approach alongside 
stronger tonification (da bu / 大补). Pre-selected packs make these ideas easier to use because 
households receive portioned ingredients and complete the cooking. 
Several labels, especially Five Finger Fig, emphasise Spleen or digestive support. The TCM Spleen 
is a broader functional system, not simply the anatomical organ (World Health Organization 2022). 
Other labels describe moistening and general nourishment. The shared cooking format therefore 
carries different traditional purposes, rather than one uniform treatment. 
3.3 Climate and soup preferences 
Staff linked demand for monk fruit and lighter soups to Singapore’s hot and humid environment. 
Singapore lacks the four temperate seasons used in classical seasonal guidance. Their explanation 
suggests that seasonal health advice is adapted to local conditions. However, climate alone cannot 
establish an individual’s TCM pattern or the suitability of a cooling product. 
The Suwen (素问) links health maintenance to harmony with seasonal cycles (Unschuld and 
Tessenow 2011, 45–57). Yu Zhu’s traditional moistening profile connects it with autumn dryness (qiu 
zao / 秋燥). It nourishes Yin and generates fluids for the Lung and Stomach (Hong Kong Polytechnic 
University n.d.; see table A1). In Singapore, Yu Zhu appears in the recorded Yu Zhu–Huai Shan 
soup. Staff described gentle moistening and fluid replenishment. This example shows how traditional 
ideas can be explained in a different climate.

4 Convenience and changing lifestyles 
4.1 Younger buyers and preparation 
Staff said younger customers preferred teas, supplements and simple preparations to raw herbs. 
Table A3 records eight product categories, including ginseng tonics, herbal teas, soup packs, 
mood-support products and medicated oils. This account points to product form as one reason 
traditional ingredients may fit younger buyers’ routines. 
Pre-selected soup packs shift selection and portioning away from buyers, while households still 
cook. Capsules reduce preparation further. Staff explained ingredient uses, and a photographed 
multi-buy promotion presented packs through a familiar supermarket offer. Buyers can start with a 
prepared combination instead of choosing herbs and quantities. Convenience changes the work 
needed to bring traditional ingredients into household routines. In this arrangement, some 
knowledge of selection is built into the product before purchase. 
4.2 Lifestyle pressures and packaged products 
Staff linked late nights, long work hours and prolonged phone use to demand for liver-care or 
mood-support products. Nature’s Green Mind Soothe Capsules use the language of soothing 
Liver-related constraint (疏肝解郁) and are listed by HSA as a Singapore-manufactured Chinese 
Proprietary Medicine (CPM) (Tong Jum Chew n.d.; Health Sciences Authority 2025b). Such 
descriptions connect TCM terminology with familiar concerns about stress and mental well-being. 
These are retail associations, rather than clinical diagnoses or evidence that the products treat 
mental illness. 
Kumari (2024), reporting on ADP’s 2023 survey, notes weekly work-related stress among 68% of 
Singapore workers, compared with 61% across surveyed Asia-Pacific markets. This provides 
context for the shop’s product language, without establishing its customers’ reasons for purchase. 
Together with Section 4.1, the staff account suggests that lifestyle pressures and convenience may 
help explain interest in packaged products: familiar concerns are expressed through traditional 
terms, while capsules fit daily routines.

4.3 Preparation and product appeal 
Lower-demand examples help explain the appeal of simpler products. Staff named Ban Zhi Lian (半
枝莲), specialist herbs requiring combinations and strong tonic formulas (大补方) (Table A4). They 
linked lower appeal to unfamiliarity and preparation effort. Staff also linked stronger tonics’ lower 
appeal to a local preference for lighter qing bu (清补) in Singapore’s hot and humid climate. They 
reported stocking fewer slow-selling items. These comments concern retail demand, not medical 
unsuitability. Unfamiliar combinations may need more advice and preparation. Prepared packs 
reduce these demands, helping explain staff-reported differences in appeal. 
5 Localisation and cultural change 
5.1 Imports and local production 
Table A5 presents five examples with Singapore links, distinguishing cultivation, manufacture, 
processing or packing, and branding. These stages establish different connections with Singapore: 
manufacturing locally does not show that an ingredient was grown there. The staff member 
described local cultivation as limited and attributed imports mainly from China to their wider variety 
and lower cost. The distinction allows us to understand localisation through production and retail 
practices even when cultivation remains overseas (Yang 2022). 
Product form and intended use also shape regulation. Finished Chinese Proprietary Medicines 
(CPMs) require HSA product listing and the relevant dealer licence (Health Sciences Authority 
2026a, 2026b). Raw medicinal materials and culinary foods fall under separate categories. Permitted 
materials may be used in soups or drinks for non-medicinal purposes (Singapore Food Agency 
2025). Tong Keow displays these products together on its shelves, but each product type retains its 
own regulatory status. 
5.2 Packaging and trust 
CPM labels must provide English information on ingredients, batch numbers, expiry dates and 
supply-chain details (Health Sciences Authority 2025a, sec. 5). These details make the product and 
its source identifiable, supporting traceability. HSA listing addresses safety and quality, but does not 
demonstrate clinical effectiveness (Health Sciences Authority 2026a). 
The photographs show two different kinds of product information: a sulphur-free claim on Dang Shen 
and a label identifying American ginseng. Staff also emphasised fresh colour and sulphur-free 
materials. Together, these details suggest how processing claims and product identification may 
build shopper confidence, although customers’ responses were not investigated.

5.3 Food and cultural contact 
Staff said most customers were Chinese, while some Malay customers bought dessert ingredients 
and some Indian customers bought nuts. Everyday foods may give different communities familiar 
reasons to enter the shop. These purchases do not require a shared understanding of TCM. The 
mixed range therefore suggests several household uses within one retail space. 
Lecture 5’s discussion of senna leaf (番泻叶) illustrates how the same plant can carry different 
meanings in Chinese and Malay medicine (GEC1044 Lecture 5 n.d.). This helps us interpret the 
shop’s shared retail space. 
Purchases alone, however, cannot establish cultural exchange or shoppers’ beliefs. Customer 
interviews could clarify whether buyers regard these products as food, medicine or both.

6 Retail adaptation and individual assessment 
At Tong Keow, food gives herbs a household role, prepared packs reduce preparation effort, and 
packaging communicates uses and origins. Unschuld (2018) discusses medicine’s adaptation to 
social and political settings, while Yang (2022) traces Singapore’s modernisation through state, 
professional and religious influences. These accounts provide a framework for interpreting the 
familiar foods and prepared products observed at the shop. 
Prepared products make everyday use easier, but convenience does not establish individual 
suitability. Pattern differentiation (辨证论治) requires a fuller assessment of signs and symptoms; a 
complaint such as fatigue alone cannot guide treatment (World Health Organization 2022). Table A6 
records the retail associations described during the visit, rather than physician prescribing data. 
7 Limitations 
One visit and one interview cannot establish demand trends or explain customers’ own views. 
Complete recipes, sourcing records and purchasing records were unavailable. Mind Soothe’s 
Singapore manufacture is confirmed, but its ingredient origins remain unknown. Staff named the 
category, not the brand. Physician presence was unclear, and we recorded no consultation, 
prescription or outcome. Practitioner regulation provides background, but does not verify an outlet 
physician’s role (Ministry of Health 2026). 
Customer interviews could test explanations of convenience and trust; sales records and branch 
comparisons could assess demand. Where records are unclear, we avoid reconstructing recipes or 
attributing external products to Tong Keow. These gaps limit generalisation, but the case still reveals 
how one medicine hall presents TCM within Singapore’s everyday retail setting. 
8 Conclusion 
Tong Keow’s significance lies in making traditional ingredients accessible through ordinary routines. 
Soup illustrates nourishing life (养生) and medicine and food sharing a common origin (药食同源), 
while packs and capsules adapt preparation to contemporary lifestyles. Local production links, labels 
and a mixed food range place these practices within Singapore’s commercial and cultural setting. 
These adaptations may sustain engagement with TCM while shifting knowledge and preparation 
from households towards businesses. Our evidence supports this interpretation of retail practice, but 
cannot establish effectiveness, customer motivation or wider trends. The medicine hall helps 
maintain everyday access to TCM; individual treatment still requires the assessment associated with 
treatment based on pattern differentiation (辨证论治).
