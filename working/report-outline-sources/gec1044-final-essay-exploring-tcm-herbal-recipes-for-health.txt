2.2 Patient’s Diagnosis 
 
(1) Yin-Deficiency 
As I conclude from the symptoms, yin deficiency is my predominant body constitution. In 
TCM, the analysis of the body employs the concept of Yin and Yang, representing opposing 
forces2. It is vital to maintain harmony between Yin and Yang3. 
Some symptoms of Yin-Deficiency include consistent thirst,  sweating, discomfort to the hot 
and humid environment, and lower immunity4. The insufficiency of Yin in the liver results in 
stagnation of Qi and heat in the liver, leading to bad emotions and affecting the metabolism, 
especially for females during menstruation. Drawing on the Five Phases Theory, where Earth 
generates Metal and subsequently Water within the body, the spleen corresponds to Earth, the 
lungs to Metal. Spleen Yin Deficiency impacts the stomach, resulting in stomach pains and 
emotional fluctuation5 (Refer to Picture 2.2.1).

(2) Dampness-Heat 
I have identified Dampness-Heat as my secondary body constitution based on the symptoms 
outlined in the section above. When considering the interplay between dampness and heat, the 
priority lies in addressing excess dampness rather than eliminating heat from the body. 
Dampness, classified as a Yin pathogen, has a detrimental impact on Yang and tends to induce 
Qi stagnation6. Dampness is further categorized into internal and external types, with internal 
dampness attributed to dietary factors, such as excessive consumption of oily foods, and 
external dampness linked to environmental conditions like humidity or damp surroundings. 
In my case, I believe I exhibit external dampness. External dampness typically manifests as a 
sensation of bodily heaviness, accompanied by symptoms like dizziness and joint soreness7. 
Among the organs affected by Dampness-Heat, the lungs, and large intestine are particularly 
relevant to my symptoms. Damp Heat in the large intestine results in the tongue displaying a 
thick yellow coat with consistent thirst8. 
(3) Menstrual cramps 
Furthermore, this aspect holds minimal significance for women, particularly considering the 
notable influence of certain lifestyle factors. The frequent consumption of substantial quantities 
of cold water, icy beverages, and chilled foods, coupled with continuous exposure to air 
conditioning, creates an environment where external heat is experienced 9. Paradoxically, 
despite this external perception of warmth, the internal nature of my body tends to be 
characterized by coldness. This dichotomy in temperature dynamics is a common occurrence 
among females, leading to the manifestation of symptoms such as menstrual cramps or, 
colloquially put, period pain.

3.2 Benefits of the Herbal Recipe 
This soup encompasses a comprehensive array of 13 key elements, notably including the 
foundational components of Bazhen Soup: Rhizoma Ligustici Chuanxiong (川芎), Radix 
Glycyrrhizae Praeparata (甘草), Radix Rehmanniae Preparata (熟地黄), Radix Codonopsis (党
参), Radix Angelicae Sinensis (当归), Poria (茯苓), Black Dates (黑枣), Rhizoma Atractylodis 
Macrocephalae (白术)12. The Ba Zhen recipes, have long been recognized as tonics with the 
traditional purpose of regulating irregular menstrual cycles. Additionally, this formula 
addresses the internal cold and external heat, effectively nourishing Yin, replenishing blood 
and Qi, elevating metabolic functions, and enhancing overall immunity. By alleviating 
stagnation in Qi and blood, it naturally resolves issues related to menstrual pain13. 
In addition to incorporating Ba Zhen elements, I have made a modification by substituting a 
small amount of Goji berries and Longan for the black dates. While Longan is intrinsically 
heaty in nature, it serves as an excellent material for nourishing Qi and blood14. By opting for 
these two ingredients with stronger medicinal properties, I have replaced black dates. 
Importantly, the usage of a minimal quantity ensures that it doesn't exacerbate the existing Yin 
deficiency and excessive internal heat in my constitution. Furthermore, this substitution not 
only avoids increasing internal heat but also contributes to enhancing overall immune function.

To enhance the effectiveness of White Atractylodes Rhizome (白术), I have introduced soil-
baked Bai Zhu (土炒白术) into the mixture. Soil baking is a method specifically employed to 
reinforce the spleen-nourishing properties15. Given that Bai Zhu inherently strengthens the 
spleen, invigorates Qi, and dispels dampness, the addition of soil-baked Bai Zhu intensifies its 
potency. Doubling the quantity serves to amplify the beneficial effects of Bai Zhu.  
I incorporated the earthworm(地龙)into the recipe. This ancient remedy finds its roots in the 
earliest Chinese pharmacological masterpiece, the "Shennong Ben Cao Jing," where 67 types 
of animal-based medicines, including earthworms, were documented. Classified as a lower-
grade herb in the "Shennong Ben Cao Jing," earthworms are recognized for their efficacy in 
clearing heat, calming agitation, promoting blood circulation, addressing asthma, and 
facilitating diuresis16. Earthworms possess a cooling nature and are associated with the liver 
meridian, displaying robust heat-clearing properties. This aligns perfectly with my usual 
tendencies towards excess heat and symptoms of damp heat.  
I use two essential regular foods—black chicken and ginger to cook the soup. With its neutral 
nature and sweet taste, black chicken is for nourishing the body and replenishing energy. 
Renowned for its ability to nourish Yin, enrich the blood, clear heat, tonify the liver and kidneys, 
strengthen the spleen, and alleviate diarrhea, black chicken serves as an excellent ingredient17. 
Adding ginger complements this by harmonizing the spleen and stomach, promoting vital 
energy and blood production18. Black chicken soup offers the dual benefit of replenishing Qi 
and blood, promoting blood circulation, invigorating the spleen, dispelling dampness, and 
enhancing the body's immune system. 
This wholesome combination ensures a robust vitality of Qi and blood, fortifying the spleen 
and kidneys against invading external pathogenic factors such as wind, cold, and dampness.

4. Evaluation 
 
4.1 Taste of the herbal soup 
To begin with, this soup boasts exceptional flavours, thanks to the addition of black chicken, 
which imparts a rich and savoury taste. The inclusion of ginger effectively eliminates any 
residual gaminess from the meat, leaving behind a broth that is purely delicious. Moreover, 
there is no need to worry about an overpowering medicinal taste; quite the contrary, the herbs 
incorporated into the soup enhance its overall flavour profile. For instance, the earthworm is 
salty, it complements the predominantly sweet taste of the other medicinal ingredients such as 
Goji and Longan. 
4.2 Feeling after drinking the herbal soup 
I prepared and consumed this soup after my menstrual cycle, when there typically tends to be 
a deficiency in both qi and blood, resulting in a pale complexion. However, this time, there was 
a noticeable improvement in my overall condition. My body quickly returned to a more 
balanced state, and remarkably, I felt a significant difference. In fact, with leftover ingredients, 
I made the soup again, and this time, I sensed a considerable reduction in damp heat within my 
body. The persistent thirst I used to experience diminished, thanks to the helpful properties of 
licorice. Simultaneously, as my qi and blood became more balanced, my mood improved 
significantly, making me feel more energetic and revitalized. 
4.3 Enhancement 
It is worth noting that those with extreme and severe Qi or Yang deficiency may consider 
reducing the amount of poria, while individuals dealing with sever dampness heat should 
exercise caution with dried longan or opt for a lesser portion, given its heat-inducing 
properties19.
