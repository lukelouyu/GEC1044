
## 1. Early Han Medicine — The Mawangdui Manuscripts

Archaeological discoveries since the 1970s have changed our understanding of early Chinese medicine. The **Mawangdui 馬王堆 manuscripts**, excavated from a second-century BCE Han tomb in Hunan, reveal a medical system very different from the later _Huangdi Neijing 黃帝內經_.

These early texts contain **no developed theory of Yin–Yang, Five Phases, acupuncture, or universal natural laws governing health**. Disease could still be blamed on spirits or demons and treated using **spoken spells, ritual gestures and medicinal substances**.

However, Han medicine was already diverse. The manuscripts include **daoyin 導引 breathing and stretching exercises**, sexual cultivation practices associated with **fangzhongshu 房中術**, pharmaceutical prescriptions and methods of prognosis.

Prognosis was especially important because physicians could suffer social consequences if they attempted to treat patients considered incurable.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Mawangdui shows that early Chinese medicine was plural rather than one unified system.**
>
> Ritual healing, medicines, bodily cultivation and practical observation existed together before the mature framework of **Yin–Yang + Five Phases + meridians** became dominant.

---

## 2. From Early Han Medicine to the _Huangdi Neijing_

By the **late Han period**, Chinese medical theory had changed considerably. The _Huangdi Neijing_ organised health and disease through **natural philosophy and systematic correspondence**, connecting the body with Yin–Yang, Five Phases, qi, zang-fu organs, meridians and the wider cosmos.

This represented the rise of a more scholarly medicine. Physicians increasingly explained disease through **natural processes and internal bodily relationships**, rather than simply through demons or spirits.

The _Neijing_ therefore helped medical elites distinguish themselves from **witches, spirit mediums and religious healers**.

However, religious medicine did not disappear. Ritual healing, pharmaceutical medicine and natural-philosophical medicine continued to coexist.

---

## 3. Acupuncture and Drug Therapy — Two Traditions

Early Chinese medicine contained a major divide between **canonical/acupuncture medicine** and **drug therapy**.

| Canonical / Acupuncture Tradition | Drug / Formula Tradition |
|---|---|
| Associated with scholarly medicine | Often associated with common or popular medicine |
| Based on Yin–Yang, Five Phases and meridians | Based on accumulated experience with medicinal substances |
| Closely associated with the _Huangdi Neijing_ | Recorded in collections of **fang 方** |
| Acupuncture considered prestigious by some elites | Drugs retained associations with earlier witchcraft |
| Strong theoretical framework | Early formularies often contained little theoretical explanation |

Literary elites tended to favour treatments based on **natural philosophy**, especially acupuncture. Drug therapy was often viewed suspiciously because of its association with older magical and demonic healing.

Despite this, practitioners continued collecting useful medicinal prescriptions.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Modern Chinese medicine commonly combines **acupuncture + herbal medicine**, but historically these developed as partly separate medical traditions.

---

## 4. Development of Formula Medicine 方

The earliest surviving pharmaceutical texts show the gradual accumulation of practical drug knowledge.

The Mawangdui text **_Prescriptions for Fifty-Two Ailments_ (Wushier Bingfang 五十二病方)** organised treatments according to categories of illness and contained approximately **283 prescriptions**.

These early formularies were mainly practical:

```text
Ailment
   ↓
Medicinal prescription
```

There was usually little discussion of Yin–Yang, Five Phases or systematic correspondence.

Another Han-period medical text discovered in Gansu contained prescriptions for external injuries, women's and children's disorders, coughing, breathing problems, bleeding and other illnesses.

The basic unit of this pharmaceutical tradition became the **fang 方**, meaning a medicinal formula or prescription.

---

## 5. _Shanghan Lun_ 傷寒論 — Linking Formulae with Theory

A major development occurred with **Zhang Ji 張機 / Zhang Zhongjing 張仲景** and the _Treatise on Cold Damage and Miscellaneous Disorders_.

Part of this work later became the **_Shanghan Lun 傷寒論_**, or _Discussion of Cold Damage_.

Unlike earlier simple formularies, the _Shanghan Lun_ connected **specific clinical patterns with particular medicinal formulae**.

```text
Earlier formula medicine:

Disease / symptom
        ↓
     Formula


Shanghan Lun:

Pattern of symptoms
+ patient's condition
+ progression of illness
        ↓
Specific medicinal formula
```

The text considered both the **external pathogenic influence** and the **patient's ability to resist it**.

Formulae were given names, ingredients, dosages and preparation methods.

Where the _Huangdi Neijing_ was especially important for **medical theory**, the _Shanghan Lun_ became important for **clinical medicinal treatment**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Neijing → theoretical framework**
>
> **Shanghan Lun → clinical application through medicinal formulae**

The _Shanghan Lun_ helped connect empirical drug therapy with the systematic medical theories that had developed during the Han.

Its influence was not immediately dominant. It later became especially important during the **Song dynasty**.

## 6. Religious Daoism and the Search for Immortality

Another major development occurred with the growth of **religious Daoism** toward the end of the Han dynasty.

It is useful to distinguish:

- **Daojia 道家** → philosophical Daoism associated with Laozi and Zhuangzi
- **Daojiao 道教** → organised religious Daoism

Political instability during the collapse of the Han encouraged religious movements that promised **healing, salvation and longevity**.

The **Taiping Dao 太平道 (Way of Great Peace)** used supernatural healing practices including incantations and preparations made from the ashes of talismans.

The **Celestial Masters 天師道** incorporated practices such as confession, deity worship, spirit possession and techniques for cultivating longevity.

Daoist health practices included:

```text
Meditation
    ↓
Moral self-cultivation
    ↓
Dietary control
    ↓
Breathing exercises
    ↓
Daoyin 導引
    ↓
Medicinal substances
    ↓
Alchemy
```

The search for **immortality** encouraged Daoist scholars to investigate plants, animals and minerals.

This unintentionally contributed to the development of **Chinese pharmacology and materia medica (bencao 本草)**.

---

## 7. Ge Hong 葛洪 and Practical Formula Medicine

One of the major representatives of Daoist medicine was **Ge Hong 葛洪**, a physician and Daoist alchemist.

He studied alchemy in search of substances that could produce longevity or immortality. This required extensive investigation of **plants, animals and minerals**.

His important medical work was the **_Emergency Formulae to Keep Up One's Sleeve_ (Zhouhou Beiji Fang 肘後備急方)**.

The book contained **simple, inexpensive and practical treatments** organised according to symptoms and emergencies.

This was important because professional physicians were expensive and difficult for ordinary people to access.

The text could therefore serve as a practical medical handbook for both physicians and common people.

### Tao Hongjing 陶弘景

**Tao Hongjing 陶弘景** later supplemented Ge Hong's work and contributed greatly to pharmacology.

He edited and organised materia medica knowledge, helping to classify and systematise medicinal substances.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The Daoist search for **immortality** contributed directly to medicine because it encouraged systematic investigation of:
>
> **Plants + Animals + Minerals → Materia Medica**

## 8. Six Dynasties — Medical Pluralism

After the collapse of the Han dynasty, China entered several centuries of political division.

Although warfare caused major social disruption, this period also produced significant cultural and intellectual exchange.

Migration and contact between Chinese and non-Chinese populations introduced new ideas into China.

At the same time:

- **Religious Daoism expanded**
- **Buddhism became increasingly influential**
- Practical books on **drug therapy** increased
- Ideas about **health preservation and longevity** became popular

Medicine therefore became increasingly plural.

```text
Classical medical theory
        +
Drug formulae
        +
Daoist cultivation
        +
Alchemy
        +
Buddhist medicine
        +
Popular healing
```

These different traditions interacted strongly during the **Sui and Tang dynasties**.

---

## 9. Buddhism and Chinese Medicine

**Buddhism entered China from India**, especially through monks, merchants and Silk Road exchanges.

Buddhist teachings were not simply copied into China. They were **adapted to existing Chinese religious and cultural traditions**.

Buddhist medicine introduced new models of the body.

### Four Great Elements

The body could be understood through four basic elements:

| Element | Association |
|---|---|
| **Earth** | Solidity |
| **Water** | Fluidity |
| **Fire** | Heat |
| **Wind** | Movement |

Buddhist medicine also discussed **three defects / humoral disturbances**, commonly associated with:

- Wind
- Bile
- Phlegm

These concepts were closely related to ancient Indian medical traditions.

---

## 10. Buddhist Healing and Meditation

Buddhism also introduced healing deities and practices.

Important Buddhist healing figures included:

- **Guanyin 觀音 / Avalokiteśvara**
- **Medicine Buddha 藥師佛**

These figures were believed to provide protection against disease and miraculous healing.

Buddhist meditation also influenced health practices.

The **mindfulness of breathing** technique required practitioners to focus on inhalation and exhalation, sometimes by counting breaths.

This resembled existing Chinese practices such as:

- **Daoyin 導引**
- **Yangsheng 養生 — nourishing life**

Thus, Buddhist practices became incorporated into Chinese ideas about bodily cultivation.

---

## 11. Sui Dynasty — Medicine Becomes a State Institution

The **Sui dynasty (581–618)** reunified China after centuries of political division.

Both major Sui emperors had close relationships with Buddhism.

Under **Emperor Yang 隋煬帝**, the government established an **Imperial Medical Office**.

This represented an important change because medicine increasingly became a responsibility of the **central government**.

The state became involved in:

- Medical administration
- Medical education
- Research
- Training

This development later expanded significantly during the Song dynasty.

---

## 12. Tang Medicine — Sun Simiao 孫思邈

The peace and prosperity of the early **Tang dynasty** encouraged medical innovation.

The physician **Sun Simiao 孫思邈**, who lived through the Sui and early Tang periods, represents the interaction between Chinese, Daoist and Buddhist medicine.

His important work was the:

**_Essential Formulae Worth a Thousand Gold Pieces_  
(Qianjin Yaofang 千金要方)**

Sun Simiao combined:

- Pharmaceutical medicine
- Health preservation
- Daoist cultivation
- Buddhist and Indian influences

He recorded **18 daoyin exercises** described as Indian or Brahman techniques.

Some exercises required practitioners to sit in the **lotus position**, which entered China through Buddhist influence.

Tang medicine therefore became increasingly cosmopolitan.

---

## 13. Wang Bing 王冰 and the _Huangdi Neijing_

Another important Tang development was the work of **Wang Bing 王冰**.

In the eighth century, Wang Bing corrected, rearranged and added material to the **_Suwen 素問_**, the _Basic Questions_ section of the _Huangdi Neijing_.

His edition was completed in **762 CE**.

Wang Bing emphasised:

- **Self-cultivation**
- Living according to the **Dao**
- Aligning the body with movements of the **cosmos**
- Maintaining harmony between humans and nature

His work shows that the _Huangdi Neijing_ was not transmitted unchanged from antiquity.

Later scholars **edited, reorganised and reinterpreted classical medical texts**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Classical medical texts were living traditions.**
>
> Wang Bing's Tang-period edition strongly influenced how later generations understood the _Huangdi Neijing_.

---

## 14. Decline of Buddhist Medical Influence

Buddhism remained influential during the Sui and early Tang, but it did not become the dominant foundation of later Chinese medicine.

During the late Tang, political instability and growing hostility toward foreign religions contributed to the **persecution of Buddhism from 842–845**.

At the same time, scholars increasingly promoted a **return to older Chinese classical traditions**.

As a result:

```text
Buddhist medical influence
        ↓
Gradually reduced in official medicine

Classical Chinese medicine
+ Daoist influence
        ↓
Remained dominant
```

During the later **Song dynasty**, the government further strengthened this development by editing, printing and promoting classical medical texts.

---

## 15. Han → Tang Development at a Glance

| Period | Major Development | Medical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Early Han** | Mawangdui manuscripts | Spirits, drugs, daoyin and cultivation coexist |
| **Late Han** | _Huangdi Neijing_ | Yin–Yang, Five Phases, qi and meridians become central |
| **Late Han / c. 220** | _Shanghan Lun_ | Connects medicinal formulae with clinical patterns |
| **Late Han onward** | Religious Daoism | Longevity and immortality encourage pharmacological research |
| **Six Dynasties** | Ge Hong and Tao Hongjing | Growth of formularies and materia medica |
| **Six Dynasties–Tang** | Buddhism | Indian theories, meditation and healing practices enter China |
| **Sui** | Imperial Medical Office | Medicine becomes increasingly institutionalised |
| **Tang** | Sun Simiao | Integration of drug therapy, cultivation and Buddhist influence |
| **Tang, 762** | Wang Bing edits _Suwen_ | Daoist and cosmological interpretation of the _Neijing_ |
| **Late Tang** | Buddhist persecution and classicism | Buddhist influence declines; classical Chinese traditions regain authority |

## Core Idea

> [!ABSTRACT]
> Chinese medicine from the **Han to Tang was not one unchanged medical tradition**. It developed through the interaction of several different traditions.
>
> **Early ritual medicine + practical drug therapy**
>
> ↓
>
> **Natural philosophy + Yin–Yang + Five Phases + meridians**
>
> ↓
>
> **_Huangdi Neijing_ → systematic medical theory**
>
> **_Shanghan Lun_ → formula-based clinical medicine**
>
> ↓
>
> **Daoist immortality and alchemy → expansion of pharmacology**
>
> **Buddhism → new doctrines, meditation and cross-cultural healing**
>
> ↓
>
> **Sui–Tang state institutions + scholarly synthesis**
>
> The major historical process was therefore **integration and selective canonisation**. Different healing traditions interacted, while scholars and governments gradually determined which texts, theories and practices would become authoritative Chinese medicine.
>
> This Han–Tang foundation prepared the way for the **Song dynasty**, when classical texts were systematically edited and printed, drug therapy gained greater prestige, and medicine became increasingly scholarly and institutionalised.
