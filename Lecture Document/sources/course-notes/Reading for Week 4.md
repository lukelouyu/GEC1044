# The Creative Reception of Chinese Medicine in the West

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Argument
> Paul U. Unschuld argues that much of what is presented as **“Traditional Chinese Medicine” (TCM) in the West is not a direct continuation of historical Chinese medicine**, but a **creative Western reconstruction** shaped by translation choices, modern cultural expectations, political circumstances, and the search for alternatives to biomedicine.
>
> The chapter therefore asks us to distinguish between **historical Chinese medicine** and the **modern Western image of TCM**.

---

## 1. The Problem: Popular Western Images of TCM

Modern descriptions of TCM often present it as:

- thousands of years old;
- founded by legendary rulers such as **Shen Nong 神農** and **Huang Di 黃帝**;
- fundamentally based on **Daoism**;
- a medicine of **energy flowing through meridians**;
- a holistic system that treats the whole person;
- fundamentally different from Western medicine.

Unschuld argues that many of these claims are **historically inaccurate, exaggerated, or oversimplified**.

Popular Western descriptions frequently present Chinese medicine as a single, coherent tradition with an ancient and unchanged philosophy. Historically, however, Chinese medicine consisted of **many competing and overlapping approaches**, and its concepts changed considerably over time.

> [!IMPORTANT] Main Criticism
> Western TCM often turns a **diverse historical medical tradition** into a **single, idealised system**.

---

## 2. Myth of an Extremely Ancient and Unchanging TCM

Some Western sources claim that Chinese medicine is **3,000, 6,000, or even 10,000 years old**.

Unschuld criticises this tendency because the supposed age of TCM is often used as evidence of its authority or effectiveness.

The problem is that modern TCM did **not exist as one unified system for thousands of years**.

Historical Chinese medicine developed through:

**different dynasties + changing theories + competing physicians + religious traditions + political reforms + new medical texts**

Therefore, treating TCM as one continuous and unchanged medical system obscures its historical development.

> [!TIP] Key Point
> **Old ≠ unchanged**
>
> The longer history of Chinese medicine contains many transformations, debates and discontinuities.

---

## 3. Problematic Association with Daoism

Western audiences often associate TCM closely with **Daoism**, especially because Daoism fits modern Western interests in:

- natural living;
- harmony;
- spirituality;
- alternative lifestyles;
- non-material understandings of the body.

Unschuld argues that this connection is frequently exaggerated.

Historical Chinese medicine drew from many intellectual and cultural influences, including:

- natural philosophy;
- political metaphors;
- Confucianism;
- Daoist practices;
- religious healing;
- state institutions;
- empirical medical traditions.

It therefore cannot simply be described as **“medicine derived from Daoism.”**

### Why This Matters

If TCM is presented as essentially Daoist, Western readers may interpret the entire tradition through concepts such as:

**harmony + energy + nature + spirituality**

while overlooking other historical dimensions such as:

**demonic healing + moral categories + bureaucracy + political metaphors + disease causality**

---

## 4. Historical Chinese Medicine Was More Diverse

Unschuld rejects broad claims such as:

> Chinese medicine is holistic, while Western medicine is reductionist.

or

> Chinese medicine thinks in networks, while Western medicine thinks only in linear cause-and-effect relationships.

These contrasts are too simple.

Historical Chinese physicians also discussed **causes of disease**, sometimes in highly systematic ways.

The chapter highlights how Western accounts can ignore historical physicians and texts that explicitly addressed **disease causality**.

Therefore:

**Chinese medicine ≠ purely relational or holistic thinking**

and

**Western medicine ≠ purely reductionist or mechanical thinking**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The chapter warns against creating a false **East-versus-West binary**.

---

# Georges Soulié de Morant

## 5. A Major Influence on Western Acupuncture

A major turning point in the Western reception of Chinese medicine was the work of **Georges Soulié de Morant (1878–1955)**.

His writings on acupuncture became highly influential in France and later throughout Europe and the wider Western world.

Unschuld credits him with a major role in shaping Western terminology, but argues that some of his translations significantly **distorted Chinese concepts**.

Two of his most influential choices were:

1. translating the channels as **“meridians”**
2. translating **qi 氣 as “energy”**

---

## 6. “Meridian” as a Western Translation

Ancient Chinese medicine described internal **conduits or pathways** through which qi and blood moved.

Soulié de Morant translated these pathways as:

**“meridians”**

The term likely came from the visual similarity between lines drawn on Chinese body models and geographical meridians drawn on globes.

Unschuld argues that this is misleading because the original Chinese concept was not based on geographical meridians.

Nevertheless, the term became standard in Western acupuncture.

> [!TIP]
> **“Meridian” is not a literal Chinese medical concept.**
>
> It is a Western interpretive term that became normalised.

---

## 7. Qi 氣 as “Energy”

Soulié de Morant's most influential translation was:

**qi 氣 = energy**

Unschuld argues strongly that this is a **European projection** rather than an historically accurate translation.

Qi had many meanings across Chinese history, but the modern physical concept of **energy** was not equivalent to historical Chinese qi.

Once qi became translated as energy, Chinese medicine could easily be presented as:

**energy medicine**

This allowed Western audiences to imagine the body as an energetic network whose health depended upon the smooth flow of invisible energy.

### Why the Translation Became Attractive

The interpretation appealed to both Western and Chinese audiences because:

- it made Chinese medicine sound compatible with modern science;
- it suited Western interest in alternative healing;
- it resonated with the cultural atmosphere of the **1970s energy crisis**;
- it supported the idea of TCM as a fundamentally different medical system.

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Exam Point
> According to Unschuld:
>
> **qi ≠ simply “energy.”**
>
> Translating qi as energy helped create a **modern Western image of Chinese medicine** that differed from its historical Chinese context.

---

# China Opens to the West

## 8. The 1970s and the Global Expansion of TCM

A second major development occurred when the People's Republic of China opened more widely to Western visitors in the **1970s**.

After **Richard Nixon's 1972 visit to China**, Western doctors, journalists and visitors gained greater access to Chinese medicine.

However, many early visitors:

- did not know Chinese;
- had little knowledge of Chinese medical history;
- relied on official guides and interpreters;
- saw only officially approved forms of medicine.

They therefore transmitted the **official modern Chinese account of TCM** to Western audiences.

This helped establish a simplified and standardised image of TCM outside China.

---

## 9. TCM Was Itself a Modern Reconstruction

Unschuld stresses that what China presented internationally as **Traditional Chinese Medicine** had itself been partly reconstructed during the twentieth century.

In the **1950s**, the PRC government promoted and institutionalised Chinese medicine.

This involved:

- selecting useful elements from older traditions;
- compiling classical material;
- removing or downplaying some historical practices;
- creating modern academies;
- standardising teaching;
- making Chinese medicine compatible with contemporary state medicine.

Thus, Westerners often assumed they were receiving a complete ancient tradition when they were actually receiving a **modern state-organised selection of older medical ideas**.

### Important Distinction

```text
Historical Chinese medicine
        ↓
many traditions, theories, practices
        ↓
20th-century Chinese reconstruction
        ↓
standardised TCM
        ↓
Western reinterpretation
        ↓
modern Western TCM
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> TCM in the West is therefore the result of **multiple layers of reinterpretation**.

---

# Ted Kaptchuk and the Holistic Image

## 10. The Web That Has No Weaver

Ted Kaptchuk's **The Web That Has No Weaver** became one of the most influential Western books on Chinese medicine.

Kaptchuk described Chinese medicine as focusing more on:

- patterns;
- relationships;
- simultaneity;
- networks;

than on simple linear causality.

This interpretation was highly attractive in the West because it appeared during a period when many people were searching for more **holistic worldviews**.

Chinese medicine was therefore presented as a medicine in which:

**everything is interconnected**

rather than as a system based on isolated causes.

### Unschuld's Criticism

Unschuld argues that this representation is historically incomplete because many Chinese physicians explicitly discussed **causal explanations of disease**.

The Western idea that:

**Chinese medicine = network thinking**

while

**Western medicine = linear causality**

is therefore another oversimplification.

---

## 11. Why Holism Became So Attractive

The Western reception of TCM cannot be separated from dissatisfaction with modern biomedicine.

Patients and practitioners often felt that Western medicine:

- focused too strongly on organs or disease categories;
- ignored subjective experiences;
- separated mind from body;
- treated symptoms instead of the whole person.

Chinese medicine was therefore increasingly presented as the opposite:

**Western medicine → materialistic / fragmented**

**Chinese medicine → holistic / energetic / patient-centred**

Unschuld does not deny that patients may benefit from Chinese medical treatment.

His criticism is that **successful treatment does not justify rewriting history**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A medical system can be clinically useful without requiring historically inaccurate claims about its origins or philosophy.

---

# Manfred Porkert

## 12. Creating a New Technical Language

Another influential Western interpreter was **Manfred Porkert (1933–2015)**.

Porkert believed Chinese medical concepts were as precise as Western scientific terminology.

To demonstrate this, he translated Chinese terms into elaborate **Latin and Greek terminology**.

Examples included:

| Chinese Concept | Porkert's Terminology |
|---|---|
| Acupuncture point 穴 | *foramen* |
| Zang 臟 | *orbis* / functional circle |
| Ying qi 營氣 | *Ch'i constructivum* |
| Wei qi 衛氣 | *Ch'i defensivum* |
| Xie qi 邪氣 | *Ch'i heteropathicum* |

Although this vocabulary sounded technical and scientific, Unschuld argues that it removed the original concepts from their **historical and cultural context**.

---

## 13. Loss of Political and Moral Meaning

Many classical Chinese medical terms were drawn from everyday political, military and moral language.

For example:

### Ying Qi 營氣
Can evoke the image of **stationed troops**.

### Wei Qi 衛氣
Can evoke **guards or defensive forces**.

Together, these terms carry metaphorical meanings related to **military organisation and protection**.

Porkert's translations turned them into abstract technical concepts such as:

**constructive energy** and **defensive energy**

This erased the original imagery.

---

## 14. Xie 邪 and Zheng 正

A particularly important example involves:

**xie 邪** and **zheng 正**

Historically, these terms carry strong moral and political meanings:

- **xie 邪** = evil, heterodox, false, misguided;
- **zheng 正** = correct, upright, orthodox, righteous.

In medicine, these concepts were therefore connected to broader Chinese categories of:

**order vs disorder**

**orthodoxy vs heterodoxy**

**upright vs deviant**

When Porkert translated them as abstract technical terms such as **heteropathic** and **orthopathic**, much of this historical meaning disappeared.

> [!TIP] Why Translation Matters
> Translation is not neutral.
>
> Changing the vocabulary can change how an entire medical system is understood.

---

# The Problem of East–West Oppositions

## 15. Chinese Medicine vs Western Medicine

Western writers frequently create sharp contrasts such as:

| Western Medicine | Chinese Medicine |
|---|---|
| Material | Energetic |
| Reductionist | Holistic |
| Linear causality | Network relationships |
| Objective | Subjective |
| Body divided into parts | Whole person |
| Scientific | Philosophical |

Unschuld argues that these binary categories are historically misleading.

Both Chinese and Western medicine contain:

- multiple schools of thought;
- disagreements;
- changing theories;
- different methods;
- mixtures of causal and relational reasoning.

Reducing each tradition to one defining characteristic creates an artificial comparison.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The problem is not simply that the stereotypes are inaccurate.
>
> They also hide the **internal diversity** of both Chinese and Western medicine.

---

# TCM as a Belief System

## 16. When TCM Becomes Unfalsifiable

Unschuld is especially critical when people claim that TCM cannot be judged by normal scientific or historical standards because it represents a completely different reality.

For example, if someone claims that:

**qi cannot be demonstrated scientifically because Western science is incapable of understanding it**

then TCM becomes difficult or impossible to test.

At this point, Unschuld argues, TCM begins functioning more like a **belief system**.

Claims are protected from criticism because any failure to demonstrate them can be blamed on the limitations of Western science.

### Important Distinction

Unschuld is not saying that all TCM practice is simply belief.

His criticism is directed toward arguments that make TCM:

**immune to historical criticism + immune to scientific testing**

---

# Multiple TCMs in the West

## 17. No Single Western TCM

The Western reception of Chinese medicine has produced many different schools.

At one end are practitioners who argue that modern Chinese TCM has lost too much historical knowledge and who attempt to recover:

**classical / canonical medicine**

At the other end are practitioners who attempt to combine traditional concepts with:

- neuroscience;
- neuroanatomy;
- modern pain science;
- contemporary biomedical knowledge.

Other groups emphasise:

- energy medicine;
- acupuncture;
- spirituality;
- classical texts;
- modern standardised TCM;
- integrative medicine.

Therefore, there is no single unified version of TCM in the West.

---

## 18. A Tradition of “Creative Reception”

The phrase **creative reception** is central to the chapter.

Westerners did not simply receive Chinese medicine unchanged.

Instead they:

1. **selected** certain concepts;
2. **translated** them into Western vocabulary;
3. **interpreted** them using Western cultural expectations;
4. **reorganised** them into new systems;
5. sometimes **projected these new systems back onto Chinese history**.

This produced forms of medicine that may be inspired by Chinese medicine but are not identical to historical Chinese practice.

```text
Chinese concepts
      ↓
Translation
      ↓
Selection
      ↓
Western cultural expectations
      ↓
Holism / energy / spirituality
      ↓
Modern Western TCM
```

# Major Figures to Remember

| Figure | Contribution to Western TCM | Unschuld's Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **Georges Soulié de Morant** | Popularised acupuncture in Europe | “Meridian” and especially **qi = energy** distorted historical concepts |
| **Ted Kaptchuk** | Popularised Chinese medicine as relational and holistic | Overemphasised network thinking and downplayed historical Chinese causal theories |
| **Manfred Porkert** | Created highly technical Latin terminology | Removed Chinese concepts from their political, moral and cultural meanings |
| **PRC government** | Standardised and institutionalised TCM after the 1950s | Produced a selected modern version that Westerners often mistook for timeless tradition |

# Connection to Earlier Course Themes

## 19. Medicine Is Shaped by Culture

This reading strongly reinforces the course idea that **medicine is culturally constructed**.

Medical ideas are shaped not only by disease and treatment but also by:

- political systems;
- language;
- translation;
- philosophy;
- historical memory;
- social expectations.

When Chinese medicine moved to the West, it was transformed because Western audiences interpreted it through their own concerns.

### Example

Historical Chinese:

**qi 氣**

became Western:

**energy**

which then supported ideas about:

**energy balance + energetic bodies + energetic healing**

The resulting concept reflects both **Chinese history and Western cultural interests**.

---

## 20. Tradition Is Not Static

The chapter also challenges the assumption that **traditional medicine must be ancient and unchanged**.

What is called “traditional” may itself be:

- recently standardised;
- politically reconstructed;
- selectively preserved;
- modernised;
- translated;
- adapted to new audiences.

Therefore:

> **Tradition is often produced through adaptation.**

This is especially important for understanding how modern **TCM** emerged.

# Exam-Focused Summary

**Central Argument:** Western TCM is not a simple transmission of ancient Chinese medicine. It is a **creative reconstruction** produced through translation, political standardisation, cultural expectations and dissatisfaction with biomedicine.

**Popular Western myths:** TCM is often described as extremely ancient, purely Daoist, holistic, energetic and unchanged. Unschuld argues that these descriptions simplify a historically diverse medical tradition.

**Soulié de Morant:** Introduced two influential Western translations:
- conduits → **meridians**
- qi → **energy**

The second became especially important in constructing Chinese medicine as **energy medicine**.

**1970s:** China's opening to the West allowed Western visitors to encounter official PRC TCM, but many lacked Chinese language skills and historical knowledge. They often transmitted the state's simplified and standardised account.

**Ted Kaptchuk:** Popularised the idea that Chinese medicine focuses on networks, relationships and holism rather than causality. Unschuld argues that historical Chinese physicians also used causal reasoning.

**Manfred Porkert:** Created technical Latin terminology for Chinese concepts, but this often removed their original political, military and moral metaphors.

**East–West binary:** Claims such as “Western = reductionist” and “Chinese = holistic” are overly simplistic. Both traditions are historically diverse.

**Belief system:** TCM can become unfalsifiable when supporters argue that it cannot be assessed by scientific or historical standards.

**Modern situation:** There is no single TCM in the West. Different schools promote classical medicine, standardised TCM, energy medicine, acupuncture or biomedical integration.

> [!SUMMARY] One-Sentence Takeaway
> **The TCM known in the West is best understood not as an unchanged ancient tradition, but as a modern, culturally shaped reinterpretation of Chinese medicine created through translation, selection and adaptation.**
