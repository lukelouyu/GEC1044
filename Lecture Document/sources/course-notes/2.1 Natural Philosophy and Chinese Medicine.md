
## 1. From Shang Spirit Medicine to Zhou Cosmology

During the **Shang dynasty**, illness and misfortune were often attributed to **unhappy ancestors** actively interfering with the living. Healing therefore involved **divination, ritual negotiation and priestly mediation**. Healers needed knowledge of the patient's lineage because treatment depended on identifying and communicating with the correct ancestor.

Under the **Zhou dynasty**, the worldview gradually shifted. The cosmos became increasingly understood through an impersonal **Heaven (Tian 天)** rather than solely through ancestral spirits. Nevertheless, ancestors remained medically important, while **demons/ghosts (gui 鬼)** became another possible source of illness. Medicine therefore still contained divination and ritual negotiation, but increasingly included **exorcistic practices**.

> [!IMPORTANT]  
> The transition was **gradual rather than an immediate replacement of religion by philosophy**. Older beliefs in ancestors and demons continued while new cosmological explanations developed.

---

## 2. Warring States: Crisis and Intellectual Change

As Zhou authority weakened from around the **8th century BCE**, competing feudal states increasingly fought for territory and resources. During the **Warring States period (481–221 BCE, as given in the lecture)**, enormous armies and intense warfare produced unprecedented violence and insecurity. People also believed that soldiers and civilians killed violently could become **vengeful demons**, encouraging more aggressive forms of ritual healing.

At the same time, widespread disorder stimulated major intellectual innovation. Thinkers increasingly argued that, beneath human chaos, the **cosmos possessed an enduring natural order**. Nature moved through regular cycles, and humans were understood as participants within these cycles. Therefore:

**Natural order → humans should follow it → deviation produces disorder/calamity**

Confucian thinkers attempted to restore harmony through proper government and society, while Daoist approaches emphasized **living according to the natural order**. These developments contributed to the gradual separation of philosophical explanation from purely supernatural explanations.

---

## 3. Rise of Chinese Natural Philosophy

By the late Zhou/Warring States period, explanations based primarily on spirits and demons increasingly coexisted with—and were eventually supplemented by—a system involving **Yin–Yang, Five Phases and qi**. Different intellectual traditions adopted these concepts for their own explanations of the **cosmos, nature, humans and society**. Medical thinkers likewise incorporated them into new theories of illness and the body.

This allowed Chinese medicine increasingly to explain disease through **natural relationships and processes**, rather than relying exclusively on supernatural agents.

> [!ABSTRACT]  
> **Major transformation:**  
> `Supernatural causation → Natural cosmology → Systematic medical theory`

---

# 4. Yin–Yang 陰陽

**Yin and Yang** describe two **opposing but complementary aspects** of phenomena. Originally, _yin_ referred to the **shady side of a hill**, while _yang_ referred to the **sunny side**. They became a general model for understanding the continual **rise, decline and transformation of natural phenomena**.

The key idea is therefore not simply “two opposites,” but **dynamic relationship**:

> **Yin ↔ Yang**  
> opposition + complementarity + continual transformation

By the late Han period, Yin–Yang had become a major explanatory framework used in classical texts and Chinese medicine.

---

## 5. Creation in the _Yijing_ 易經

The lecture connects Yin–Yang cosmology with the **Book of Changes (_Yijing_)**, originally associated with divination but later reinterpreted cosmologically by different intellectual traditions.

The cosmological sequence can be remembered as:

```text
Wuji 無極
"without limit / infinity"
        ↓
Taiji 太極
Grand Ultimate / One
        ↓
Liangyi 兩儀
Two Forms: Yin + Yang
        ↓
Sixiang 四象
Four Images
        ↓
Bagua 八卦
Eight Trigrams
        ↓
64 Hexagrams
        ↓
10,000 Things / phenomenal world
```

**Wuji 無極** represents an undifferentiated state or “without limit.” **Taiji 太極** represents the Grand Ultimate or unity from which differentiation emerges. Taiji produces the **Two Forms (Liangyi)**, identified with Yin and Yang; these generate the **Four Images (Sixiang)** and subsequently the **Eight Trigrams (Bagua)**. Combining two trigrams produces a **hexagram**, giving **64 possible hexagrams**.

---

## 6. Yao 爻 and the Eight Trigrams

In the _Yijing_, Yin and Yang are represented through lines called **yao 爻**:

```text
Yang yao:  ━━━━━     unbroken line
Yin yao:   ━━  ━━    broken line
```

Combinations of these lines generate the Four Images and eventually the **Eight Trigrams**. The trigrams were correlated with phenomena such as **heaven, fire, thunder, wind, water, mountain and earth**, as well as seasons and directions. Two particularly important trigrams are **Qian 乾 (Heaven)** and **Kun 坤 (Earth)**, representing major limits/poles of the natural world.

---

# 7. Systematic Correspondence

Scholars use **systematic correspondence** to describe the Chinese tendency to organize different phenomena into interconnected classificatory schemes.

> [!IMPORTANT]  
> **Core principle:** things belonging to different domains of reality can be understood as corresponding to one another.

For example:

```text
Cosmic pattern
     ↕
Seasons ↔ Directions ↔ Yin–Yang ↔ Trigrams
     ↕
Five Phases ↔ Human body ↔ Disease ↔ Treatment
```

Thus, natural phenomena, bodily processes and medical conditions were not treated as completely separate categories. They could be interpreted through the **same cosmological relationships**. This became fundamental to Chinese medicine from the **Han dynasty onward**.

---

# 8. Five Phases 五行 (_Wuxing_)

Another major system was the **Five Phases (Wuxing 五行)**. The lecture explains that these concepts existed earlier but were subsequently **redefined and systematized by Warring States thinkers**. They became five major categories of natural processes:

**Wood 木 → Fire 火 → Earth 土 → Metal 金 → Water 水**

“**Phases**” or “agents” is often preferable to “elements” because _wuxing_ describes **dynamic processes and relationships**, rather than five permanently fixed substances.

|Phase|Seasonal association|Basic imagery/process|
|---|---|---|
|**Wood 木**|Spring|Birth, growth, new shoots|
|**Fire 火**|Early summer|Flowering, warmth, activity|
|**Earth 土**|Late/long summer|Ripening, stabilization|
|**Metal 金**|Autumn|Harvest, collection|
|**Water 水**|Winter|Storage, stillness, latent life|

The five-phase cycle therefore models recurring natural transformation rather than static material composition.

---

## 9. Mutual Generation 相生

The **generating/production cycle (xiangsheng 相生)** describes how one phase supports the next:

```text
Wood → Fire → Earth → Metal → Water
 ↑                              ↓
 └──────────────────────────────┘
```

A simple interpretation is:

- **Wood generates Fire** — wood fuels fire.
    
- **Fire generates Earth** — ash returns to earth.
    
- **Earth generates Metal** — metal/minerals arise from earth.
    
- **Metal generates Water** — traditionally incorporated into the correspondence cycle.
    
- **Water generates Wood** — water supports plant growth.
    

The cycle represents **birth → growth → maturation → collection → storage → renewed birth**.

---

## 10. Mutual Control 相克

The Five Phases also contain a **controlling/restraining cycle (xiangke 相克)**. Its purpose is to prevent any one phase from becoming excessively dominant.

```text
Wood ─controls→ Earth
Earth ─controls→ Water
Water ─controls→ Fire
Fire ─controls→ Metal
Metal ─controls→ Wood
```

Examples include **water extinguishing fire**, **fire melting metal**, **metal cutting wood**, and **wood penetrating/breaking earth**. Control is therefore not inherently destructive—it functions as a **balancing mechanism**.

> [!IMPORTANT]  
> Chinese medicine places enormous emphasis on **balance**. Normal control maintains order; **excessive control** becomes destructive and creates pathological imbalance.

---

# 11. Yin–Yang + Five Phases

Eventually, **Yin–Yang and Five-Phase theory were combined**, producing a much more elaborate system of correspondences. Increasing numbers of phenomena—seasons, directions, natural processes and eventually bodily functions—could be classified according to these frameworks.

```text
Yin–Yang
  + 
Five Phases
  ↓
Systematic Correspondence
  ↓
Cosmos ↔ Nature ↔ Human Body
  ↓
Medical theory of physiology,
disease and treatment
```

The lecture also connects the development of these theories to the violent **Warring States environment**, noting that concepts such as opposition, generation, restraint, domination and destruction emerged within a society accustomed to conflict.

---

# 12. The Human Body as a Microcosm

Chinese medical thinkers were **not unaware of physical anatomy**; the lecture notes that investigation of the body's interior occurred during the Han period. However, Chinese medicine increasingly emphasized **physiological functions and relationships** patterned after the external natural world.

The body was therefore not understood as an isolated object. Instead:

> **Human body = microcosm of the larger cosmos**

```text
Heaven 天
   ↕
Humanity 人
   ↕
Earth 地
```

Because the same natural patterns were believed to operate throughout reality, concepts such as **Yin–Yang and the Five Phases** could explain the **structure and functions of the body, development of disorders and principles of treatment**. By the end of the **Han dynasty**, the basic structure of this conceptual medical framework had largely taken shape.

---

## Key Takeaway

> [!ABSTRACT] Core Argument  
> Chinese medicine gradually shifted from explanations centred on **ancestors, spirits and demons** toward a **natural-philosophical model**. During the Warring States and Han periods, **Yin–Yang, Five Phases and systematic correspondence** provided a framework linking **cosmos → nature → human body → disease → treatment**. The body consequently came to be understood as a **microcosm embedded within the larger universe**, rather than as an entirely independent entity.
