# Reading for Week 5 — A Brief History of Chinese Medicine in Singapore

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Argument
> Yan Yang argues that Chinese medicine in Singapore developed through a **distinctive process of localisation and modernisation** shaped by three main agents:
>
> **government + professional associations + religious organisations**
>
> Singapore's TCM system is therefore not simply a copy of China's. It reflects the interaction of **Chinese medical traditions, British colonial institutions, biomedical standards, local community needs, and Singapore's regulatory culture**.

---

## 1. Chinese Medicine Arrives in Singapore

Chinese medicine arrived with **Chinese migrants from the nineteenth century onward**, especially during the British colonial period.

At the time, there was no comprehensive municipal healthcare or poor-relief system. Chinese medical institutions therefore filled an important gap for working-class Chinese communities.

Treatment was provided through:

- medical halls;
- charitable medical institutions;
- clan-based recuperation centres;
- temples;
- marketplaces;
- physicians' homes.

Chinese medicine thus developed first as a **community-based healthcare system**, especially for people who had limited access to Western medical care.

> [!TIP] Key Point
> In early Singapore, Chinese medicine was important not only because of culture, but because it provided **accessible healthcare where formal medical services were limited**.

---

## 2. Chinese Medicine in Contemporary Singapore

Western medicine later became the dominant healthcare system, but Chinese medicine remained popular.

A 1994 government survey reported that:

- **45%** of Singaporeans had consulted a TCM practitioner at least once;
- **19%** had visited a TCM clinic in the previous year;
- consultation was highest among Chinese respondents;
- common reasons included **sprains, aches, pain and common colds**.

Chinese medicine in Singapore is mainly confined to **outpatient care**.

Practitioners are officially referred to as:

**TCM practitioners / Chinese physicians / herbalists / therapists**

rather than "doctors", because the title **doctor** is reserved for registered Western medical practitioners.

---

# State Laws and Policies

## 3. Medical Registration Ordinance 1905

The **Medical Registration Ordinance of 1905** was the first major medical law under British rule.

It explicitly stated that the law should not prevent the practice of:

**Indian, Chinese or other Asian systems of therapeutics.**

This had three major consequences.

### 1. Chinese Medicine Was Allowed to Continue

The colonial state did not ban TCM, leaving space for it to develop.

### 2. Western Medicine Became the Official System

Chinese medicine was classified as a **"native" system**, while Western medicine was positioned as the official and modern medical system.

### 3. Chinese and Western Medicine Were Kept Separate

The colonial framework established a boundary between the two systems.

This separation remained an important characteristic of Singapore medicine even much later.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Singapore's medical pluralism did **not** mean equal status.
>
> **Western medicine = official system**
>
> **Chinese medicine = tolerated but separate system**

---

## 4. Chinese Medicine as a Business

Because Chinese medicine was not integrated into the official healthcare system, TCM practitioners were treated largely as **business operators**.

They therefore had to pay:

- business registration fees;
- signboard taxes.

This reflects the colonial government's approach:

**allow the practice → but do not recognise it as part of official medicine**

---

## 5. Japanese Occupation: First Direct Regulation

During the **Japanese Occupation (1942–1945)**, Chinese physicians were directly regulated by the government for the first time.

Practitioners had to:

- register;
- pass examinations;
- obtain certificates before practising.

Only registered practitioners were given quotas of Chinese herbs.

However, the main goal was not simply medical professionalisation. The regulation also helped the Japanese authorities **control herb supplies and the Chinese community**.

---

## 6. Post-War Expansion of Western Medicine

After the Second World War, the returning British administration expanded healthcare through the **Medical Plan**.

This marked the beginning of the stronger institutional dominance of **Western medicine** in Singapore.

At the same time, the government began indirectly regulating Chinese medicine through the:

**Medicines (Advertisement and Sale) Act 1955**

The law restricted exaggerated or "unscientific" medical advertisements.

Claims such as:

**"guaranteed to cure"**

were prohibited for a range of serious diseases and conditions.

### Significance

The government increasingly judged medical claims according to:

**science + evidence + biomedical standards**

This logic became more important in later regulation of TCM.

---

# Biomedical Standards and TCM

## 7. Scientific Comparison After Independence

After Singapore became independent in **1965**, colonial medical laws and policies largely remained.

Chinese medicine still lacked direct statutory regulation.

The government therefore commissioned comparative research between Chinese medicine and Western medicine.

The studies attempted to determine:

- whether Chinese and Western diagnoses correlated;
- similarities between the two systems;
- differences between them.

The research found some similarities but also emphasised major **epistemological differences**.

This became important because later government regulation increasingly assessed TCM using **biomedical concepts of scientific evidence**.

---

## 8. The Berberine / Huanglian Controversy

A major conflict occurred in **1978**, when berberine / huanglian-related medicine was restricted under the **Poisons Act**.

Biomedical authorities argued that tests showed harmful effects on:

- babies;
- pregnant women.

The **Singapore Chinese Physicians' Association (SCPA)** challenged the decision and cited research from Chinese physicians in China.

However, the biomedical committee rejected much of this evidence because it was grounded in **TCM theory rather than accepted scientific methodology**.

### Why This Case Matters

This episode demonstrated the government's position:

> Chinese medicine could continue, but its safety and legitimacy would increasingly be judged according to **biomedical scientific criteria**.

It marked a move from indirect regulation toward more explicit biomedical oversight of TCM.

---

# TCM Practitioners Act 2000

## 9. Transition to Direct State Regulation

The **Traditional Chinese Medicine Practitioners Act 2000** marked a major turning point.

Before 2000:

**TCM was mainly self-regulated by professional associations.**

After 2000:

**TCM became directly regulated by the state.**

The government established the:

**Traditional Chinese Medicine Practitioners Board (TCMPB)**

Its structure was closely modelled on the regulation of Western medical doctors.

The TCM system adopted similar:

- ethical codes;
- disciplinary procedures;
- investigation processes;
- penalties for misconduct;
- professional registration systems.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The modernisation of TCM in Singapore was strongly shaped by the **administrative model of Western medicine**.

---

## 10. Separation from Western Medicine Remains

Even after formal regulation, Chinese and Western medicine remain institutionally separate.

TCM practitioners are not allowed to:

- use Western diagnostic equipment as part of TCM practice;
- prescribe Western pharmaceuticals.

Chinese proprietary medicines must also not contain undeclared Western drugs.

This preserves the historical boundary established during the colonial period.

---

## 11. Registration and National Examination

During the transition to statutory regulation, experienced practitioners were grouped according to:

- years of experience;
- previous qualifications;
- professional certificates.

Some older practitioners were partially or fully exempted from the new qualifying examination.

After the transitional period ended in **2004**, all new practitioners had to pass the **national qualifying examination** to register.

### Growth of Registered Practitioners

The chart in the chapter shows steady growth in registered practitioners from **2004 to 2015**.

| Year | TCM Physicians | Acupuncturists |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2004 | 1,772 | 138 |
| 2008 | 2,167 | 206 |
| 2012 | 2,538 | 235 |
| 2015 | 2,808 | 249 |

This indicates increasing **professionalisation and institutional recognition** of TCM.

---

# Training and Research

## 12. Formalisation of TCM Education

Government-approved TCM education increasingly followed the organisational structure of biomedical education.

Important developments included:

- university programmes;
- standard curricula;
- professional qualifications;
- continuing education.

A major example was the five-year double-degree programme in:

**Biomedical Sciences + Chinese Medicine**

jointly organised by **Nanyang Technological University** and **Beijing University of Chinese Medicine**.

---

## 13. Government-Supported Research

The government increasingly encouraged:

**TCM practitioners + biomedical doctors + scientists**

to collaborate on clinical research.

Research grants emphasised:

- controlled studies;
- scientific methodology;
- biomedical evidence.

This reveals an important power relationship:

> TCM was supported, but the standard used to validate it was largely **biomedical science**.

---

# Charity and Community Medicine

## 14. Thong Chai Medical Institution 同济医院

Charitable medicine was crucial to the survival and development of TCM in Singapore.

**Thong Chai Medical Institution**, founded in **1867**, was the earliest major charitable institution providing:

- free Chinese medical treatment;
- free Chinese herbs;
- care for the poor.

The colonial government supported Thong Chai by granting land.

Other charitable TCM institutions also received:

- tax exemptions;
- licence-fee exemptions.

### Why the Government Supported TCM Charity

The colonial government had limited medical resources.

Allowing TCM institutions to care for local communities reduced pressure on the formal healthcare system.

This continued after independence, especially because TCM charitable clinics could:

- provide low-cost community care;
- support elderly populations;
- reduce healthcare costs.

> [!TIP]
> **Charity was not peripheral to Singapore TCM — it was one of the main reasons the system survived and expanded.**

---

# Professional Associations

## 15. Bottom-Up Modernisation

Before the government directly regulated TCM, **professional associations** played the main role in modernising the profession.

They developed systems for:

- practitioner registration;
- professional discipline;
- education;
- clinical practice;
- research.

This process is described as:

**bottom-up modernisation**

because it was initiated by practitioners and community organisations rather than by the state.

---

## 16. The Three-in-One / Four-in-One Structure

Many Singapore TCM associations developed a distinctive organisational structure.

One association could operate:

**professional association + clinic + college + research institute**

These units supported one another.

```text
Professional Association
        ↓
 ┌──────┼─────────┐
Clinic  College  Research Institute
  ↕       ↕          ↕
Clinical practice + training + research
```

### Why This Was Effective

The structure allowed:

- students to observe clinical cases;
- physicians to teach;
- patient records to support research;
- research findings to feed back into practice;
- institutions to share staff and resources.

This made the associations largely **self-sufficient**.

---

## 17. Singapore Chinese Physicians' Association (SCPA)

The **Singapore Chinese Physicians' Association**, founded in **1946**, became one of the most important professional organisations.

It initially followed regulations based on the **Republic of China Physicians Act**.

After Singapore became independent, the association adapted its own:

- registration requirements;
- practice guidelines;
- disciplinary procedures.

Membership became a sign of **professional legitimacy**.

Practitioners registered with the SCPA were expected to obey professional rules, and misconduct could result in punishment.

---

## 18. Learning from Western Professional Systems

The SCPA adopted some practices from Western medicine.

For example, it began issuing:

**medical certificates**

to its practitioners.

This demonstrates that Singapore TCM modernisation was not simply Chinese tradition being preserved.

Instead, it involved:

**selective borrowing + adaptation**

from both Chinese and Western systems.

---

# TCM Education in Singapore

## 19. From Apprenticeship to Formal Colleges

Before formal schools, TCM knowledge could be acquired through:

- apprenticeship;
- self-learning;
- family transmission.

The SCPA established a TCM training school in **1953**, partly because colonial immigration restrictions reduced the number of physicians entering Singapore.

Initially:

- teachers created their own notes;
- there was no standard syllabus;
- teaching resembled apprenticeship.

From the **1960s**, the school increasingly adopted textbooks and curricula from China.

However, these were adapted to suit the Singapore context.

---

## 20. Singapore vs China: Training Differences

The chapter highlights several differences.

| Singapore TCM Training | China TCM Training |
|---|---|
| Historically more part-time courses | More full-time education |
| Clinical exposure begins early | More standardised national system |
| More teaching hours integrating theory and practice | More formalised classroom structure |
| Less biomedical content historically | Greater biomedical integration |

The reduced biomedical component in Singapore was linked to the legal separation between **Chinese and Western medicine**.

---

## 21. China's Growing Influence After the 1980s

After China opened up in the **1980s**, Singapore TCM practitioners increasingly:

- attended postgraduate training in China;
- invited Chinese experts to Singapore;
- established exchange programmes;
- formed joint degree programmes.

From **1994**, Singapore colleges partnered with major TCM universities in China.

This helped raise the perceived academic standard of local training before government regulation became fully established.

---

# Research in Singapore TCM

## 22. Different Approaches to Research

Professional associations conducted research long before state regulation.

Methods included:

- textual analysis of classics;
- annotation;
- chemistry;
- statistics;
- systematic observation;
- logical deduction.

Singapore's wider emphasis on **science, mathematics and English-language education** also encouraged more scientific forms of TCM research.

However, because Chinese and Western medicine were legally separated, researchers could not simply integrate the two systems clinically.

As a result, local researchers often combined:

**scientific methods + traditional pattern differentiation 辨證論治**

---

# Coordination with Government

## 23. Singapore TCM Organisations Coordinating Committee

In **1995**, major TCM organisations formed the:

**Singapore Traditional Chinese Medicine Organisations Coordinating Committee**

It represented the TCM community in negotiations with the government.

The committee helped draft:

- education guidelines;
- practice guidelines;
- ethical codes;
- lists of recognised practitioners.

This was important during the transition from:

**self-regulation → government regulation**

---

# Religious Organisations and Folk Medicine

## 24. Scholarly Medicine vs Folk Medicine

Chinese medicine in Singapore has never consisted only of scholarly TCM.

Forms of **folk and religious healing** have also continued.

Before the mid-twentieth century, some physicians combined medical treatment with:

- Daoist rituals;
- religious ideas;
- spirit-based healing.

From around the **1930s**, professional physicians increasingly separated themselves from these practices, describing them as **superstition**.

This was part of the attempt to make Chinese medicine appear more:

**professional + rational + scientific**

---

## 25. Temple-Based Healing

Religious forms of healing nevertheless survived in temples.

Examples mentioned include temples where medical deities such as:

- **Hua Tuo 華佗**
- **Wu Tao / Baosheng Emperor**
- **Ji Gong 濟公**

are worshipped.

Patients may seek treatment from these **divine physicians**.

Methods include:

### Yaoqian 藥籤
Obtaining prescriptions through divination.

### Fuji 扶乩
Spirit-writing through which prescriptions may be communicated by a deity.

This demonstrates that modern Singapore still contains **multiple forms of Chinese healing**, not only officially regulated TCM.

---

## 26. Rationalisation of Religious Healing

Religious organisations adapted to Singapore's increasingly rationalised and modern social environment in two ways.

### 1. Reinterpretation

Some attempted to justify ritual practices through scholarly explanations.

### 2. Secular Medical Institutions

Some religious organisations established:

**free TCM clinics**

alongside religious activities.

This demonstrates another form of adaptation:

**religious healing → institutionalised community medicine**

---

# Two Forms of Modernisation

## 27. Bottom-Up and Top-Down Modernisation

The history of Singapore TCM can be understood through two interacting processes.

### Bottom-Up Modernisation

Driven by:

- professional associations;
- physicians;
- charities;
- religious organisations.

They created:

**training + registration + clinics + research + professional standards**

before strong government regulation existed.

### Top-Down Modernisation

Driven by:

- legislation;
- TCMPB;
- national examinations;
- approved training;
- biomedical research standards.

The state formalised and standardised TCM after **2000**.

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Framework
> **Before 2000 → professional self-regulation**
>
> **After 2000 → statutory state regulation**
>
> Modern Singapore TCM developed through the interaction of both.

---

# Why Singapore TCM Is Unique

## 28. Different from China

Singapore's Chinese medicine system differs from China's because of:

- British colonial legal structures;
- strong biomedical dominance;
- legal separation of Chinese and Western medicine;
- community-based charitable institutions;
- professional self-regulation;
- later state regulation;
- adaptation of Chinese medical education to local needs.

Unlike China, Singapore did not simply integrate TCM and Western medicine into one healthcare system.

Instead, the two remained institutionally distinct.

# Exam-Focused Summary

**Arrival:** Chinese medicine came with Chinese migrants and became important because formal healthcare was limited.

**1905 Ordinance:** TCM was legally permitted, but Western medicine remained the official medical system. Chinese and Western medicine were kept institutionally separate.

**Japanese Occupation:** Chinese physicians were registered and examined for the first time under direct government control.

**Post-war period:** Western medicine became dominant. TCM was increasingly judged according to scientific and biomedical standards.

**Berberine case (1978):** Demonstrated the government's preference for biomedical evidence when regulating TCM.

**TCM Practitioners Act 2000:** Introduced direct state regulation, the TCMPB, professional registration and modern disciplinary systems.

**Professional associations:** Before 2000, they were crucial in regulating practitioners, providing education, running clinics and conducting research.

**SCPA:** One of the major organisations responsible for professionalisation and self-regulation.

**Charity:** Institutions such as **Thong Chai Medical Institution (1867)** helped TCM survive by providing free or affordable healthcare.

**Education:** TCM training moved from apprenticeship and self-learning toward formal colleges and standardised curricula.

**Research:** Singapore combined traditional TCM theory with selected scientific methods, while maintaining legal separation from Western medicine.

**Religious medicine:** Folk and temple healing continued alongside scholarly TCM, showing that Chinese medicine in Singapore remained internally diverse.

**Main historical process:**  
**bottom-up modernisation by associations + top-down regulation by the state**

> [!SUMMARY] One-Sentence Takeaway
> **Chinese medicine in Singapore became modern through continuous localisation: community organisations professionalised it from below, while the state later regulated it from above using a framework strongly shaped by biomedical and colonial institutions.**
