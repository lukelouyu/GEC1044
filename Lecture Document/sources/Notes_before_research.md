## Lecture 7 — The Art and Science of TCM Diagnostics

Sources: [Part One, 24:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mh6CxGYLvtQ), [Part Two, 23:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZ6-nS-Tgco).

### 1. Overall purpose: disease diagnosis plus pattern differentiation

**Part One, 3:23–4:32.** TCM diagnostics studies the theories and methods used to identify disease and differentiate syndromes or patterns. The practitioner gathers observations, interprets the patient's current pattern of disharmony, identifies the disease, and combines these to plan individualized care.

The lecturer distinguishes two levels of explanation:

| Concept | Main question | Role in understanding the patient |
|---|---|---|
| Disease | What illness is occurring and how does it develop? | Cause, mechanism, course and likely outcome |
| Syndrome/pattern | What configuration of signs and bodily responses exists now? | Current nature of imbalance, affected systems and treatment priorities |

The same disease can appear with different patterns in different people. A patient's pattern can also change over the course of illness. A disease label alone therefore does not determine the TCM treatment described in this lecture.

The lecturer contrasts this approach with biomedical emphasis on pathogens, structural lesions and biological markers. Treat that contrast as an explanation of TCM's emphasis; it is not evidence that contemporary biomedicine never individualizes treatment.

### 2. The philosophical basis of diagnosis

**Part One, 4:35–8:54.** Ancient practitioners worked without modern imaging or laboratory tools. Their system linked the person to nature and treated body, mind and spirit as interconnected. External manifestations were interpreted as information about internal states.

Three ideas organize this reasoning:

1. **Infer the interior from the exterior:** observable signs are interpreted as expressions of internal change.
2. **Infer the whole from a part:** a localized observation, such as tongue appearance or pulse characteristics, is used to understand the wider condition.
3. **Know the normal to recognize change:** abnormality can only be recognized against a sufficiently familiar range of healthy variation.

The lecturer compares this general logic with biomedical observations such as cyanosis and palmar erythema. The similarity is the use of outward clues; it does not establish that every TCM sign has the same validation as those biomedical signs.

**Two practical challenges:** defining normality in a framework that includes constitution, environment and emotions; and developing the perceptual skill to detect deviations consistently. Pulse taking is used to illustrate the importance of extensive practice and observer subjectivity.

**Gardener analogy, 8:57–9:56:** yellow or wilting leaves can prompt investigation of sunlight, water, soil, roots and pests. Likewise, the TCM practitioner considers the surrounding system before interpreting an isolated symptom.

### 3. Three diagnostic principles

**Part One, 10:00–12:22.**

- **Holistic examination:** relate symptoms to disease history, mechanisms, constitution, qi and blood, yin–yang, family/social circumstances, seasons and climate.
- **Synthesize all four diagnostic methods:** collect broad information, identify the most informative findings and cross-check them against the remainder. One striking sign should not automatically override everything else.
- **Combine disease and syndrome:** disease supplies the longitudinal overview; syndrome describes the current configuration. Both inform the plan.

**Illustrative case, 12:22–13:31:** an older man with hypertension and persistent blood-pressure fluctuation was described as unusually nervous, with a TCM pattern of Liver qi stagnation and blood deficiency. The lecturer reports improvement after an individualized herbal prescription. The educational point is disease–pattern combination. This is a reported individual case, not a controlled demonstration of efficacy or a reason to alter antihypertensive treatment.

**Learning requirements, 13:34–15:30:** learn the foundational theories, obtain repeated clinical exposure, and develop logical reasoning that checks alternatives instead of relying on impressions or one-sided explanations.

### 4. The four diagnostic methods — 望闻问切

**Part One, 15:34 onward; Part Two, 0:50–13:18.**

| Method | Information sought | Examples in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection, 望 | Visible presentation | Spirit, colour, form, posture, tongue, excretions |
| Listening and smelling, 闻 | Sounds and odours | Voice, speech, breathing, cough, breath and discharge odours |
| Inquiry, 问 | Reported experience and history | Symptoms, diet, emotions, lifestyle and previous illness |
| Palpation, 切 | Findings obtained through touch | Pulse, temperature, moisture, tenderness and texture |

#### Inspection: whole-body and local information

**Part One, 16:33–23:44.** Five inspection categories are introduced: general appearance; local regions such as face, limbs and skin; tongue; excretions; and pediatric finger inspection. The detailed discussion concentrates on general appearance and tongue diagnosis.

General appearance is organized into **spirit, colour, form and posture**:

- **Spirit (shen):** alertness, eyes, expression, manner, consciousness, emotional presentation and responsiveness. Speech can supplement the visual assessment.
- **Colour and lustre:** complexion and its brightness/dryness are interpreted in relation to qi, blood and organ-system function. The lecturer also describes facial regions associated with different systems.
- **Form:** build, strength, thinness, body proportions and constitutional features. A historical classification uses five basic element types, each subdivided into five, giving 25 constitutions.
- **Posture:** sitting, lying, standing, walking, changes in movement and abnormal positioning; examples include convulsions, imbalance and impaired coordination.

**Five-colour associations as taught in the lecture:**

| Colour | Element / TCM system | Pattern associations mentioned |
|---|---|---|
| Green/blue-green | Wood / Liver | Cold, qi stagnation, blood stasis, pain, convulsions |
| Red | Fire / Heart | Heat |
| Yellow | Earth / Spleen | Spleen deficiency, dampness |
| White/pale | Metal / Lung | Qi, blood or yang deficiency; cold; blood loss |
| Black/dark | Water / Kidney | Kidney deficiency, cold, retained fluids, blood stasis, severe pain |

The lecture combines colour, facial region and five-element relationships. These associations belong to the traditional interpretive system and are not stand-alone biomedical diagnoses.

Historical constitution examples contrast a Wood-type figure with a relatively long face and straight build against an Earth-type figure with a rounder, fleshier build. The purpose is to explain constitutional differentiation, not to diagnose personality or disease reliably from appearance alone.

#### Tongue inspection

**Part Two, 0:50–2:54.** The practitioner considers tongue **colour, shape and coating**, and interprets them alongside the rest of the examination. The lecturer presents the tongue as reflecting organ systems, qi, blood and body fluids.

| Appearance described | TCM interpretation in the lecture |
|---|---|
| Normal example | Baseline comparison |
| Pale tongue with tooth marks | Qi deficiency |
| Redness | Heat |
| Swollen or “fat” tongue | Dampness |
| Purple colour or dark spots | Blood stasis |

The exercises also interpret changes in particular tongue regions. These are image-dependent: the captions alone do not preserve the full diagrams. Do not infer an organ diagnosis from a colour in isolation.

#### Listening and smelling

**Part Two, 2:54–3:56.** Listening includes voice strength, clarity and coherence; ability to respond; snoring, groaning, sighing and sneezing; breathing and cough; vomiting, hiccups, belching and intestinal sounds.

Smelling includes breath, sweat, sputum/mucus, urine, stool, menstrual or vaginal discharge, vomit and general body odour. This method contributes information that visual inspection and inquiry may miss.

#### Inquiry: the ten categories

**Part Two, 3:56–5:36.** Inquiry covers the present illness, medical history, emotions and lifestyle. The historical “ten inquiries” are presented in a later modified form:

1. Sensations of hot and cold.
2. Sweating.
3. Head and body symptoms.
4. Urination and bowel movements, including nocturia, pain and bowel habits.
5. Appetite and diet.
6. Chest and abdominal symptoms.
7. Hearing.
8. Thirst.
9. Medical history.
10. Causes or precipitating factors.

Additional questions depend on the patient: menstruation and discharge in relevant patients, and pediatric history for children. The point is a structured but adaptable interview.

#### Palpation and pulse diagnosis

**Part Two, 5:36–13:18.** Palpation includes warmth/cold, moisture/dryness, hardness/softness, pain and other abnormalities in the chest, ribs, abdomen, skin, hands, feet and acupoints.

Pulse examination assesses depth, length, frequency, rhythm, width, tension, shape, strength and smoothness. The radial artery is the main site discussed. Three positions are assessed on each wrist, from distal to proximal: **cun, guan, chi**.

| Position | Right wrist: lecture association | Left wrist: lecture association |
|---|---|---|
| Cun | Lung | Heart |
| Guan | Spleen and Stomach | Liver and Gallbladder |
| Chi | Kidney yang and lower abdomen | Kidney yin and lower abdomen |

The demonstration uses the middle finger to locate guan near the radial styloid, with the index and ring fingers at cun and chi. Assessment varies pressure from light to moderate to deep. These details explain the demonstration and do not constitute sufficient training to perform clinical pulse diagnosis.

| Pulse quality | Description | Associations taught |
|---|---|---|
| Superficial/floating | Felt readily at light pressure; less apparent with deeper pressure | Exterior patterns |
| Deep | Requires greater pressure | Interior/chronic conditions or constitutional variation |
| Rapid | Increased rate | Heat |
| Moderate/relaxed | Relatively relaxed rate and quality | Can be normal; interpreted with other findings |
| Intermittent | Pauses in the rhythm | Lecture discusses irregular versus regular pauses and possible heart/health problems |
| Excess/full | Strong across positions | Excess; sometimes a strong healthy pulse |
| Thin | Fine but palpable | Qi/blood deficiency or yin deficiency |
| Wiry | Tense, string-like quality | Stress, anger, pain, Liver/Gallbladder patterns, qi stagnation |
| Slippery | Smooth, rolling quality | Phlegm, food retention, heat or dampness; can occur in pregnancy or health |

**Avoid over-memorizing the caption's numerical pulse thresholds.** The spoken-caption conversion between beats per breath and beats per minute is approximate and unclear; the conceptual distinctions are more reliable in this source.

**Confounders explicitly mentioned:** age, sex, body size, exercise, emotional state, recent meals, athletic training and pregnancy. A deep pulse in a larger person, a slower pulse in an athlete or a slippery quality after a large meal cannot automatically be treated as disease. Findings must be combined with the other diagnostic methods.

### 5. Turning observations into patterns: the eight principles — 八纲

**Part Two, 13:22–19:17.** The lecture names several complementary systems, including eight-principle, disease-nature, organ-system and six-meridian differentiation. One additional system is unclear in the captions and is not reconstructed here.

The eight principles comprise four paired dimensions:

| Pair | Main question | Features/examples in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| Yin / yang | What is the broad nature of the pattern? | Yin: quieter, suppressed, colder/damper, darker; yang: more agitated, active, hotter, brighter/redder |
| Exterior / interior | Where or how deep is the pattern? | Exterior: surface-associated symptom complexes; interior: deeper organ/tissue involvement |
| Cold / heat | What thermal nature characterizes it? | Cold: chills, cold sensation, clear excretions; heat: thirst, flushing, restlessness, increased warmth, yellow/dry excretions |
| Deficiency / excess | What is the relationship between bodily resources and pathogenic factors? | Deficiency emphasizes weakness of healthy qi; excess emphasizes pathogenic predominance |

**Yin–yang is the overarching classification**, incorporating aspects of the other three pairs. These are interacting dimensions, not eight unrelated diagnoses.

Exterior and interior can coexist, and an illness can change from an exterior presentation to deeper involvement. The lecturer illustrates this with an acute respiratory illness and a persistent or more severe lower-respiratory presentation. This is a TCM classification example, not a universal progression of every cold.

Healthy or upright qi is used to describe defensive and regulatory capacity. Pathogenic qi refers to disease-producing influences in the TCM framework. Their changing relationship helps explain progression and recovery within that model.

### 6. Mixed patterns and the worked case

**Part Two, 19:20–21:44.** A menopause-related example illustrates simultaneous heat manifestations and deficiency: hot flushes, irritability and night sweating may be discussed alongside cold sensations or other deficiency signs. The four pairs are not mutually exclusive boxes.

In the final case, the reported findings include nervousness, a reddish tooth-marked tongue, hot flushes/night sweats and a wiry, thin pulse. The lecturer interprets these as an **interior pattern with qi and yin deficiency and heat manifestations**, with further organ-system differentiation suggested by the pulse.

The reasoning sequence matters more than memorizing a single label:

**Gather findings → cross-check the four methods → classify the pattern → combine it with disease history → individualize the plan.**

## Lecture 8 — Acupuncture: Theory & Practice

[Recording, 33:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eqV0_Lff5nc)

### 1. Acupuncture and the idea of balance

**1:02–3:27.** Acupuncture is introduced as insertion of needles at selected acupoints. The TCM explanation is that stimulation regulates qi, blood and yin–yang and supports coordination among organ systems. The guiding principle is **reduce excess and reinforce deficiency**.

The lecturer's clinical interests include pain and stroke-related problems. This provides context for the examples but does not mean every listed condition has equally strong evidence for acupuncture.

### 2. Needles, insertion and manipulation

**3:30–9:04.** The lecture contrasts historical silver needles with modern single-use steel needles. It shows disinfection, point identification, insertion and removal in a supervised demonstration.

Three insertion orientations are named:

| Orientation | Approximate angle shown | Context in the demonstration |
|---|---|---|
| Transverse/flat | 15° | Relatively superficial tissue regions |
| Oblique | 45° | Regions where angled insertion is selected |
| Perpendicular/straight | 90° | Thicker tissue regions |

These are descriptions of lecture content, not safe instructions for self-needling. Actual site, depth and angle depend on anatomy and professional training.

**Deqi** is the term for the needling sensation discussed after insertion. The caption renders it incorrectly; it is not the English word appearing in the automatic text.

Two manipulation families illustrate reinforcement and reduction:

- **Twirling:** rotating the needle; the lecturer contrasts thumb-forward and the opposite rotational emphasis.
- **Lifting–thrusting:** the reinforcing example emphasizes thrusting relative to lifting; reduction reverses that emphasis.

The conceptual lesson is that the point selected and the way it is stimulated both belong to the treatment strategy. The demonstrations should not be reconstructed as a clinical procedure from captions.

### 3. Fourteen channels in the teaching system

**9:06–11:36.** The lecture's fourteen-channel system consists of **twelve principal channels plus Ren and Du**. Yin channels are associated with the inner aspects of limbs, yang channels with outer aspects. The three yin divisions are Taiyin, Shaoyin and Jueyin; the three yang divisions are Yangming, Taiyang and Shaoyang.

The circulation sequence described is:

**Lung → Large Intestine → Stomach → Spleen → Heart → Small Intestine → Bladder → Kidney → Pericardium → Sanjiao/Triple Energizer → Gallbladder → Liver → Lung.**

| Yin channel | Paired yang channel |
|---|---|
| Hand Taiyin Lung | Hand Yangming Large Intestine |
| Foot Taiyin Spleen | Foot Yangming Stomach |
| Hand Shaoyin Heart | Hand Taiyang Small Intestine |
| Foot Shaoyin Kidney | Foot Taiyang Bladder |
| Hand Jueyin Pericardium | Hand Shaoyang Sanjiao |
| Foot Jueyin Liver | Foot Shaoyang Gallbladder |

The lecturer mentions channel-associated times of day, but the caption text does not enumerate a complete timetable. No clock table has been invented here.

- **Ren, Conception Vessel:** associated with the anterior midline and called the “sea of yin” in the lecture.
- **Du, Governing Vessel:** associated with the posterior midline and called the “sea of yang.”

### 4. Acupoints: location and special categories

**11:39–16:04.** The lecturer presents **361 classical points along the fourteen channels**. Point choice is related to the illness and TCM pattern; points may also contribute to diagnostic assessment.

Three location methods are compared:

1. **Anatomical landmarks:** palpable bones, muscles, protrusions and depressions.
2. **Proportional body measurement:** uses the patient's own body proportions and reference landmarks.
3. **Finger measurement:** convenient but less precise and unsuitable as the sole method for every body region.

The lecture selects four special-point groups from a larger classification:

| Group | Main location/organization | Function as taught |
|---|---|---|
| Yuan-source (“origin”) points | One for each principal channel; near wrists/ankles | Associated with source qi and the corresponding organ system |
| Back-shu points | Back, on the inner Bladder-channel line | Associated with particular organ systems and related disorders |
| Front-mu points | Chest/abdomen | Organ-related collecting points; complementary to back-shu |
| Xi-cleft points | Mostly distal to elbows/knees | Acute channel disorders; yin-channel examples include blood-related disorders, yang-channel examples acute pain |

The back-shu distance is a proportional traditional measure; the caption's apparent conversion into ordinary inches should not be relied upon.

### 6. Cupping

**29:51–32:26.** Cupping creates suction at the skin. The lecturer describes increased local blood flow and lists muscle relaxation, pain relief, healing, lymphatic effects and digestive effects among its intended benefits.

| Method | What distinguishes it in the lecture |
|---|---|

## Lecture 10 — Chinese Ideas of Health Preservation & Ageing

[Recording](https://mediaweb.ap.panopto.com/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=620237df-0fd0-4b8d-9264-b37800d0b846)

**Source: screenshots of all 16 sidebar entries.** These notes cover the visible concepts, historical texts and exercise list. They do not reconstruct uncaptioned narration or a complete movement demonstration. The title slide retains the older module code GEH1070.

### 1. What yangsheng means

**0:15–3:06.** **养 yang** means cultivating, preserving or nourishing; **生 sheng** refers to life/health. **养生 yangsheng** is presented as maintaining health, preventing illness and extending life.

The philosophical basis connects humans with heaven, earth and the natural environment. Qi transformation and the changing relationship of yin and yang are used to explain bodily function and change. The slide also notes that the term yangsheng is widely used and can be misused: a commercial label alone does not define the historical concept.

The health-preservation slide distinguishes:

| Dimension | Chinese term | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Cultivating the body | 养身 | Physical condition and bodily practice |
| Cultivating the mind | 养心 | Mental/emotional cultivation |
| Cultivating character | 养性 | Disposition and conduct |
| Cultivating the spirit | 养神 | Spirit/shen |

Note that 养身 and 养神 may look similar in romanization but have different characters and meanings. A second set of concepts is **精 jing (essence), 气 qi (rendered “breath” on this slide), 神 shen (spirit)**. The two lists are related teaching frameworks, not identical categories.

### 2. Why the mind is the starting point

**11:30.** The slide names **joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hate and desire** as seven emotional activities. This is the particular list used in this lecture; do not silently replace it with a different seven-emotion list from another source.

The argument has three steps:

1. Emotional activity is normal and can remain within bodily regulation.
2. Personality, prolonged emotional immersion, sudden shock or violent changes may exceed that capacity; emotions then become excessive passions and potential causes of disease within the framework.
3. TCM links those passions to zang–fu systems and relates a person's tolerance to qi, blood and constitution.

**Key distinction:** emotion itself is not automatically pathological. The lecture focuses on intensity, duration, sudden change and the individual's capacity to regulate and tolerate it.

### 3. Ji Kang and aspirations for longevity

**20:03.** Ji Kang, 嵇康 (220–265 CE), illustrates historical beliefs in the possibility of cultivating exceptional longevity or immortality. The slide associates failure to attain it with inadequate guiding of qi and cultivation of life, 导气养生.

These statements are **historical beliefs being taught**, not evidence that immortality or a lifespan of 120 years can be reliably produced through a practice. Their significance is the older view that lifespan and health could be influenced through disciplined cultivation.

### 4. Daoyin: guiding qi and stretching the body

**22:06.** **导引 daoyin** is explained as guiding qi (导) and stretching the body (引), and as a forerunner of qigong. It brings together:

- Mental awareness.
- Controlled breathing.
- Slow physical movement.

The practice belongs to a worldview involving qi, dao, yin–yang and the five elements. The slide connects bodily development, health and spiritual attainment; it is broader than exercise understood only as muscle work.

**25:30–28:51. Mawangdui and the Daoyin Tu.** The archaeological slides show a tomb/coffin and a chart of people in varied postures. They associate the finds with Mawangdui in Changsha, Hunan, and a Han-dynasty tomb dated 168 BCE. The chart is presented as evidence that breathing and bodily cultivation practices have a long history.

**What the evidence supports:** the existence and historical form of such practices. The age of an illustration does not itself prove the effectiveness of every exercise or later therapeutic claim. The chart has many postures, but the slides do not supply a safe, complete modern instruction sequence for each one.

### 5. Ageing as a problem of maintaining balance

**29:54.** The slide defines good health through holistic equilibrium and emphasizes prevention. It distinguishes **harmony within the individual** from **harmony between the individual and the outside world**.

Examples of cultivation include observing seasonal change, adjusting yin–yang, attending to diet, massage, exercise, adequate rest and a disciplined lifestyle. These are combined as a way of maintaining function rather than presented as one isolated remedy for ageing.

The conclusion later acknowledges deterioration with age. Read the lecture as a discussion of preserving function and balance within the ageing process, while recognizing that some historical sources express much stronger longevity aspirations.

Translated on the slide as **Heart Mirror for Preserving Life**, this work illustrates exercises associated with restoration of health and management of particular complaints.

## Lecture 11 — TCM outside China: History and Development in Hong Kong

[Recording](https://mediaweb.ap.panopto.com/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=38e1dfaf-434f-44eb-9c5b-b083009f0bf4)

### 1. Main argument

Chinese medicine's position in Hong Kong is presented as a result of **historical events, public trust, government policy, professional training, research and healthcare organization**. It is not a simple continuous rise or decline. Infectious-disease crises appear at several turning points: the plague reduced confidence in the older service model; SARS stimulated collaboration; post-COVID problems created further research questions.

### 2. From community practice to marginalization

**2:04–8:41.** Chinese medicine initially spread through community practice, herbal businesses and charitable institutions. The lecture discusses early commercial branches and herbal shops, followed by the establishment of a Chinese-medicine hospital service for the Chinese community.

The **1894 plague** is presented as a turning point. The lecturer argues that inadequate results against plague weakened trust in Chinese medicine and pushed patients toward Western medicine. Western medical education, hospitals and registered practitioners subsequently expanded.

### 5. Integrative medicine: three collaboration models

**20:50–28:29.** Early research examples include acupuncture studies and later professional organizations, international conferences and networks. SARS is again presented as a practical catalyst for joint care.

| Model | How collaboration works | Main organizational requirement |

**Key distinction:** having both systems available is not the same as integrating their decisions. Integration requires agreement about who treats what, when referral is needed, how information is shared and how risks are handled.

## Self-test with brief answer guide

1. **Why combine disease and syndrome?** Disease supplies course/causes; syndrome captures the present pattern, allowing the plan to reflect the individual and the stage of illness.
2. **Why combine all four diagnostic methods?** Findings can be ambiguous, variable or observer-dependent; cross-checking provides a fuller basis for interpretation.
3. **Can heat and deficiency coexist?** Yes. The lecture's yin-deficiency example includes heat manifestations despite underlying deficiency.
4. **What are the eight principles?** Yin/yang, exterior/interior, cold/heat, deficiency/excess.
5. **How many channels are in Lecture 8's main teaching scheme?** Fourteen: twelve principal channels plus Ren and Du.
6. **How do back-shu and front-mu points differ?** Posterior versus anterior organ-associated point groups in the lecture's framework.
7. **What distinguishes wet from dry cupping?** Deliberate skin puncture/blood removal in wet cupping.
8. **What distinguishes referral from joint case management?** Referral transfers or requests care; joint case management coordinates decisions for the same patient across disciplines.
9. **Does ICD inclusion validate a treatment?** No. Classification and effectiveness are separate questions.
10. **Does an uncontrolled improvement establish treatment causation?** No. Natural recovery, concurrent care and other biases must be considered.
