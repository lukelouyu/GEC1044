## Lecture 9 — Food Therapy: Living in Harmony with Nature

### 1. Two organizing ideas

**2:12–3:24.** The lecture introduces **药食同源**, the shared origin of food and medicine, and **living in harmony with nature**. Food is treated as potentially relevant both to nourishment and to restoring balance, with its role depending on the person's condition.

The slide attributes a food/medicine distinction to the *Inner Canon of the Yellow Emperor*: the same act of eating is discussed differently for hunger and illness. Record this as the slide's historical attribution, not a separately authenticated quotation.

**4:42–5:42.** Hippocrates and Galen provide Western historical comparisons. The Hippocrates slide stresses natural explanations of illness and connects diet with medicine. The Galen slide introduces the qualities hot, cold, wet and dry and the Greek elements. The useful comparison is that qualitative ideas about food, bodies and balance existed in more than one medical tradition; their categories should not be assumed identical. The quotation attributed to Hippocrates on the slide has not been independently authenticated.

### 2. Three ways foods are classified

**7:03.** The Chinese-food-therapy slide describes three properties:

- **Nature:** thermal qualities such as hot, cold or warm.
- **Taste:** sweet, sour, bitter, salty and spicy/pungent.
- **Colour:** white, black, green, red and yellow.

These are traditional interpretive categories. “Hot” and “cold” in this framework are not simply the food's serving temperature, and colour–organ relationships are not equivalent to a modern nutrient analysis.

### 3. Constitution and dietary matching

**12:51.** The table contrasts two broad presentations. It is a teaching simplification, not a complete diagnostic system.

| Presentation in the slide | Signs listed | Foods offered as examples | Foods the slide advises limiting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot / excessive yang | Red complexion, sweating, agitation, dry mouth, thirst, dark urine, constipation | Starfruit, apple, cucumber, beet, watermelon, barley, eggplant, radish | Hot/spicy/yang-classified foods |
| Cold / excessive yin | Pallor, coldness, low energy, fatigue, loose stool, much sleep, weak voice, poor appetite, clear urine, edema | Garlic, ginger, spring onion, black beans, sesame, mutton, coconut, coriander, cinnamon | Excess cold-classified foods/drinks |

**Reasoning pattern:** identify the presentation → consider the food's traditional qualities → select a combination intended to counterbalance it. The slide's broad “yin” and “yang” labels should not be conflated with every deficiency/excess distinction in Lecture 7. The same symptom can occur in different conditions; the table is not sufficient for self-diagnosis.

### 4. Colour, element and organ correspondences

| Element | Colour | Taste | TCM organ pair | Food examples on colour slide |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | Green | Sour | Liver / Gallbladder | Leafy greens, cucumber, green pepper, peas |
| Fire | Red | Bitter | Heart / Small Intestine | Beet, tomato, red meat, kidney beans |
| Earth | Yellow/orange | Sweet | Spleen / Stomach | Squash, pumpkin, yam, lemon, banana |
| Metal | White | Spicy/pungent | Lung / Large Intestine | Radish, pear, white mushrooms, white fish, rice |

Memorize this as the lecture's correspondence system. A food can appear in several classifications: for example, its colour category does not eliminate its taste or thermal nature. The slides do not provide a complete rule for resolving every overlapping classification.

### 5. Five tastes and their attributed actions

**22:51.** The following condenses the effects table. These are traditional effects claimed in the lecture, not independent efficacy findings.

| Taste | Main traditional action described | Examples from slide |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet | Nourishment/moistening and moderation of acute reactions | Honey, potato, pumpkin, rice, soybean, corn, chestnut, dates, peanut |
| Sour | Astringency: restraining excessive losses of fluid or other substances | Lemon, pineapple, orange, plum, mango, grapes, vinegar |
| Bitter | Clearing heat, drying dampness and promoting downward/eliminating functions | Bitter gourd, mugwort, ginkgo, lotus leaf, asparagus, gentian |
| Salty | Softening hard accumulations and supporting bowel movement | Seaweed, shellfish, sea cucumber and other examples |
| Spicy/pungent | Dispersing/moving and stimulating appetite | Ginger, onion, garlic, chili, cinnamon, mustard |

**Exam distinction:** the taste category is associated with a proposed action, not merely a sensory label. The table is not advice to increase salt or use medicinal plants freely.

### 6. Harmony and examples of food–medicine overlap

**28:51.** *Harmony Rules: The Chinese Way of Health Through Food* by Gary Butt and Frena Bloomfield is used as an example of adapting Chinese dietary ideas for an American audience. Its framework employs balance between hot/cold, wet/dry and yin/yang, with recipes and diet plans. The broader lesson is the translation of a traditional framework across cultures, rather than evidence for a universal fixed diet.

**31:12. Oldenlandia, 白花蛇舌草.** The slide describes a cooling, diuretic herb and connects it with “heaty” complaints such as sore throat, headache, pimples and dry stool. It also mentions fever and cancer in describing traditional uses. These are claims in the source, not proof of cancer-treatment benefit. The “toxins” terminology belongs to the traditional explanation and is not a measured toxicology result.

**33:42. Su Shi and *Su Shen Liangfang*, 苏沈良方.** A historical passage praises cang'er/cocklebur for cheapness, availability and longevity-related benefits. Its role in the lecture is to illustrate historical food–medicine thinking and beliefs about accessible remedies. **Historical claims of edibility and lack of toxicity are not a present-day safety determination; this is not a recommendation to consume cocklebur.**

### 7. Yaoshan and two recipe examples

**38:51.** *Pursuing the Elixir of Life: Chinese Medicine for Health* by Hong Hai and Karen Wee introduces **药膳 yaoshan**, food prepared with medicinal ingredients. The slide emphasizes both palatability and the person's current syndrome/pattern. Familiar meal ingredients can also appear in prescriptions, but formulation and patient context matter.

**41:21. Complexion-enhancing tea, 贵妃养颜茶.** The example contains rose, jasmine and red dates, steeped in hot water. The slide explains the proposed cosmetic benefit through movement of qi and blood and nourishment/moistening of the skin. This illustrates the TCM explanatory chain linking an internal functional state to external appearance. The benefit is a slide claim rather than a demonstrated outcome in this lecture.

**44:57. Job's tears and green-bean soup, 薏仁绿豆汤.** Ingredients shown are yi yi ren/Job's tears, green beans and dried tangerine peel. The preparation shown involves soaking, then simmering; exact recipe quantities are retained in the screenshot rather than presented as a treatment prescription here.

The formulation explanation is more important than the cooking steps:

- The soup is intended for a **heat–dampness** presentation.
- Job's tears is the main ingredient, with the largest amount and the strongest clearing-dampness role in the slide's explanation.
- Dried tangerine peel is used in a smaller amount as an adjunct to move qi and help the Spleen/Stomach tolerate the cooler ingredients.
- The slide explicitly says the soup is unsuitable for someone with a weak Spleen/Stomach presentation.

This is a useful counterexample to “natural food is always appropriate for everyone”: within the lecture's own model, suitability depends on the person's state.

### 8. Four roles within an herbal formula — 君臣佐使

**54:45.** The lecture uses a political hierarchy as an analogy for complementary functions:

| Role | Term on slide | Function in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| Chief/sovereign | 君, imperial herb | Main ingredient targeting the primary problem |
| Minister | 臣, ministerial herb | Supports the principal action |
| Assistant | 佐 | Reduces unwanted effects within the combination |
| Envoy/helper | 使, servant herb | Helps the formulation work as a coordinated whole |

The visible slide gives a brief version of these roles; more elaborate functions from other textbooks have not been added. Together with the soup example, it explains why a formula is considered a designed combination rather than an arbitrary mixture.

### 9. Conclusion and historical spread

**59:21.** The concluding slide states that food-therapy ideas were **not identical or completely systematic across all periods and places**. It nevertheless identifies shared themes: food and medicine overlap; five tastes are linked to particular organ functions; and food may be used with preventive or therapeutic intent.

The slide also traces Chinese dietary-medical influence in East Asia, mentioning Japanese works from the tenth century and Korean literature into the Joseon period. The emphasis is historical transmission and adaptation, not an unchanging single tradition.

### Additional research — diet evidence and the food–medicine boundary

**Diet can affect disease outcomes, but the tested pattern matters.** The republished PREDIMED analysis included 7,447 adults at high cardiovascular risk. Mediterranean-diet groups supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts had fewer major cardiovascular events than the comparison diet. The republication addressed allocation irregularities using revised analyses. This is evidence about a specified dietary intervention and population; it does **not** test the five-taste/organ correspondence table. [R9: Estruch et al., NEJM, 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)

**A modern comparison with “harmony.”** WHO describes healthy diets through adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity. Its guidance emphasizes whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses; for people older than ten it specifies at least 400 g of fruit/vegetables daily, and for adults less than 5 g of salt daily. This makes an important distinction for the lecture: a traditional “salty” category is not a recommendation for high sodium intake. A range of colourful foods can support variety without establishing that each colour selectively nourishes one anatomical organ. [R10: WHO healthy-diet guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)

**Oldenlandia: what the cancer evidence actually supports.** Memorial Sloan Kettering's specialist monograph distinguishes laboratory/animal anticancer findings from a lack of human evidence sufficient to confirm safe and effective use. It also notes product-quality and substitution concerns. The lecture example illustrates traditional use and a research hypothesis, not an established cancer treatment. [R11: MSK clinical herb monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/oldenlandia-diffusa)

**Cocklebur: historical praise does not establish safety.** Hong Kong Hospital Authority identifies the whole plant, particularly the fruit, as poisonous. This directly qualifies the historical passage on Su Shi's slide describing edibility and lack of poisoning. Preserve the passage as a record of historical belief, not as contemporary food advice. [R12: Hospital Authority clinical-toxicology atlas](https://www3.ha.org.hk/toxicplant/en/xanthium_sibiricum.html)

**How to evaluate a medicinal-food claim:** specify botanical identity, plant part, preparation, amount, duration, patient population and outcomes. “Used traditionally,” “active in cells,” and “improves outcomes in patients” are different evidence levels. This is a methodological synthesis of the sources above. A formula role such as chief or assistant explains intended design; it does not replace testing of the final combination.

### Studocu cross-check — PDF pp. 19–22

Food/medicine overlap, nature/taste/colour, constitution examples and formula roles are already covered. **Added explicit seasonal pairing from p. 22:** nourish yang in spring/summer and yin in autumn/winter, within the traditional cycle of activity and conservation. This is a cultivation principle, not evidence that season alone determines an individual's diet. Singapore's climate also requires locality rather than mechanical transfer of temperate seasons.

The PDF's simple “yang”/“yin” hot/cold labels should not erase distinctions among excess heat, yang deficiency and yin-deficiency heat. Its food-colour chart expresses traditional correspondences, not proof of organ-specific nutritional effects.
