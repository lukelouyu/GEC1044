## Developments in Chinese Medicine from the Song through the Qing

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Argument
> From the **Song dynasty onward**, Chinese medicine became increasingly **clinical, systematic, scholarly, and individualised**. Earlier classics such as the [[Huangdi Neijing 黃帝內經]] supplied broad theoretical principles, while later physicians interpreted these principles into concrete diagnostic systems and treatment strategies.
>
> The history of Chinese medicine is therefore not simply the preservation of ancient knowledge. It is a continuing process of **reinterpreting the classics to solve new medical problems**.

---

## 1. Why the Song Dynasty Was a Turning Point

Before the Song, texts such as the [[Huangdi Neijing 黃帝內經]] mainly supplied broad concepts concerning the body, qi, organs, channels, Yin-Yang and cosmology. From the **Song dynasty (960–1279)** onward, medical literature increasingly explained **how these theories should actually be used in clinical practice**.

The Song is therefore a crucial period because many features now associated with Chinese medicine reached a mature form:

- authoritative versions of classical texts were printed;
- pharmacology became more systematic;
- drug therapy became increasingly prestigious;
- government became involved in healthcare;
- medicine became an acceptable scholarly profession;
- [[Shanghan Lun 傷寒論]] became a major clinical authority.

> [!TIP] Key Historical Shift
> **Earlier classics → theoretical foundations**
>
> **Song onward → interpretation + practical clinical application**

---

## 2. Government and the Medical System in the Song

The Song government became unusually involved in medicine, partly because of repeated **epidemics** and partly because emperors themselves were interested in medical knowledge.

The state published official formularies such as the **Taiping Huimin Hejiju Fang 太平惠民和劑局方**, which contained standard prescriptions for particular illnesses. These prescriptions made treatments accessible beyond specialist physicians.

The government also established the **Bureau for Revising Medical Texts 校正醫書局**, which corrected, edited and standardised important medical writings. Government involvement gradually expanded into:

**text standardisation → practitioner credentialling → public dispensaries → wider access to medicine**

The first government-run dispensary mentioned by Chace was established in **1076**.

### Significance

Medicine was increasingly treated as a matter of **public governance**, rather than simply an activity carried out privately by individual healers.

This also strengthened the authority of particular medical texts because the state determined which versions were considered authoritative.

---

## 3. Revival of the Shanghan Lun 傷寒論

One of the most important Song developments was the revival of Zhang Ji's [[Shanghan Lun 傷寒論|Discussion of Cold Damage]].

Although written around **220 CE**, much of its later clinical importance came from interpretations developed during the Song and subsequent periods.

Song scholars:

- edited authoritative versions of the text;
- organised its theories;
- expanded its clinical applications;
- connected Cold Damage theory with existing theories of organs and channels.

Important Song commentators included:

### Pang Anshi 龐安時
**General Discussion of the Disease of Cold Damage 傷寒總病論**

Helped systematise Cold Damage theory.

### Zhu Gong 朱肱
**Lifesaving Book that Categorises the Presentations of Cold Damage 傷寒類證活人書**

Organised Cold Damage presentations into a form that was easier for practitioners and laypeople to understand.

### Cheng Wuji 成無己
**Annotation and Explanation of the Discussion of Cold Damage 注解傷寒論**

Connected Cold Damage disease progression with classical theories of:

**channels 經絡 ↔ viscera 臟腑 ↔ disease progression**

His interpretation became highly influential and contributed to later medical orthodoxy.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Chace argues that although the **Shanghan Lun originated in the Han period**, its clinical meaning today was largely shaped by **Song and later physicians**.

---

## 4. Rise of the Scholar-Physician 儒醫

During the Song, medicine increasingly attracted members of the educated elite.

The idea emerged that if a scholar could not serve society as a good government minister, becoming a **good physician** was another respectable way to contribute.

By the Southern Song, the ideal of the **ruyi 儒醫 — scholar-physician** became increasingly important.

A good physician was expected to possess:

**medical knowledge + intellectual cultivation + moral cultivation**

This development was strongly connected to [[Neo-Confucianism]].

Neo-Confucian scholars emphasised **investigation of things 格物**, meaning that knowledge could be developed through careful investigation of the principles underlying the world.

Medicine therefore became an appropriate subject for scholarly investigation.

### Printing and Medical Knowledge

The expansion of printing greatly increased intellectual exchange.

Medical knowledge could now circulate:

**physician ↔ physician**

and

**physician ↔ scholar**

This helped transform medicine into a more explicitly **text-based scholarly discipline**.

---

# Jin–Yuan Medicine

## 5. From Disease 病 to Pattern 證

During the **Jin and Yuan dynasties (1115–1368)**, scholar-physicians increasingly criticised the use of fixed prescriptions for named diseases.

Instead of simply asking:

> “What disease does this patient have?”

physicians increasingly asked:

> “What pathological process is occurring in this particular patient?”

The diagnostic focus therefore shifted from:

**disease 病 (bing)**

toward

**pattern / presentation 證 (zheng)**

A **zheng 證** represents the particular way in which the patient's qi transformation has become disturbed.

Treatment therefore needed to consider:

- the patient's constitution;
- environmental conditions;
- location of disease;
- hot/cold characteristics;
- excess/deficiency;
- internal/external factors.

> [!IMPORTANT] Major Change
> **Fixed disease → fixed formula**
>
> became increasingly replaced by
>
> **individual pattern → individualised treatment**

This principle became one of the most important foundations of later Chinese medicine.

---

## 6. Systematisation of Diagnosis

### Zhang Yuansu 張元素

Zhang Yuansu helped organise diagnostic theory by relating:

**zangfu 臟腑 + jingluo 經絡 + Six Qi**

He also distinguished between:

### Root Disease 本病
The underlying pathological mechanism responsible for the disorder.

### Branch Disease 標病
The symptoms or outward manifestations produced by the underlying problem.

This distinction became the familiar concept:

**治病求本 — treatment should seek the root of disease.**

---

## 7. Eight-Principle Differentiation 八綱

Zhang Yuansu also helped systematise what later became known as the **Eight Principles 八綱**.

A disease presentation could be analysed through four paired dimensions:

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Yin 陰 / Yang 陽** | Overall character of the disorder |
| **Interior 裏 / Exterior 表** | Location of pathology |
| **Cold 寒 / Heat 熱** | Thermal character |
| **Deficiency 虛 / Excess 實** | Relative strength of body and pathogen |

These categories allowed physicians to describe the patient's **current pathological state**, rather than simply attaching a disease name.

---

# Four Great Masters of the Jin–Yuan 金元四大家

## 8. Liu Wansu 劉完素 — Fire

Liu Wansu argued that pathogenic factors ultimately tended to transform into **fire 熱/火**.

Therefore, many illnesses should be treated using **cooling and cold medicinals**.

A characteristic strategy was:

**drain Heart fire + nourish Kidney water**

He also emphasised that treatment must account for:

**climate + environment + individual constitution**

This concern with the patient-environment relationship became characteristic of Jin-Yuan medicine.

### Remember

**Liu Wansu → FIRE → COOL IT**

---

## 9. Zhang Congzheng 張從正 — Attack the Pathogen

Zhang Congzheng emphasised **external pathogenic factors** and improper food intake.

Instead of strengthening the patient immediately, his main strategy was to **remove pathogens**.

His major methods were:

1. **Sweating 汗**
2. **Vomiting 吐**
3. **Purging 下**

He expanded these techniques beyond herbal medicine to include:

- acupuncture;
- moxibustion;
- massage;
- exercise.

Zhang was particularly suspicious of excessive use of tonifying medicines. When supplementation was necessary, he often preferred **dietary therapy**.

### Remember

**Zhang Congzheng → REMOVE THE PATHOGEN**

---

## 10. Li Gao 李杲 — Spleen and Stomach

Li Gao placed the **spleen and stomach 脾胃** at the centre of his medical system.

Based on the [[Huangdi Neijing 黃帝內經]], he believed the spleen and stomach were fundamental to the production of the body's qi.

They could become damaged by:

- improper diet;
- overexertion;
- emotional disturbance.

His major work was:

**Piwei Lun 脾胃論 — Discussion of the Spleen and Stomach**

Where Liu Wansu and Zhang Congzheng often focused on **eliminating pathogenic excess**, Li Gao increasingly focused on **supporting physiological deficiency**.

His approach became extremely influential in later Chinese medicine.

### Political Metaphor

Later writers compared the body with government:

**weak central government ≈ weak spleen/stomach**

Therefore:

**strengthen the centre → restore order**

### Remember

**Li Gao → SPLEEN & STOMACH → SUPPORT THE CENTRE**

---

## 11. Zhu Zhenheng 朱震亨 — Ministerial Fire and Yin

Zhu Zhenheng is often regarded as the culmination of Jin-Yuan medical thought.

He was strongly influenced by Neo-Confucian ideas of:

**格物致知 — investigating things and extending knowledge**

Zhu argued that **ministerial fire 相火** was necessary for physiological activity but could become pathological.

Excessive:

- desire;
- emotional activity;
- especially sexual desire

could cause ministerial fire to flare and damage:

**Yin + Blood**

The major therapeutic response was therefore:

**nourish Kidney Yin → control pathological fire**

This also follows Five-Phase logic:

**Water controls Fire**

However, Zhu did not ignore digestion. Supporting the **spleen and stomach** remained an important part of nourishing Yin.

Important works:

- **Danxi Xinfa 丹溪心法 — Danxi's Essential Methods**
- **Gezhi Yulun 格致餘論 — Extra Treatises Based on Investigation and Inquiry**

### Remember

**Zhu Zhenheng → MINISTERIAL FIRE → NOURISH YIN**

---

## 12. Comparing the Four Masters

| Physician | Main Problem | Main Treatment Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Liu Wansu 劉完素** | Pathogens become **fire** | Cool heat, nourish water |
| **Zhang Congzheng 張從正** | Accumulated pathogenic factors | Remove through sweating, vomiting, purging |
| **Li Gao 李杲** | Weak **spleen/stomach** | Strengthen digestive centre |
| **Zhu Zhenheng 朱震亨** | Ministerial fire damages Yin | Nourish Yin and control fire |

> [!TIP] Easy Memory
> **Liu = Fire**
>
> **Zhang = Attack**
>
> **Li = Spleen/Stomach**
>
> **Zhu = Yin**

# Ming Dynasty

## 13. Consolidation of Medical Knowledge

By the **Ming dynasty (1368–1644)**, the image of the respected physician was strongly associated with the scholar-physician tradition.

Medicine had also become a possible means of **social advancement**, although the profession itself remained poorly regulated.

Two especially important physicians were:

- [[Li Shizhen 李時珍]]
- [[Zhang Jiebin 張介賓]]

---

## 14. Li Shizhen 李時珍

Li Shizhen's major achievement was the **systematisation and consolidation of existing medical knowledge**.

### Bencao Gangmu 本草綱目
**Comprehensive Materia Medica, 1578**

This massive work expanded materia medica beyond a simple list of drugs into an encyclopaedic compilation covering:

- medicine;
- pharmacology;
- plants;
- animals;
- minerals;
- other natural substances.

Li organised the material into increasingly systematic categories.

### Binhu Maixue 瀕湖脈學
**Pulse Studies of the Lakeside Master**

Organised pulse diagnosis into **27 pulse types**, forming an important basis for later pulse diagnosis.

### Qijing Bamai Kao 奇經八脈考
**Exposition on the Eight Extraordinary Vessels**

Systematised earlier knowledge concerning the [[Eight Extraordinary Vessels 奇經八脈]] and connected them more closely with herbal medicine.

It also incorporated ideas from **Daoist internal cultivation / inner alchemy 內丹**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Li Shizhen represents the Ming tendency toward **collecting, organising and systematising large bodies of medical knowledge**.

---

## 15. Zhang Jiebin 張介賓 — Life Gate 命門

Zhang Jiebin challenged the idea that excessive Yang was normally the main cause of disease.

For Zhang:

**Yang = Qi**

**Yin = Form**

He therefore argued that the deeper problem was usually damage to **True Yin 真陰**.

Zhang became particularly associated with the theory of the:

**Life Gate 命門**

located conceptually between the kidneys and representing primordial:

**Yin + Yang**

Although primordial essence was inherited before birth, Zhang argued that it could be supported using **postnatal essence 後天精**, produced through food and digestion.

This connects:

**Life Gate → True Yin → Essence/Blood → Spleen & Stomach**

His medicine therefore synthesised several earlier currents, particularly:

**Li Gao's spleen/stomach theory + Zhu Zhenheng's Yin-nourishing approach**

---

# Qing Dynasty

## 16. Evidential Scholarship 考證

The Qing dynasty developed a major intellectual movement known as:

**kaozheng 考證 — evidential scholarship**

Its scholars argued that earlier classical texts were more reliable than later interpretations that had been influenced by Neo-Confucian, Buddhist and Daoist ideas.

The approach therefore emphasised:

**return to original texts + careful textual investigation + observation**

Western scientific knowledge entering China may also have encouraged this stronger emphasis on empirical investigation.

However, Qing physicians did not simply return to ancient texts. They also became increasingly interested in **local clinical experience**.

---

## 17. Local Medical Traditions

Physicians increasingly recognised that disease could vary according to:

- environment;
- climate;
- geography;
- local population;
- constitution.

A particularly important medical community developed in **Jiangnan**, especially around Suzhou.

Some physicians argued that southern populations possessed a relatively more delicate constitution than northern populations because of differences in **local qi 土氣**.

This reinforced the idea that medicine must account for:

**person + environment + locality**

---

# Warm Disease 溫病

## 18. From Cold Damage to Warm Disease

One of the most important developments of late imperial medicine was **Warm Disease theory 溫病學**.

Warm Disease should not simply be understood as the rejection of [[Shanghan Lun 傷寒論]].

Instead:

**Cold Damage theory → reinterpretation → Warm Disease theory**

Earlier thinkers such as Liu Wansu had already emphasised the importance of heat.

### Wu Youxing 吳有性

Wu Youxing argued that epidemic febrile illnesses could result from:

**pestilential qi 癘氣**

which was hot in nature.

This became an important conceptual foundation for Warm Disease medicine.

---

## 19. Ye Gui 葉桂 — Flexible Treatment

Ye Gui was among the most influential physicians of the Qing period.

Rather than relying on fixed prescriptions, Ye emphasised:

**treatment strategies 法**

which could be adjusted according to the changing condition of each patient.

The physician therefore needed to understand many theories and then determine which strategy best fitted the **specific clinical presentation**.

### Four-Level Model 四分

Ye organised the progression of pathogenic heat through four increasingly deep levels:

**Defensive 衛 → Qi 氣 → Nutritive 營 → Blood 血**

```text
Surface
  ↓
衛 Defensive
  ↓
氣 Qi
  ↓
營 Nutritive
  ↓
血 Blood
Deeper disease
```

The deeper the pathogen penetrates, the more serious the illness becomes.

His major Warm Disease text was:

**Wenre Lun 溫熱論 — Discourse on Warm Disease**

---

## 20. Xue Xue 薛雪 — Three Burners 三焦

Xue Xue developed another framework for Warm Disease based on the:

**Three Burners 三焦**

| Burner | Approximate Location |
|---|---|
| **Upper Burner 上焦** | Thorax |
| **Middle Burner 中焦** | Epigastric/digestive region |
| **Lower Burner 下焦** | Lower abdomen |

His approach was especially suited for analysing **Damp-Heat 濕熱**.

Treatment depended on identifying:

**where the pathogen is located + whether dampness or heat predominates**

The spleen and stomach were particularly important because Xue regarded them as vulnerable to Damp-Heat.

---

## 21. Wu Tang 吳瑭 — Synthesis of Warm Disease

Wu Tang brought previous Warm Disease theories together.

He combined:

**Ye Gui's Four Levels**

with

**Xue Xue's Three Burners**

and connected these models with the **zangfu organs**.

This created a detailed framework for determining:

1. **where the pathogen is located**
2. **how deeply it has entered**
3. **which organs are affected**

His major text was:

**Wenbing Tiaobian 溫病條辨 — Systematic Differentiation of Warm Disease**

---

# Overall Historical Development

The period from the **Song to Qing** can be understood as a gradual development from textual preservation toward increasingly sophisticated clinical interpretation.

```text
Classical foundations
Huangdi Neijing / Shanghan Lun
        ↓
SONG
Printing + state medicine + Cold Damage revival
        ↓
Scholar-Physicians 儒醫
        ↓
JIN–YUAN
Pattern differentiation + individualisation
        ↓
Four Great Masters
Fire / Pathogen / Spleen-Stomach / Yin
        ↓
MING
Consolidation and systematisation
Li Shizhen + Zhang Jiebin
        ↓
QING
Classical re-examination + local experience
        ↓
Warm Disease theory
Ye Gui → Xue Xue → Wu Tang
```

---

## The Bigger Intellectual Pattern

Chace identifies a recurring tension in Chinese medical history.

### Expansive Interpretation

Physicians such as the **Four Great Masters** creatively developed new theories from the classics.

### Conservative Interpretation

Qing evidential scholars attempted to recover what they regarded as the authentic meaning of classical texts.

Yet these approaches were not simply opposites.

Both repeatedly returned to the **canonical texts** in response to new medical problems.

> [!IMPORTANT] Main Conclusion
> Chinese medical history should not be understood as the passive preservation of an ancient tradition.
>
> It developed through a continuous cycle:
>
> **Classics → interpretation → clinical problem → innovation → return to classics**
>
> Physicians repeatedly reinterpreted ancient knowledge to produce **effective treatments for changing diseases, environments and patients**.

---

# Exam-Focused Summary

**Song:** Chinese medicine becomes more institutionalised, scholarly and clinically detailed. Government prints medical texts, promotes formularies and revives the [[Shanghan Lun 傷寒論]]. The **scholar-physician 儒醫** emerges.

**Jin–Yuan:** Focus shifts from simply diagnosing **disease 病** toward identifying individual **patterns 證** based on qi transformation, constitution and environment. [[Eight Principles 八綱]] and individualised treatment become increasingly important.

**Four Great Masters:**  
**Liu Wansu → Fire**  
**Zhang Congzheng → Expel pathogens**  
**Li Gao → Spleen/Stomach**  
**Zhu Zhenheng → Ministerial Fire + nourish Yin**

**Ming:** Medical knowledge is increasingly consolidated and systematised. [[Li Shizhen 李時珍]] organises materia medica, pulse diagnosis and Extraordinary Vessels. [[Zhang Jiebin 張介賓]] develops Life Gate and True Yin theory.

**Qing:** Evidential scholarship encourages a return to classical sources, while clinical medicine becomes increasingly sensitive to **locality and individual experience**.

**Warm Disease:** Develops from, rather than simply rejects, Cold Damage medicine.  
**Ye Gui → Four Levels**  
**Xue Xue → Three Burners**  
**Wu Tang → combines both systems**

> [!SUMMARY] One-Sentence Takeaway
> **From the Song to Qing, Chinese medicine developed by repeatedly reinterpreting classical theory into increasingly systematic, flexible and patient-specific clinical practices.**
