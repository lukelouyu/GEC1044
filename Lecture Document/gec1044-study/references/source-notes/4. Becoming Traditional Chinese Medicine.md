
## 1. Chinese Medicine Meets Europe

Chinese medicine did not first encounter Western medicine only after the Opium Wars. Medical knowledge was already moving between China and Europe from the **late sixteenth century**, with much greater exchange during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Catholic missionaries, merchants and travellers brought descriptions of Chinese medical practices back to Europe. At the same time, Jesuit missionaries introduced European mathematics, astronomy, anatomy and medicine into China.

The exchange therefore worked in **both directions**:

```text
China
→ pulse diagnosis, materia medica, medical practices
→ Europe

Europe
→ anatomy, mathematics, astronomy, medical knowledge
→ China
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The history of Chinese and Western medicine is not simply a story of two isolated systems suddenly colliding in the nineteenth century.
>
> **Medical exchange had already been occurring for centuries.**

---

## 2. Jesuit Science and the Ming-Qing Court

Jesuit missionaries used **science as a way to gain access to Chinese elites and the imperial court**.

The Italian Jesuit **Matteo Ricci 利瑪竇** worked closely with the Chinese scholar-official **Xu Guangqi 徐光啟**, who was interested in mathematics, astronomy and scientific knowledge.

Together, Jesuits and Chinese scholars translated European scientific works into Chinese. This contributed to an early wave of scientific exchange between China and Europe.

Under the Qing, Jesuits continued serving at the imperial court because of their expertise in:

- Astronomy
- Cartography
- Mathematics
- Medicine
- European science

Western medicine was therefore present in China well before the nineteenth-century expansion of colonial power.

---

## 3. European Fascination with Chinese Pulse Diagnosis

One feature of Chinese medicine that particularly fascinated Europeans was **pulse diagnosis**.

Early European travellers observed that Chinese physicians placed great importance on feeling the pulse and classified many different pulse qualities.

European readers sometimes misunderstood these accounts because they treated travellers' descriptions as complete and literal explanations of Chinese medicine.

This produced two competing interpretations:

| View of Chinese Medicine | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Scholarly/systematic medicine** | Chinese medicine possessed its own theories and organised system |
| **Empirical craft** | Chinese doctors had useful practical techniques but lacked coherent theory |

Many Europeans admired the **clinical skill** of Chinese physicians while simultaneously assuming that their theories were inferior or nonexistent.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Europe showed both **attraction and resistance** toward Chinese medicine.
>
> Chinese techniques could be admired without accepting Chinese medical theory.

---

## 4. Michael Boym and the Translation of Chinese Medicine

The Polish Jesuit **Michael Boym** was one of the most important seventeenth-century European writers on Chinese medicine.

His knowledge of Chinese language and culture allowed him to study Chinese medical texts and compile information about medicine and botany.

His work on pulse diagnosis, later published in Europe, presented Chinese medicine in a much more **systematic and scholarly form** than many earlier travel descriptions.

This was significant because Chinese medicine was increasingly presented not merely as a collection of exotic remedies, but as an **organised intellectual tradition**.

---

## 5. John Floyer and European Adaptation

The English physician **Sir John Floyer** became interested in Chinese pulse diagnosis and attempted to compare it with European medical traditions.

His work on the pulse combined:

```text
Chinese pulse methods
        +
Ancient Greek medicine
        +
European medical observation
```

European physicians therefore did not simply copy Chinese medicine. They **interpreted and adapted it through their own medical frameworks**.

In France, physicians associated with **vitalism** were particularly interested in Chinese medicine because vitalists believed that living organisms possessed an organising vital force. This appeared somewhat comparable to the Chinese concept of **qi 氣**.

---

## 6. Orientalism — Attraction to an “Exotic” Medicine

By the eighteenth century, European interest in China formed part of a wider fascination with Asian cultures.

Chinese medicine could therefore be understood through what later scholars such as **Edward Said** called **Orientalism**: the construction of the “East” as exotic, mysterious and fundamentally different from Europe.

This created an important tension:

```text
Chinese medicine
        ↓
Admired as exotic and effective
        +
Judged using European standards
```

Some scholars saw Chinese medicine as evidence of an ancient sophisticated civilisation, while others regarded it as interesting but scientifically inferior.

The European image of Chinese medicine was therefore often shaped as much by **European assumptions** as by Chinese medical practice itself.

---

## 7. Western Anatomy Enters Qing China

Medical exchange also moved from Europe into China.

Jesuit scholars at the **Qing imperial court** translated Western scientific and medical materials for the emperors. Western anatomical knowledge was among the subjects introduced.

Some works were translated into **Manchu**, the language of the Qing ruling elite.

These translations showed a very different approach to the body:

| Chinese Medical Emphasis | Western Anatomical Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Functional relationships | Physical structures |
| Qi and transformation | Morphology |
| Zang-fu systems | Anatomical organs |
| Systematic correspondence | Observation and dissection |

However, these early Western medical works had **limited circulation outside the imperial court**, so they did not immediately transform Chinese medicine.

---

## 8. Why Western Medicine Became More Powerful in the 19th Century

During the nineteenth century, Western medicine underwent major transformations.

Important developments included:

- **Anaesthesia** → made new surgical procedures possible
- **Modern surgery** → visible and immediate interventions
- **Pharmacology** → increasingly reliable drugs and active ingredients
- **Pathological anatomy** → disease linked to physical changes in tissues and organs
- **Germ theory** → specific microorganisms identified as causes of diseases
- **Public health** → prevention of disease at population level

These changes greatly increased the confidence and authority of Western medical practitioners.

```text
Observation
+ anatomy
+ experiment
+ laboratory science
        ↓
Specific disease causes
        ↓
Targeted intervention
```

Western medicine increasingly presented itself as **scientific medicine**.

---

## 9. Why Western Medicine Appealed to Chinese Patients

Western medicine was not accepted in China simply because foreigners imposed it.

Medical missionaries discovered that their medical services often attracted far greater interest than Christian preaching.

Surgery was particularly persuasive because the relationship between:

```text
Intervention
        ↓
Visible physical result
```

could be immediately understood by patients.

Some Western concepts were also not entirely alien to educated Chinese. Chinese medicine already contained concepts resembling:

- External pathogenic influences
- Bodily resistance to illness
- Circulation and movement within the body

The more radical difference was Western medicine's growing emphasis on:

**anatomy + morphology + experimentation + abandoning unquestioned authority of ancient texts**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The attraction of Western medicine came partly from its **clear relationship between intervention and observable effect**.

---

## 10. Imperialism, National Crisis and Medicine

The nineteenth-century encounter with Western medicine occurred alongside China's military defeats and foreign imperial expansion.

The **Opium Wars** and subsequent unequal political arrangements weakened Qing sovereignty and created a widespread sense of national crisis.

Chinese intellectuals increasingly asked:

**Why had China become weak while Western nations became powerful?**

Science and medicine became part of this debate.

Reformers increasingly associated:

```text
Modern science
+ Western medicine
+ public health
        ↓
Strong population
        ↓
Strong modern nation
```

Medicine was therefore no longer only about treating individual illness. It became connected to **national survival and state-building**.

---

## 11. Public Health as a New Model of Medicine

One of the strongest attractions of Western medicine was its ability to organise **public hygiene and epidemic control**.

Traditional Chinese medicine primarily focused on relationships between:

- Individual constitution
- Environment
- Pathogenic influences
- Pattern of illness

Modern public health introduced another level:

```text
Population
→ surveillance
→ sanitation
→ quarantine
→ masks
→ disease prevention
```

This transformed medicine into an important function of the **modern state**.

---

## 12. Wu Lien-teh 伍連德 and the Manchurian Plague

The **Manchurian plague of 1910–1911** became a powerful demonstration of modern scientific medicine.

The Qing authorities placed epidemic control under **Wu Lien-teh 伍連德**, a Penang-born, Cambridge-trained physician.

Wu recognised the outbreak as a respiratory form of plague and promoted measures including:

- Isolation and quarantine
- Public-health organisation
- Control of infected bodies
- Protective masks

His inexpensive mask became widely used among healthcare workers and the public.

The eventual control of the epidemic demonstrated that modern medicine could operate not only at the level of individual treatment but at the level of **entire populations**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The Manchurian plague strengthened the association:
>
> **Science → effective public health → modern nation**

This became a powerful challenge to older medical practices.

---

## 13. Science, Modernity and the Attack on Chinese Medicine

By the early twentieth century, many Chinese intellectuals associated **science with national progress**.

Chinese medicine was increasingly criticised for concepts such as:

- Yin–Yang 陰陽
- Five Phases 五行
- Qi 氣
- Six climatic qi 六氣

Critics described these ideas as **metaphysical, superstitious or scientifically unverifiable**.

This attitude was closely connected with **scientism** — the belief that modern science provided the most legitimate way to understand reality and solve China's problems.

The debate therefore became larger than medicine:

```text
Chinese medicine
→ associated with tradition

Western medicine
→ associated with science and modernity
```

---

## 14. Chen Duxiu 陳獨秀 and the New Culture Critique

**Chen Duxiu 陳獨秀**, an important intellectual associated with the **New Culture Movement**, strongly promoted Western science.

He attacked Chinese medical theories for lacking anatomy, bacteriology and experimental verification.

For reform-minded intellectuals such as Chen, rejecting older medical concepts formed part of a broader attempt to replace traditional Chinese intellectual structures with **science and modern knowledge**.

Chinese medicine therefore became caught in a cultural struggle over China's future:

```text
Tradition
vs
Modernity

Chinese learning
vs
Western science
```

---

## 15. Lu Xun 魯迅 and Distrust of Traditional Medicine

The writer **Lu Xun 魯迅** also became closely associated with criticism of traditional medicine.

His short story **_Medicine_ (藥)** portrays a family attempting to treat tuberculosis with a steamed bun soaked in the blood of an executed prisoner.

The remedy fails.

The story illustrates how ineffective healing practices could become associated with:

- Ignorance
- Superstition
- Social backwardness
- Human suffering

Lu Xun's criticism was also influenced by his own family's experience with expensive traditional treatments during his father's long illness.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> For many early twentieth-century reformers, criticism of Chinese medicine became symbolic of a larger criticism of **traditional Chinese society**.

---

## 16. The Rise of Western-Trained Chinese Physicians

By the 1910s and 1920s, increasing numbers of Chinese students trained in modern medicine overseas and returned to China.

The **National Medical Association of China** was established in **1915**, helping promote modern medical education and professionalisation.

Western medicine was now increasingly promoted by **Chinese physicians themselves**, not simply by foreign missionaries.

Their objective was often explicitly nationalist:

> **Modern medicine could strengthen China as a modern sovereign nation.**

This distinction is important. Western medicine was being transformed from something introduced by foreigners into something Chinese reformers could use for **national reconstruction**.

---

## 17. Peking Union Medical College

The establishment of the **Peking Union Medical College (PUMC)** in **1921**, supported by the Rockefeller Foundation, symbolised the institutional establishment of modern Western medicine in China.

PUMC emphasised:

- Laboratory science
- Anatomy
- Clinical observation
- Scientific research
- Professional medical education

It represented a new model of medical authority based on:

```text
University training
+ laboratory
+ hospital
+ scientific research
        ↓
Professional modern physician
```

This increasingly competed with traditional forms of medical education based on family transmission, apprenticeship and classical study.

---

## 18. The Creation of “Chinese Medicine” 中醫

The concept of **Chinese medicine (zhongyi 中醫)** as a single clearly defined system is itself partly a **modern construction**.

Historically, Chinese healing had included many different traditions:

```text
Systematic correspondence
+ herbal formulae
+ materia medica
+ acupuncture
+ bone-setting
+ popular medicine
+ religious healing
+ local practices
```

These traditions were not always organised into one coherent system.

The rise of Western medicine created pressure to define an opposite category:

```text
Western medicine 西醫
        ↕
Chinese medicine 中醫
```

Different older traditions were therefore increasingly grouped together under the single label **“Chinese medicine.”**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **“Chinese medicine” became more clearly defined when it had to distinguish itself from “Western medicine.”**
>
> What appears today to be one ancient unified system was historically much more diverse.

---

## 19. The Debate: Abolish, Preserve or Reform?

By the Republican period, Chinese intellectuals and physicians disagreed over what should happen to Chinese medicine.

Three broad positions emerged:

| Position | Main Argument |
|---|---|
| **Abolish** | Chinese medicine was unscientific and harmful |
| **Preserve** | Chinese medicine represented valuable national culture |
| **Reform / Scientise** | Keep useful practices but test and reinterpret them scientifically |

Supporters of preservation sometimes argued that Chinese medicine represented the **national essence (guocui 國粹)** and embodied a more humanistic Chinese civilisation.

Reformers instead tried to distinguish between:

```text
Useful clinical practices
vs
Unscientific traditional explanations
```

This third position eventually became especially important.

---

## 20. The 1929 Crisis and the Scientisation of Chinese Medicine

In **1929**, proposals to restrict or abolish traditional medicine produced strong opposition from Chinese medical practitioners.

The government could not simply eliminate Chinese medicine because:

- Large numbers of people still relied on it
- There were insufficient Western-trained physicians
- Practitioners organised politically
- Chinese medicine had cultural and economic importance

The Nationalist government therefore moved toward a compromise: **Chinese medicine could survive, but it had to modernise**.

The **Institute of National Medicine** was established in **1931**, and Chinese practitioners eventually gained legal recognition and licensing.

This process encouraged the **scientisation of Chinese medicine**.

```text
Traditional medical practices
        ↓
Scientific investigation
        ↓
Retain what is useful
Remove what is considered harmful / irrational
        ↓
Modernised Chinese medicine
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Chinese medicine survived modernity not by remaining unchanged, but by **redefining itself in response to science, regulation and Western medicine**.

---

## 21. 1949 — Chinese Medicine in the People's Republic

When the **Chinese Communist Party** established the People's Republic of China in **1949**, Chinese medicine still faced an uncertain future.

Marxist intellectual culture strongly respected modern science, and some Communist thinkers had previously been highly critical of traditional medicine.

However, abolishing Chinese medicine was impractical.

China faced:

- A huge population
- A shortage of Western-trained physicians
- Limited healthcare resources
- Large numbers of existing Chinese medical practitioners

Chinese medicine therefore had both **practical and political value**.

---

## 22. Mao Zedong and the Reinvention of Chinese Medicine

During the 1950s, **Mao Zedong** increasingly supported the development and integration of Chinese medicine.

The objective was not simply to restore every historical healing practice.

Instead, Chinese medicine was to be:

- Organised
- Standardised
- Taught institutionally
- Examined scientifically
- Integrated where possible with Western medicine
- Adapted to socialist society

Research increasingly focused on subjects such as:

- Scientific explanations of acupuncture
- Pharmacological analysis of medicinal substances
- Active ingredients in materia medica

This produced a new version of Chinese medicine that differed from the extremely diverse medical world of imperial China.

---

## 23. From Chinese Medicine to “Traditional Chinese Medicine”

What is now commonly called **Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)** was therefore not simply an unchanged medical system passed directly from antiquity to the present.

It was **selected, reorganised and standardised during the twentieth century**.

```text
Imperial Chinese medical traditions
        ↓
Selection
        ↓
Removal / marginalisation of some practices
        ↓
Standardisation
        ↓
Scientific reinterpretation
        ↓
Institutional education
        ↓
Modern TCM
```

Religious and explicitly demonic forms of healing were increasingly excluded, while theories such as:

- Yin–Yang
- Five Phases
- Qi
- Zang-fu
- Meridians
- Pattern differentiation
- Herbal formulae
- Acupuncture

were organised into a more systematic curriculum.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TCM is both traditional and modern.**
>
> Its theories draw heavily from premodern medicine, but the way they were selected, organised and taught was shaped strongly by the **twentieth-century Chinese state**.

---

## 24. Chinese Medicine under Maoism

During the Maoist period, Chinese medicine also had to fit the political language of socialist China.

Older terminology associated with imperial hierarchy could sometimes be reinterpreted because concepts such as **ruler and minister** were connected with the rejected feudal order.

More broadly, medical knowledge was expected to serve the new socialist society.

Maoist thought emphasised examining phenomena as interconnected wholes, which could be presented as compatible with the **holistic language of Chinese medicine**.

During the Cultural Revolution, Western influence could also be criticised as bourgeois or imperialist, giving indigenous Chinese medicine new ideological importance.

However, this did not mean a return to premodern medicine. The new system remained heavily shaped by:

```text
Traditional theories
+ modern institutions
+ scientific research
+ socialist ideology
```

---

## 25. Chinese and Western Medicine Were Not Mutually Exclusive

Attempts to portray Chinese medicine and Western medicine as completely incompatible were difficult to sustain in practice.

Chinese medicine increasingly incorporated:

- Scientific research
- Modern pharmaceutical analysis
- Institutional education
- Government licensing

Western medical institutions in China existed alongside Chinese medicine.

The result was not simply:

```text
Chinese medicine
VS
Western medicine
```

but increasingly:

```text
Chinese medicine
↔ scientific study
↔ Western medicine
```

The relationship could include competition, criticism, borrowing and integration simultaneously.

---

## 26. Transmission of Modern TCM to the West

At the same time that Chinese medicine was being reorganised inside twentieth-century China, it was also increasingly transmitted internationally.

The version encountered by many Western practitioners after the mid-twentieth century was therefore often the **modern standardised form of TCM**, rather than the full diversity of earlier Chinese healing traditions.

This helps explain an apparent paradox:

> Chinese medicine is extremely old, but **“Traditional Chinese Medicine” as a unified and standardised system is comparatively modern**.

---

## 27. Historical Transformation at a Glance

| Period | Major Development | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Late 16th–17th c.** | Missionaries transmit Chinese medical knowledge to Europe | First sustained Sino-European medical exchange |
| **17th c.** | Michael Boym studies Chinese medicine | Chinese medicine presented as a systematic tradition |
| **17th–18th c.** | European interest in pulse diagnosis | Attraction and resistance coexist |
| **Qing period** | Western anatomy translated at court | European medical knowledge enters China |
| **19th c.** | Surgery, anatomy, pharmacology and germ theory expand | Western medicine gains scientific authority |
| **Late Qing** | National crisis and reform | Medicine becomes linked to national strength |
| **1910–1911** | Wu Lien-teh and Manchurian plague | Modern public health demonstrates effectiveness |
| **1910s** | Anti-traditional intellectual movements | Chinese medicine attacked as unscientific |
| **1915** | National Medical Association of China | Western-trained Chinese physicians organise |
| **1921** | Peking Union Medical College | Modern Western medical education institutionalised |
| **1929** | Attempt to restrict Chinese medicine | Strong practitioner resistance |
| **1931** | Institute of National Medicine | Chinese medicine receives institutional support |
| **1930s** | Scientisation | Traditional practice reinterpreted using science |
| **1949 onward** | Communist government | Chinese medicine reorganised rather than abolished |
| **1950s onward** | Integration and standardisation | Foundations of modern TCM education |
| **Cultural Revolution era** | Maoist reinterpretation | Chinese medicine adapted to socialist ideology |
| **Post-1949** | International transmission | Standardised TCM increasingly spreads globally |

---

## Core Idea

> [!ABSTRACT]
> **Traditional Chinese Medicine was not simply an ancient system preserved unchanged until today. It was created through a long process of encounter, competition, reform and reinvention.**
>
> **Premodern Chinese medical traditions**
>
> ↓
>
> **Contact with European medicine**
>
> ↓
>
> **Western anatomy + surgery + laboratory science + public health**
>
> ↓
>
> **Chinese intellectual crisis: tradition vs science**
>
> ↓
>
> **Abolish? Preserve? Reform?**
>
> ↓
>
> **Scientisation + government regulation**
>
> ↓
>
> **Post-1949 standardisation and integration**
>
> ↓
>
> **Modern Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)**
>
> The key historical point is that **TCM is a modern organisation of older Chinese medical traditions**. Its core concepts originate in classical medicine, but its modern identity, curriculum, institutions and relationship with science were shaped by the political and intellectual transformations of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
>
> Chinese medicine survived the rise of Western medicine not by remaining untouched, but by **adapting, redefining itself and establishing a new form of legitimacy in the modern world**.
