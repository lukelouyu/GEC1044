
## 1. Why the Song Dynasty Was a Turning Point

![[Recording 20260905205935.m4a]]

The **Song dynasty (960–1279)** was a major turning point in Chinese medicine. Earlier medical theories such as Yin–Yang, Five Phases, qi, zang-fu organs and meridians already existed, but during the Song they were **reorganised, standardised and connected more closely to clinical practice**.

The Song government and scholar-officials actively collected ancient medical texts, corrected errors, printed authoritative editions and encouraged physicians to study the classics. Medicine therefore became more intellectually organised and socially respected.

By this period, many of the intellectual habits associated with later Chinese medicine had assumed a more mature form:

```text
Classical medical theory
        +
Drug therapy
        +
Clinical practice
        +
Government regulation
        +
Scholarly interpretation
        ↓
More integrated Chinese medical system
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The importance of the Song lies not in inventing Chinese medicine from nothing, but in **standardising, canonising and integrating earlier traditions into a more coherent medical system**.

---

## 2. From a Lowly Craft to Scholarly Medicine

Before the Song, medicine was often regarded as a **lower-status occupation** compared with scholarship and government service. Physicians could be grouped socially with diviners, spirit mediums and other technical specialists.

This changed during the Song because members of the **imperial family and scholar-official elite** became personally interested in medicine. Some emperors collected prescriptions, studied drug therapy, acupuncture and moxibustion, or sponsored major medical publications.

Medicine also became connected with the Confucian idea of **benevolent government**. Caring for the health of the population could be understood as similar to caring for the state.

Fan Zhongyan 范仲淹 famously expressed this new attitude through the idea that:

> If one cannot become a good minister, one should strive to become a good physician.

The physician was increasingly expected to be more than someone who simply knew remedies. A good physician should understand **medical classics, cosmology, physiology, ethics and cultivation**.

---

## 3. The Rise of the Scholar-Physician 儒醫

The Song elite itself was changing. During the Tang, political status had been strongly associated with **aristocratic family background and genealogy**. During the Song, the **imperial examination system** became increasingly important in selecting officials based on education and merit.

This produced a new elite based on **learning and cultivated ability rather than simply noble birth**.

At the same time, Daoist and Buddhist concepts were absorbed into new forms of Confucian thought, later broadly described as **Neo-Confucianism**.

Medicine became attractive to this new scholarly elite because studying the body could be treated as another form of intellectual and moral investigation.

The term **ruyi 儒醫 (scholar-physician)** came to describe physicians whose medical authority rested not only on technical skill but also on:

- Knowledge of classical texts
- Intellectual cultivation
- Moral responsibility
- Psychological and spiritual cultivation
- Ability to reason from theory to clinical practice

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Song physician → increasingly a cultured scholar, not simply a healer.**
>
> Medical knowledge became connected to the Neo-Confucian ideal of **investigating things (gewu 格物)** in order to understand the world and improve society.

---

## 4. Printing and the Democratisation of Medical Knowledge

Woodblock printing existed before the Song, but the Song developed it into a **large publishing industry**.

Printing transformed medicine because books became:

- Cheaper
- More widely available
- Easier to reproduce
- More consistent between copies

Before mass printing, medical texts were copied by hand. Copying introduced errors, so different physicians could possess significantly different versions of the same text.

Printing made it possible for physicians in different places to work from **the same edition**.

```text
Hand-copying
→ different copies + accumulated errors

Mass printing
→ standardised editions
→ wider circulation
→ shared medical knowledge
```

This increased access to medical knowledge and helped create a larger community of physicians and scholars discussing the same texts.

---

## 5. The Bureau for Editing Medical Texts 校正醫書局

The Song government's most important intervention in medical publishing was the establishment of the **Bureau for Editing Medical Texts (Jiaozheng Yishu Ju 校正醫書局)**.

Between **1057 and 1069**, the Bureau collected important medical works, compared different versions, corrected errors and produced authoritative editions.

Its publications included works such as:

- _Huangdi Neijing Suwen 黃帝內經素問_
- _Shanghan Lun 傷寒論_
- _Jingui Yaolüe 金匱要略_
- _Maijing 脈經_
- _Qianjin Yaofang 千金要方_
- Materia medica works
- Acupuncture texts

The Bureau produced around **15 medical works**, and many became some of the most influential medical texts in later Chinese history.

This process helped create what later generations would regard as the **Chinese medical canon**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A text did not become “classical” simply because it was old.
>
> **Government selection + editing + printing + distribution → increased authority and canonical status.**

This is especially important for the _Huangdi Neijing_. Its later central position in Chinese medicine was strengthened substantially through **Song institutions and printing culture**.

---

## 6. Revival of the _Shanghan Lun_ 傷寒論

One of the most important Song developments was the revival of **Zhang Zhongjing's _Shanghan Lun_**, originally composed around the end of the Han dynasty.

Before the Song, the text was not continuously dominant and had circulated imperfectly. The Song government's publication of an authoritative edition gave it new prestige and accessibility.

The revival was connected partly with the problem of **epidemics**. Song officials searched earlier medical literature for theories that could explain and treat epidemic and febrile disorders.

The _Shanghan Lun_ became increasingly important because it offered a structured way to understand:

- Disease progression
- Clinical patterns
- Relationship between symptoms and bodily processes
- Appropriate medicinal formulae

Later Song physicians further interpreted Cold Damage theory and linked it more closely to **channels, zang-fu organs and classical physiology**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The _Shanghan Lun_ was written much earlier, but its **major clinical influence expanded during the Song and later periods**.

---

## 7. Government Formularies and the Expansion of Drug Therapy

Drug therapy became increasingly prestigious during the Song.

Earlier formula collections often followed a simple structure:

```text
Disease / symptom
        ↓
Fixed formula
```

Song formularies became increasingly sophisticated. The lecture highlights large government-sponsored compilations containing thousands of formulae, while later works increasingly added **classical theoretical explanations** to the prescriptions.

Instead of only saying what formula should be used, physicians increasingly tried to explain:

- Why the formula worked
- What bodily process was disturbed
- Whether the patient was deficient or excessive
- Which zang-fu system was involved
- How the formula related to classical medical theory

This brought **pharmaceutical therapy into closer contact with Yin–Yang, Five Phases, qi and zang-fu physiology**.

The number of medicinal substances recorded in materia medica literature also expanded greatly during the Northern Song.

---

## 8. Imperial Pharmacies and Standardisation

The Song government also became involved in the **production and distribution of drugs**.

Government dispensaries and pharmacies helped:

- Regulate medicinal substances
- Control prices
- Supply drugs
- Provide standard prescriptions
- Improve access during emergencies

A government dispensary was established in **1076**, and official pharmaceutical institutions expanded afterward.

This had an important effect on medical practice because ordinary people could gain access to **standardised formulae and subsidised medicines** rather than depending entirely on expensive individual physicians.

However, this success later produced a problem: **fixed formulae could be applied too mechanically**.

This became an important target of criticism among Jin-Yuan scholar-physicians.

---

## 9. Medicine Becomes a Government Responsibility

Before the Song, organised care for the sick and poor was often provided by **religious or charitable institutions**.

The Song government increasingly treated public health as part of the state's **moral responsibility**.

Government involvement expanded into:

- Medical publishing
- Physician training
- Medical examinations
- Pharmacies
- Hospitals and charitable institutions
- Drug subsidies
- Epidemic response

This reflects the Confucian concept that a good government should **care for the people**.

Medicine therefore became both:

```text
A scholarly profession
        +
An instrument of government
```

---

## 10. Song → Jin-Yuan: From Diseases to Patterns 證

During the **Jin and Yuan dynasties**, scholar-physicians continued to reinterpret classical medical knowledge.

A major change was the growing emphasis on **zheng 證 (pattern/presentation)** rather than simply identifying a named disease.

It is useful to distinguish three ideas:

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Symptom** | Individual manifestation such as nausea, fever or headache |
| **Bing 病** | Named disease or medical disorder |
| **Zheng 證** | Overall pattern of bodily disharmony shown by the patient's manifestations |

A single disease could therefore appear as **different patterns in different patients**, depending on factors such as:

- Body constitution
- Environment
- Climate
- Stage of disease
- Qi and blood
- Zang-fu condition

This changed treatment from:

```text
Same disease
→ same formula
```

toward:

```text
Disease
+ symptoms
+ body constitution
+ environment
+ stage of illness
        ↓
Identify zheng 證
        ↓
Individualised treatment
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Pattern differentiation became the essential bridge between diagnosis and treatment.**
>
> The question was no longer only **“What disease does this patient have?”**
>
> It also became **“What pattern of disharmony is this particular patient showing?”**

---

## 11. Eight-Principle Differentiation 八綱

During the Jin-Yuan period, diagnostic thinking became increasingly systematised.

A patient's pattern could be organised using major paired categories:

| Principle | Question |
|---|---|
| **Yin 陰 / Yang 陽** | What is the overall nature of the disorder? |
| **Interior 裏 / Exterior 表** | Is the disorder mainly internal or external? |
| **Cold 寒 / Heat 熱** | Does the pattern show cold or heat characteristics? |
| **Deficiency 虛 / Excess 實** | Is there weakness of normal function or excessive pathogenic influence? |

These categories later became known collectively as the **Eight Principles (bagang 八綱)**.

Zhang Yuansu 張元素 was especially important in systematising these diagnostic relationships and connecting:

**zang-fu organs ↔ meridians ↔ six qi ↔ clinical patterns**

He also distinguished:

- **Ben 本 (root)** → underlying pathological process
- **Biao 標 (branch)** → outward or symptomatic manifestation

This encouraged physicians to treat the **underlying mechanism**, not merely the visible symptom.

# The Four Great Masters of the Jin-Yuan 金元四大家

The Jin-Yuan period produced four especially influential scholar-physicians:

| Physician | Main Theory | Main Treatment Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Liu Wansu 劉完素** | Fire and heat | Cooling / draining fire |
| **Zhang Congzheng 張從正** | External pathogenic factors | Attack and expel pathogens |
| **Li Gao 李杲** | Spleen and stomach weakness | Tonify the centre |
| **Zhu Zhenheng 朱震亨** | Ministerial fire + Yin deficiency | Nourish Yin |

Although they interpreted disease differently, all four shared an important principle:

> **Treatment should respond to the patient's actual pattern, constitution and environment rather than relying blindly on fixed formulae.**

---

## 12. Liu Wansu 劉完素 — Fire and Heat

**Liu Wansu (c. 1120–1200)** lived during the Song-Jin transition.

He emphasised the classical theory of the **Five Phases and Six Climatic Qi** and argued that abnormal environmental and climatic influences could generate disease.

His central idea was that many pathogenic processes eventually transformed into **fire or heat**.

```text
Different pathogenic influences
        ↓
Transformation
        ↓
Fire / Heat
```

Treatment therefore often required:

- Cooling
- Draining pathogenic fire
- Nourishing water
- Protecting Kidney Yin

This approach became associated with the **Cooling School (Hanliang Pai 寒涼派)**.

Liu also emphasised that diagnosis should consider:

**climate + environment + individual constitution**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Liu Wansu:
>
> **Disease tends toward Fire/Heat → drain fire + nourish water.**

This uses Five-Phase logic because **Water controls Fire**.

---

## 13. Zhang Congzheng 張從正 — Attack and Purgation

**Zhang Congzheng (1156–1228)** emphasised **external pathogenic influences** and improper food intake.

He argued that many illnesses occur because harmful influences enter or accumulate in the body.

These could include:

- Wind
- Cold
- Heat
- Dampness
- Dryness
- Fire
- Improper food and drink

Instead of strengthening the body immediately, Zhang believed the priority was often to **expel the pathogen**.

His three major strategies were:

| Method | Function |
|---|---|
| **Diaphoresis** | Induce sweating |
| **Emesis** | Induce vomiting |
| **Purgation** | Evacuate through the bowels |

He also employed acupuncture, moxibustion, massage, exercise and dietary measures.

His approach became associated with the **School of Attack and Purgation (Gongxia Pai 攻下派)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Zhang Congzheng:
>
> **Foreign pathogenic influence is causing the problem → remove it decisively.**
>
> He generally preferred elimination over excessive tonification.

---

## 14. Li Gao 李杲 — Spleen and Stomach

**Li Gao (1180–1251)**, also known as **Li Dongyuan 李東垣**, shifted attention toward the body's internal ability to generate and distribute qi.

His central idea was that the **Spleen and Stomach are the basis of the body's qi**.

They transform food and drink into usable nourishment. If this digestive centre becomes weak, the body cannot properly produce, transform and distribute qi.

Li identified several major causes of Spleen-Stomach damage:

1. **Improper diet**
2. **Overexertion**
3. **Excessive emotions**

```text
Diet / exhaustion / emotions
        ↓
Spleen-Stomach weakened
        ↓
Poor transformation of food and qi
        ↓
Qi deficiency
        ↓
Multiple illnesses
```

Treatment should therefore **replenish and strengthen the centre**.

His influential work was the:

**_Piwei Lun 脾胃論 — Discussion of the Spleen and Stomach_**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Li Gao:
>
> **Weak Spleen/Stomach → weak source of qi → tonify the centre.**

This school became highly influential because later Chinese medicine continued to regard digestive function as fundamental to overall health.

---

## 15. Zhu Zhenheng 朱震亨 — Ministerial Fire and Nourishing Yin

**Zhu Zhenheng (1281–1358)**, also known as **Zhu Danxi 朱丹溪**, is often considered the culmination of Jin-Yuan medical thought.

He combined earlier medical ideas with **Neo-Confucian philosophy**, especially the principle of **investigating things and extending knowledge (gewu zhizhi 格物致知)**.

Zhu criticised both fixed formula books and overly simplified applications of Cold Damage theory.

His major concept concerned **Ministerial Fire (xianghuo 相火)**.

Ministerial Fire is an active Yang force that supports:

- Physiological activity
- Human desires
- Bodily functions

However, excessive desires and emotions could cause this fire to become pathological.

```text
Excessive desire / emotions
        ↓
Ministerial Fire flares
        ↓
Yin and Blood are damaged
        ↓
Heat-related disorder
```

Treatment should therefore frequently **nourish Yin**, especially **Kidney Yin**, to restrain excessive Fire.

This again uses Five-Phase logic:

```text
Kidney → Water
Water controls Fire
Therefore:
Nourish Kidney Yin
→ strengthen Water
→ restrain pathological Fire
```

This became associated with the **Yin-Nourishing School (Ziyin Pai 滋陰派)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Zhu Zhenheng:
>
> **Excessive Ministerial Fire consumes Yin → nourish Kidney Yin to control Fire.**

---

## 16. Comparing the Four Great Masters

The four masters can be understood as offering different answers to the same question:

**Where does disease primarily come from, and what should treatment do?**

| Master | Main Problem | Treatment Logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Liu Wansu** | Pathogens transform into Fire/Heat | **Cool and drain** |
| **Zhang Congzheng** | External pathogens accumulate | **Attack and expel** |
| **Li Gao** | Spleen/Stomach and qi become deficient | **Tonify and replenish** |
| **Zhu Zhenheng** | Ministerial Fire damages Yin | **Nourish Yin** |

A useful larger distinction is:

```text
Liu Wansu + Zhang Congzheng
        ↓
Emphasis on removing EXCESS pathogenic factors

Li Gao + Zhu Zhenheng
        ↓
Emphasis on replenishing DEFICIENCY
```

However, none of these theories should be treated as an isolated universal rule. Their importance lies in showing how physicians could use the **same classical tradition to construct different clinical models**.

---

## 17. From Fixed Formulae to Individualised Medicine

The Jin-Yuan masters criticised the Song tendency toward **government-standardised formulae for named diseases**.

They argued that simply matching:

```text
Disease → standard formula
```

was insufficient.

A formula should instead be adapted to:

```text
Patient
+ pattern 證
+ constitution
+ climate
+ environment
+ stage of illness
        ↓
Tailored treatment
```

This was one of the most important developments in later Chinese medicine.

The physician became responsible for **reasoning from classical theory to the unique condition of the individual patient**.

---

## 18. Systematic Correspondence Enters Pharmacology

The integration of theory and drug therapy continued during the Jin-Yuan period.

Wang Haogu 王好古's **_Tangye Bencao 湯液本草_ (Materia Medica for Decoctions, 1298)** developed a pharmacology in which medicinal substances could be explained using **systematic correspondence and Five-Phase theory**.

This represents the long-term closing of the earlier divide between:

```text
Classical theory
        ↔
Drug therapy
```

By the later imperial period, formula medicine was no longer simply an empirical collection of remedies. It had become increasingly embedded within the theoretical framework of Chinese medicine.

## 19. Song → Jin → Yuan Development at a Glance

| Period / Development | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Northern Song** | Major transformation and standardisation of medicine |
| **Imperial interest in medicine** | Raises medical legitimacy |
| **Examination-based elite** | “Men of culture” increasingly replace hereditary aristocracy |
| **Scholar-physicians 儒醫** | Medicine becomes an intellectual and moral profession |
| **Printing revolution** | Cheaper, identical and widely circulated medical texts |
| **1057–1069 Bureau for Editing Medical Texts** | Corrects and canonises major medical works |
| **Revival of _Shanghan Lun_** | Cold Damage becomes a major clinical tradition |
| **Government formularies** | Drug therapy becomes standardised and accessible |
| **Government pharmacies / hospitals** | Healthcare increasingly treated as state responsibility |
| **Jin-Yuan period** | Shift toward pattern differentiation 證 |
| **Eight Principles 八綱** | Systematises diagnosis |
| **Liu Wansu** | Fire/Heat → cooling |
| **Zhang Congzheng** | Pathogens → attack/purge |
| **Li Gao** | Spleen/Stomach deficiency → tonify |
| **Zhu Zhenheng** | Ministerial Fire / Yin deficiency → nourish Yin |

## Core Idea

> [!ABSTRACT]
> The **Song-Jin-Yuan period transformed Chinese medicine from a collection of inherited theories and therapeutic traditions into a more standardised, scholarly and clinically adaptable system**.
>
> **Song Dynasty**
>
> Classical texts  
> + Government editing and printing  
> + Scholar-physicians  
> + Drug formularies  
> + Public healthcare  
> ↓  
> **Standardisation and medical legitimacy**
>
> **Jin-Yuan**
>
> Criticism of fixed formulae  
> + Pattern differentiation 證  
> + Individual constitution  
> + Environment and climate  
> + New interpretations of classical theory  
> ↓  
> **Individualised clinical medicine**
>
> The Song established the **texts, institutions and scholarly culture** of mature Chinese medicine. The Jin-Yuan physicians then used this foundation to develop **multiple competing clinical theories**, especially the ideas of **Fire/Heat, pathogen elimination, Spleen-Stomach deficiency and Yin nourishment**.
>
> Together, these developments helped establish the pattern-based and individualised style of Chinese medicine that remained influential in later imperial medicine and continues to shape Chinese medical practice today.
