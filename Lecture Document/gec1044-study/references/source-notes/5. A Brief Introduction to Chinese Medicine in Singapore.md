

## 1. Chinese Medicine in Everyday Singapore

Chinese medicine in Singapore is not confined to **TCM clinics or medical halls**. It also appears in everyday habits, consumer products, health beliefs and even veterinary care.

Examples discussed in the lecture include:

- Drinking **coconut water after vaccination** because it is believed to “clear heat” and cool the body.
- Chinese proprietary medicines sold beside biomedical products in supermarkets.
- Increasing use of **traditional Chinese veterinary medicine**, including pulse-taking and acupuncture for pets.

These examples show that Chinese medical ideas continue to shape everyday understandings of health even among people who may not regularly visit a TCM physician.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Chinese medicine in Singapore survives not only as a formal medical profession, but also as a **cultural framework for understanding the body, food, heat/cold, illness and recovery**.

---

## 2. Why Do People in Singapore Use TCM?

A survey presented in the lecture suggested several common reasons for seeking TCM:

| Reason | Approx. proportion |
|---|---:|
| TCM is considered effective | **16%** |
| Avoid side effects of biomedicine | **16%** |
| Recommended by family or friends | **14%** |
| Biomedicine had not worked well enough | **12%** |

TCM is especially associated with conditions such as **ankle sprains, back pain and chronic pain**, although some patients also seek TCM for coughs, colds and other illnesses.

The important point is that patients do not necessarily see Chinese and Western medicine as complete opposites. Instead, they may choose between them depending on:

```text
type of illness
+ previous experience
+ family recommendation
+ cost
+ perceived side effects
+ expectations of treatment
```

---

## 3. Three Main Questions of the Lecture

The lecture approaches Chinese medicine in Singapore through three broad questions:

```text
1. What is special about Chinese medicine in Singapore,
   and how has it been shaped?

2. How have Chinese physicians understood and treated
   common illnesses in Singapore?

3. What similarities, differences and interactions exist
   between Chinese medicine and Malay medicine?
```

These questions show that Chinese medicine in Singapore should be studied as a **historically changing and locally adapted medical tradition**, not simply as Chinese medicine transplanted unchanged from China.

---

## 4. From a Central Healing System to Complementary Medicine

In **1921**, Chinese physicians greatly outnumbered Western-trained Chinese doctors in Singapore. The lecture gives the approximate figures of:

```text
300+ Chinese medicine physicians
vs
34 Chinese doctors practising Western medicine
```

At that time, most migrants and local communities still relied heavily on traditional systems such as Chinese medicine, Malay medicine and Ayurveda.

By **2019**, the situation had reversed: Western medical doctors were much more numerous than registered TCM physicians.

```text
Early 20th century
Chinese medicine = major community healing system

            ↓

Modern Singapore
Biomedicine = dominant formal healthcare system
TCM = complementary / peripheral system
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The history of Chinese medicine in Singapore is therefore also a history of its **changing social position** within the healthcare system.

---

## 5. Why Traditional Medicine Was Important in Colonial Singapore

Before the mid-twentieth century, the colonial government did not provide comprehensive healthcare for the migrant population.

Chinese, Malay and Indian migrants therefore brought their own:

- Medical knowledge
- Therapeutic practices
- Medicines
- Healers
- Institutional models

Chinese migrants did not simply bring treatments from China. They also brought ways of **organising medical charity and professional authority**.

This created space for Chinese medicine to become an important part of community life.

---

## 6. Thong Chai Medical Institution and Medical Charity

**Thong Chai Medical Institution** became one of the most important examples of Chinese medical charity in Singapore.

It was established by wealthy Chinese merchants to provide treatment for poor labourers and immigrants.

This model followed earlier charitable institutions in China, where local elites sponsored dispensaries and medical halls in order to:

```text
meet community needs
+
help the poor
+
demonstrate moral responsibility
+
strengthen elite leadership and prestige
```

Medical charity therefore had both a **social welfare function** and a **political/social leadership function**.

---

## 7. Testing Physicians Before Formal Licensing

In colonial Singapore, Western medical practitioners were trained, licensed and registered, but Chinese physicians initially had no equivalent state licensing system.

Thong Chai responded by organising examinations modelled partly on the **Chinese imperial examination system**.

Candidates were tested on matters such as:

- Diagnosis
- Treatment of common illnesses
- Application of classical knowledge to Singapore conditions

Successful candidates could gain reputation and employment.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Before government licensing, professional legitimacy could be created through **community institutions, examinations and public reputation**.

---

## 8. Localisation — Chinese Medicine Had to Adapt

Chinese medicine prospered in Singapore because it was **localised**.

Localisation meant adapting Chinese medical theories and practices to:

- Tropical climate
- Local diseases
- Local foods and plants
- Multi-ethnic society
- Colonial and postcolonial law
- Local religious requirements
- Availability of medical resources

This is one of the central themes of the lecture.

```text
Chinese medical tradition
        ↓
Migration to Singapore
        ↓
Adaptation to local climate, society and law
        ↓
Singapore Chinese medicine
```

---

## 9. Separation from Biomedicine in Singapore

A major difference between Singapore and China is the legal relationship between Chinese medicine and biomedicine.

The **Medical Registration Ordinance of 1905** created a framework in which Chinese medicine and other traditional systems were treated as separate from Western medicine.

In Singapore, TCM physicians cannot simply prescribe biomedical drugs as part of TCM treatment.

By contrast, in China, Chinese and Western medical methods may be used together more directly.

For example:

```text
Biomedical drug
→ used for rapid symptom control

Chinese herbal medicine
→ used to consolidate treatment or address the underlying pattern
```

Singapore therefore developed a clearer **institutional boundary** between the two systems.

---

## 10. Halal Certification and Multi-Ethnic Adaptation

Singapore TCM also adapted to the needs of a multi-ethnic population.

Some locally produced Chinese medicinal products obtained **halal certification**, making them more acceptable to Muslim consumers.

Malay patients may also choose therapies such as:

- Acupuncture
- Massage
- Other non-ingestible treatments

because these may be easier to reconcile with religious requirements.

This reflects a broader process:

```text
Chinese medicine
        ↓
adaptation to multicultural society
        ↓
greater use beyond the Chinese community
```

Research in Singapore has also examined whether TCM syndrome patterns differ across ethnic groups.

---

## 11. The Role of the Colonial and Singapore State

The colonial government often regarded Asian traditional medicine as “native” or unscientific, but it still **tolerated and indirectly supported** Chinese medical institutions.

Why?

Because the colonial state did not have enough medical resources to serve everyone.

Local Chinese leaders and charitable clinics therefore helped fill the healthcare gap.

The government could:

- Permit traditional practice
- Grant land
- Allow charitable medical institutions to operate
- Rely on community organisations to provide low-cost care

After independence, this basic logic continued in a new form. TCM could help provide **complementary, preventive and relatively low-cost healthcare**.

---

## 12. TCM as Complementary Medicine

Modern Singapore does not position TCM as a replacement for biomedicine.

Instead, it is more commonly framed as **complementary medicine**, especially in areas such as:

- Pain relief
- Rehabilitation
- Chronic conditions
- Health maintenance
- Preventive care

The lecture gives the example of mobile TCM services for lower-income and elderly residents.

This illustrates how Chinese medicine continues to serve a social welfare role similar to earlier charitable clinics, even within a modern biomedical healthcare system.

---

## 13. Professional Associations and Self-Regulation

Before the **TCM Practitioners Act (2000)**, professional associations played a major role in organising and regulating Chinese medicine.

One important example is the **Singapore Chinese Physicians’ Association (SCPA)**, founded in **1946**.

Many such organisations developed a **3-in-1 or 4-in-1 structure** combining:

```text
Professional association
+ charity clinic
+ medical college
+ research institute
```

This created a self-supporting institutional system.

---

## 14. Why the Association–Clinic–College Model Mattered

The integrated structure allowed different functions to support one another.

For example:

```text
Clinic
→ provides patients and clinical cases

College
→ trains future physicians

Research centre
→ studies treatment outcomes

Association
→ organises and regulates practitioners
```

Students could receive practical training in clinics, while clinical experience generated material for research.

This structure helped Chinese medicine become more **institutionalised and professionalised** before strong state regulation existed.

# Part II — Treating Common Illnesses in Singapore

## 15. Geographical Locality

A key Chinese medical idea used by local physicians is **geographical locality**.

Disease patterns are shaped by local conditions such as:

- Climate
- Environment
- Diet
- Lifestyle
- Body constitution
- Seasonal changes

Singapore is therefore not medically treated as if it were identical to China.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The same disease may require a different interpretation or treatment in Singapore because **place matters in Chinese medicine**.

---

## 16. Singapore as a Hot and Humid Environment

Singapore is a tropical island with persistent heat and humidity.

Local Chinese physicians therefore frequently emphasised:

- **Summer heat**
- **Dampness**
- **Heat**
- **Damp-heat**

These environmental factors interact with diet and lifestyle.

For example:

```text
humid climate
+
spicy / oily food
+
cold drinks
+
irregular eating
+
overwork
        ↓
Spleen weakness
        ↓
poor fluid transformation
        ↓
dampness accumulation
        ↓
damp-heat
```

This is why **damp-heat and Spleen deficiency** appear repeatedly in local disease explanations.

---

## 17. Tuberculosis — “Lung Consumption”

Tuberculosis was a major disease in colonial Singapore.

Overcrowded and poorly ventilated migrant housing contributed to transmission, while government public-health measures remained limited for many years.

Chinese physicians interpreted TB through the framework of **“lung consumption”** rather than bacterial infection.

They explained the illness through several interacting causes.

---

## 18. Three Main Causes Used to Explain TB

Local physicians referred to:

### External climatic factors 六淫

These include:

- Wind
- Cold
- Summer heat
- Dampness
- Dryness
- Fire

Singapore's heat and humidity were seen as especially relevant.

### Internal emotional factors 七情

Emotions such as:

- Anger
- Worry
- Anxiety
- Sadness
- Fear
- Fright

could disrupt the movement of qi.

### Pestilential qi

Bad or epidemic air could also be used to explain widespread illness.

Thus TB was understood through an interaction of **climate, emotion, qi and phlegm**, rather than a single microbial cause.

---

## 19. Emotion, Qi and Phlegm in TB

The lecture explains a pathway such as:

```text
worry / sadness / fear
        ↓
qi stagnation or qi deficiency
        ↓
weakened transformation and transport of fluids
        ↓
fluid accumulation
        ↓
phlegm
        ↓
phlegm-heat affecting the Lung
```

Treatment therefore focused not only on “heat”, but especially on **resolving phlegm while separately clearing heat**.

This illustrates a major difference between TCM and biomedical disease explanation.

---

## 20. Flu and Common Cold — Cold Damage vs Warm Disease

Chinese medicine did not classify influenza primarily through the category of viral infection.

Instead, local physicians often discussed two broad patterns:

| Feature | **Cold Damage 傷寒** | **Warm Disease 溫病** |
|---|---|---|
| Main pathogen | Wind-cold | Wind-heat / warm toxin |
| Route of attack | Exterior / skin | Nose and mouth |
| Fever | Mild | Higher |
| Aversion to cold | Stronger | Less prominent |
| Sweating | Usually little / none | More common |
| Phlegm | White / clear | Yellow / sticky |
| Pulse | Floating, tight | Floating, rapid |
| Tongue | Less red | Redder, especially front/sides |
| Treatment tendency | Pungent-warm herbs | Pungent-cool herbs |

This distinction matters because **similar symptoms can require opposite treatment strategies**.

---

## 21. Why Warm Disease Was Important in Singapore

Singapore was considered especially suitable for **warm-disease theory** because of its tropical climate.

Physicians described Southeast Asia as:

```text
hot
+ humid
+ maritime
        ↓
easy accumulation of dampness and heat
        ↓
higher likelihood of warm-disease patterns
```

Many migrant physicians therefore relied heavily on approaches associated with the **Warm Disease School 溫病學派**.

---

## 22. Damp-Heat and the Spleen

Heat alone was not the only problem.

Humidity and dietary habits were said to weaken the **Spleen's transforming and transporting functions**.

When the Spleen is weak:

```text
fluids are not transformed properly
        ↓
dampness accumulates
        ↓
dampness may combine with heat
        ↓
damp-heat
```

Common signs mentioned include:

- Loss of appetite
- Heavy body or head
- Sticky/greasy tongue coating
- Mouth ulcers
- Red/swollen lips
- Skin problems such as eczema

---

## 23. Adapting Classical Formulae to Singapore

Local physicians did not simply copy classical prescriptions unchanged.

They used the principle of **addition and subtraction 加減**:

```text
classical formula
        ↓
assess local climate + patient + available ingredients
        ↓
add / remove / substitute herbs
        ↓
locally adapted prescription
```

During influenza outbreaks, locally available foods and plants such as **water chestnut** were used in simplified remedies influenced by warm-disease traditions.

This reflects two important ideas:

1. **Prevention is better than cure.**
2. **Local foods and plants may be especially suitable for local people and local illness.**

---

## 24. Does Cold Damage Exist in Tropical Singapore?

Yes.

Local physicians debated whether a hot country could still produce **wind-cold** disorders.

They argued that cold exposure could occur through:

- Monsoon weather
- Cool evenings or mornings
- Walking barefoot
- Cold or raw food
- Iced drinks

Therefore, geographical locality does not mean every illness in Singapore is automatically a warm disease.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> TCM diagnosis still depends on the **actual syndrome pattern**, not only the general climate.

---

## 25. Hand, Foot and Mouth Disease — A Warm-Disease Interpretation

HFMD was interpreted as a form of **warm disease**, especially a **wind-warm / epidemic toxin** pattern.

Typical symptoms include:

- Fever
- Lethargy
- Poor appetite
- Blisters and sores
- Mouth ulcers
- Sometimes cough

The lecture explains the condition using several factors:

```text
epidemic toxin
+
heat
+
dampness
+
Spleen weakness
```

Treatment therefore has to address more than one mechanism.

---

## 26. Different HFMD Patterns Require Different Priorities

The relative severity of **dampness and heat** changes the treatment strategy.

| Pattern | Typical indication | Main treatment priority |
|---|---|---|
| Dampness ≈ heat | Thirst + heavy head | Resolve dampness, clear heat, remove toxin |
| Dampness > heat | Fluid-filled blisters, slippery pulse | Remove dampness first |
| Heat > dampness | Insomnia, dark/yellow urine, red tongue tip | Clear heat / Heart fire first |

This illustrates the logic of **syndrome differentiation**:

> Same biomedical disease → different Chinese medical patterns → different treatment.

---

## 27. Eczema — “Damp Sore” and Wind

Eczema is also strongly associated with **dampness**, but wind is especially important because wind is linked with **itching**.

A simplified mechanism is:

```text
humid environment
+ improper diet
        ↓
Spleen deficiency
        ↓
internal damp-heat

external wind + dampness + heat
        ↓
obstruction in the skin
        ↓
itching, dryness, lesions
```

At later stages, prolonged illness may damage yin and consume blood, making the skin rough and leathery.

---

## 28. Why Not Everyone Gets Eczema

Even when people live in the same humid environment, they do not all develop eczema.

Chinese medicine explains this through **body constitution**.

```text
same external environment
        +
different constitutions
        ↓
different susceptibility
```

Some people may be more vulnerable to wind, dampness or heat than others.

This again shows that Chinese medicine focuses on the **relationship between person and environment**, not merely the external disease agent.

---

## 29. Removing Dampness

The lecture explains that dampness can be treated through different routes.

Two major strategies are:

```text
1. Promote urination
2. Promote sweating
```

The choice depends on where the dampness is located and what other patterns accompany it.

For example:

| Pattern | General herbal tendency |
|---|---|
| Dampness in urinary/digestive system | Bland / neutral herbs to drain dampness |
| Exterior dampness | Aromatic or pungent herbs to disperse |
| Wind-damp | Pungent-warm herbs |
| Damp-heat | Bitter-cold herbs |
| Cold-damp | Bitter-warm herbs |

The important point is that “dampness” is not treated with one fixed medicine.

# Part III — Chinese and Malay Medicine

## 30. Why Compare Chinese and Malay Medicine?

Singapore is a multi-ethnic society.

Chinese medicine developed alongside Malay, Indian and other healing traditions.

Comparing Chinese medicine with **traditional Malay medicine (TMM)** helps reveal:

- Shared assumptions
- Different theories
- Cross-cultural borrowing
- Local adaptation
- Effects of religion and culture
- Modernisation under biomedical regulation

---

## 31. Shared Holistic View

Both Chinese and Malay medicine emphasise the interconnectedness of:

```text
body
+ mind
+ environment
+ lifestyle
+ spiritual / cultural world
```

They therefore place strong importance on:

- Preventing illness
- Maintaining balance
- Preserving health
- Understanding the whole person

This differs from a strongly reductionist model that isolates one organ or one pathogen.

---

## 32. Vital Energy, Spirit and the Human Body

Malay medicine also contains concepts resembling vital energy and spirit.

The lecture discusses **semangat**, a soul or life substance whose disturbance or loss may make a person vulnerable to illness.

This can be compared, cautiously, with Chinese medical concepts such as:

- Qi
- Shen
- Balance between physical and non-physical aspects of the person

The concepts are not identical, but both traditions treat health as involving more than physical anatomy alone.

---

## 33. Four Elements and Humoral Balance in Malay Medicine

Traditional Malay medicine uses a framework based on four elements:

```text
Earth
Water
Air / Wind
Fire
```

These correspond with qualities such as:

- Hot
- Cold
- Damp
- Dry

The human body is also understood through humoral balance.

Disease occurs when one element or humour becomes excessive or deficient.

This creates an important similarity with Chinese medicine:

```text
health = balance
illness = imbalance
treatment = restore balance
```

---

## 34. Shared Therapeutic Practices

Chinese and Malay medicine may use overlapping kinds of treatment, including:

- Herbal medicine
- Food as medicine
- Massage
- Bone setting
- Cupping
- Incantations or ritual practices
- Rules about timing, number, direction or frequency

This does **not** mean the theories are identical.

Similar techniques may be explained differently in each medical system.

---

## 35. Food and Medicine

Both traditions blur the boundary between **food and medicine**.

In Malay medicine, **jamu** may be prepared from plants, animal products and minerals for treatment or general wellbeing.

In Chinese medicine, everyday foods may also be chosen according to their warming, cooling or other therapeutic properties.

The common principle is:

> Use ordinary substances to maintain balance and prevent illness before stronger medical intervention becomes necessary.

---

## 36. Religion and Healing

Traditional medicine is closely connected to cultural and religious worldviews.

Malay medical manuscripts may incorporate:

- Quranic prayers
- Prophetic traditions
- Incantations
- Spiritual protection
- Taboos

Chinese medicine also historically interacted with Daoist ritual, spirit concepts and religious healing.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Medical traditions cannot be fully understood apart from the **culture and religious worldview** in which they developed.

---

## 37. Modernisation of Traditional Medicine

Chinese and Malay medicine have both undergone modernisation.

This includes:

```text
institutional education
+ professional regulation
+ standard textbooks
+ scientific research
+ quality control
+ pharmaceutical standardisation
```

Traditional medicines are increasingly expected to meet modern requirements for:

- Safety
- Efficacy
- Manufacturing quality
- Standardisation
- Professional registration

This parallels the transformation of Chinese medicine discussed in Lecture 4: traditional medicine survives by **adapting to modern institutions and scientific regulation**.

---

## 38. What Gets Marginalised During Modernisation?

Not every traditional practice fits modern regulatory systems.

Spiritual healers, shamans and ritual specialists may be excluded from professional registration because their practices do not match biomedical or scientific standards.

As a result:

```text
traditional medicine
        ↓ modern regulation
standardised / professionalised practices survive more easily
ritual / supernatural practices become marginalised
```

This is another example of how modernity **selects and reshapes tradition**.

---

## 39. Dry Cupping vs Wet Cupping

Cupping is a useful example of how similar techniques can have different meanings.

| | **Chinese Medicine** | **Malay / Islamic Tradition** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Dry cupping | Wet cupping / hijama |
| Bloodletting | No | Yes |
| Main logic | Dispel cold/dampness, move qi and blood | Remove excess fluids, waste or toxins |
| Method | Suction using heated cup or air pump | Suction → skin puncture → suction again |

Thus “cupping” is not one universal practice. Its **theory, procedure and therapeutic purpose** depend on the medical tradition.

---

## 40. The Same Herb Can Mean Different Things

The lecture uses **senna** as an example.

In Chinese medicine, senna is treated mainly as a **bitter-cold purgative** used for constipation and heat accumulation.

In Malay medicine, it may appear in much broader health recipes and be associated with cleansing or restoring balance.

This demonstrates that:

```text
same plant
≠
same medical meaning
```

Medicinal knowledge depends on the **theoretical system** in which the plant is used.

# Part IV — Exchange of Medical Knowledge and Materia Medica

## 41. Southeast Asia and China Were Connected for Centuries

Medical exchange between China and Southeast Asia did not begin only in modern Singapore.

Trade, diplomacy and maritime travel circulated:

- Herbs
- Spices
- Minerals
- Animals
- Medical knowledge

The voyages associated with **Zheng He** are one example of these long-distance exchanges.

Chinese records also described Southeast Asian medicinal substances long before the modern period.

---

## 42. Southeast Asian Drugs Enter Chinese Materia Medica

Some plants used in Chinese medicine originated in Southeast Asia and entered Chinese pharmacological knowledge through trade.

One example discussed in the lecture is **pang da hai 胖大海**, a tropical Southeast Asian plant later recorded in Chinese materia medica.

This reminds us that Chinese materia medica was historically **open and expanding**, rather than limited only to substances native to China.

---

## 43. Cross-Linguistic Transmission

The lecture introduces the **Polyglot Medical Traditions in Southeast Asia** project.

Its purpose is to study how medicinal knowledge moved across languages and communities by comparing sources such as:

- Chinese medical texts
- Malay manuscripts
- Peranakan recipes
- Other Southeast Asian medical traditions

Similarities in drug names across languages can reveal the routes through which knowledge travelled.

Thus medical history can be reconstructed through:

```text
language
+ trade
+ migration
+ recipes
+ materia medica
```

---

## 44. Local Chinese Medical Halls Used Local Plants

A 1929 study by a British botanist examined **456 medicinal samples** collected from Chinese medical halls in Malaya.

The lecture notes that **48 products** were used in local Chinese medicine even though they were neither imported from China nor commonly used there.

This is strong evidence of localisation.

```text
Chinese theory
+
Southeast Asian plants
=
locally adapted Chinese medicine
```

Local practitioners could therefore preserve the **Chinese theoretical framework** while changing the actual materia medica.

---

## 45. Malay Medicine Also Borrowed from Chinese Medicine

Exchange worked in both directions.

Some Malay plant names explicitly refer to China, suggesting historical association with Chinese materia medica.

Examples discussed include:

- Chinese licorice
- Chinese rice wine used in particular remedies
- Plants whose Malay names contain “China”

However, even when the same plant was borrowed, the **use could change**.

The receiving tradition reinterpreted the substance according to its own theories.

---

## 46. Exchange Does Not Mean Simple Copying

Cross-cultural medicine should not be understood as:

```text
Tradition A
→ directly copied by
Tradition B
```

Instead:

```text
medical idea / herb / technique
        ↓
crosses cultural boundary
        ↓
reinterpreted through new theory
        ↓
new local meaning and use
```

This is why the same herb may have similar, partially similar or completely different applications across Chinese and Malay medicine.

---

## 47. Main Historical Forces Shaping Chinese Medicine in Singapore

Chinese medicine in Singapore was shaped by several interacting forces:

```text
Migration from China
        +
Colonial healthcare limitations
        +
Chinese charitable institutions
        +
Professional associations
        +
Singapore law and regulation
        +
Tropical climate
        +
Local foods and plants
        +
Multi-ethnic interaction
        +
Modern biomedical dominance
        ↓
Singapore Chinese Medicine
```

It is therefore best understood as a **local medical tradition produced through adaptation**, rather than merely an overseas copy of Chinese medicine in China.

---

| Condition | Main Chinese medical interpretation emphasised in lecture |
|---|---|
| **Tuberculosis** | Lung consumption; climatic factors + emotional factors + pestilential qi → qi problems → phlegm |
| **Flu / common cold** | Differentiate cold damage vs warm disease |
| **HFMD** | Warm disease / epidemic toxin with varying damp-heat severity |
| **Eczema** | Wind + dampness + heat + Spleen deficiency |

## 50. Comparison with Malay Medicine at a Glance

|---|---|---|

| Cupping | Mainly dry | Wet / hijama |

# Core Idea

> [!ABSTRACT]
> **Chinese medicine in Singapore is a localised form of Chinese medicine shaped by migration, climate, law, community institutions, multi-ethnic exchange and the rise of biomedicine.**
>
> **Chinese medicine from China**
>
> ↓
>
> **Migrants bring medical knowledge and charitable institutions**
>
> ↓
>
> **Colonial Singapore provides limited public healthcare**
>
> ↓
>
> **Chinese medical halls, Thong Chai and associations become important**
>
> ↓
>
> **Medicine adapts to Singapore’s tropical climate**
>
> ↓
>
> **Damp-heat, Spleen deficiency, warm disease and local materia medica become important**
>
> ↓
>
> **Interaction with Malay medicine and other traditions**
>
> ↓
>
> **Modern regulation + biomedical dominance**
>
> ↓
>
> **TCM becomes a regulated complementary medicine in modern Singapore**
>
> The key point is that Chinese medicine in Singapore did not remain unchanged after migration. It was **reorganised and localised** through Singapore’s climate, foods, plants, ethnic diversity, colonial history, professional associations and healthcare policies.
>
> A second key point is that Chinese and Malay medicine show both **similarities and differences**. Both emphasise balance, environment and prevention, but they explain the body and use therapies differently.
>
> Finally, local medical history shows that traditions are not isolated. **Herbs, techniques and ideas move across cultural boundaries and are reinterpreted in new settings.**
