 ## Daoism, Medicine & the *Huangdi Neijing*

## Reading 1 — Michael Stanley-Baker, *Daoism and Medicine*

### 1. Did Daoism and Medicine Come from the Same Source?

A traditional saying claims that **“Daoism and medicine emerged from the same source” (醫道同源, yidao tongyuan)**. Both can be traced back to Shang-period healing, when **wu 巫 (spirit mediums/diviners)** communicated with gods and ancestors about illness. Later healing included drugs, divination and exorcism. By the Han dynasty, medicine and emerging Daoist traditions also shared concepts such as **Qi 氣, Yin-Yang 陰陽 and the Five Agents 五行**.

However, this does **not** mean early medicine and Daoism were the same thing. Classical medical texts sometimes attacked spirit-medium healing; the *Huangdi Neijing*, for example, portrayed some incantation practices as outdated. Early Daoists themselves could also reject **wu** and even certain medical practices. The boundaries between **medicine, religion and ritual were therefore complex and changing**, rather than clearly separated.

> [!important]
> Avoid projecting modern categories such as **“science vs religion”** onto early China. Medicine, ritual, religion and self-cultivation often overlapped.

### 2. Eastern Han Crisis and the Rise of Daoism

The **Eastern Han (25–220 CE)** was crucial for both medicine and Daoism. This period saw the consolidation of medical classics and materia medica, growing interest in **transcendents (仙, xian)**, and the emergence of organised Daoist movements.

At the same time, the Han experienced political corruption, warfare, migration, famine and devastating epidemics. Epidemics could kill enormous proportions of local populations and were sometimes interpreted as **ghost-borne disease (鬼注, guizhu)** caused by restless or wronged dead. Daoism developed within this unstable environment and offered explanations and practices addressing health, morality, society and cosmic order together.

### 3. The Problem of *Ming* 命

Stanley-Baker describes the late Han crisis through the concept of **ming 命**, which can refer to **life, lifespan, destiny, command and cosmic or political order**. The collapse of political and social stability threatened all these dimensions simultaneously.

Different traditions responded differently. Medicine tried to regulate the body; self-cultivation sought longer life and independence; transcendents attempted to overcome ordinary human limitations; and early Daoist communities sought to restore moral, bodily and cosmic order.

This reflects a broader Chinese analogy:

**governing the body ↔ governing the state ↔ maintaining cosmic order**

### 4. Prevention and *Yangsheng* 養生

By the late Warring States and Han periods, **Yin-Yang, Qi and the Five Agents** provided a shared cosmological framework for understanding the natural world and body. The *Neijing* famously argues that the sage should act **before disease becomes manifest**, just as a good ruler prevents disorder before it becomes chaos.

This preventive ideal contributed to **yangsheng 養生 (“nourishing life”)**, which included exercise, diet, breathing, sexual cultivation and adjusting one's behaviour to daily and seasonal rhythms. The goal was not merely curing disease but **preventing illness and extending life**.

> [!important]
> A major ideal of early Chinese medicine is therefore **prevention before treatment**.

### 5. Medicine, Longevity and Transcendence

Medicine and transcendence occupied overlapping territory. Transcendents were believed to use special diets, herbs, minerals, talismans, spells and bodily cultivation to resist disease, dramatically extend life and potentially escape death.

The Han imperial catalogue even grouped **Medical Classics, Drug Formulae, Bedchamber Arts and Divine Transcendence** together under *fangji* 方技 (“recipes and techniques”). The *Shennong Bencao Jing* similarly ranked drugs: superior drugs supported longevity and prevented ageing, middle drugs strengthened the body, while lower drugs primarily treated disease.

The goals can therefore be imagined as a continuum:

**cure disease → prevent disease → strengthen the body → extend life → transcend mortality**

The techniques might overlap; what differed was often the **practitioner's goal and the effects claimed for the practice**.

### 6. Early Daoist Communities as Healing Communities

The **Celestial Masters (天師, Tianshi)** emerged around the second century CE and organised communities using ritual, morality and healing. Their practices included communal gatherings, grain contributions, fasting, purification and **confession of sins**.

Stanley-Baker notes that some of these practices could also have practical health effects: fasting and retreat could resemble dietary treatment, convalescence or temporary quarantine, while confession might provide psychological support. Daoist communities also provided infrastructure and food support during periods of migration and famine.

The **Great Peace (太平, Taiping)** movement similarly connected epidemic disease with political and moral disorder, combining religious practices with talismans, prayer, drugs and needles.

### 7. Daoist Explanations of Epidemics

Classical medicine mainly explained disease through the body's vulnerability to external environmental influences and internal emotions. It lacked a developed theory explaining **human-to-human transmission**.

Daoist theories filled this conceptual gap differently. Disease could result from sins shared across **individuals, families, ancestors and communities**, while ghosts could transmit punishment through *guizhu*. This helped explain why epidemics moved rapidly through populations and why apparently good people could become sick.

Stanley-Baker calls this an **“illness theodicy”**: a moral explanation for why illness could strike someone who appeared innocent. Daoist ritual confession then provided people with something they could actively do in response.

Despite their differences, Daoist and medical theories shared an important assumption: **the universe was ordered and predictable**, meaning illness could be understood and managed.

## 8. Blended Health Practices

Many techniques commonly labelled “Daoist” actually circulated across different communities and were **not exclusively Daoist**.

**Daoyin 導引** involved stretching, movement and circulating Qi. It appeared before organised Daoism, was later adopted by Daoists, and eventually entered official court medicine.

**Fangzhongshu 房中術 (Bedchamber Arts)** used sexual cultivation for preserving vitality and preventing ageing. These techniques circulated widely and later became connected with Daoist cultivation and inner alchemy.

**Fushi 服食 (dietary practices)** could involve ordinary foods, herbs, minerals, alchemical substances, Qi and fasting. Different substances were believed to produce different bodily and spiritual qualities.

**Bigu 辟穀 (grain avoidance)** involved avoiding cultivated grains and sometimes relying on herbs or other substances. Besides spiritual purification and transcendence, its popularity may also have reflected the famine and social instability of the late Han: independence from agriculture could represent independence from an unstable social system.

**Fuzhou 符咒 (talismans and incantations)** addressed spiritual causes of disease. Although classical medical authors sometimes distanced themselves from such methods, ritual healing never completely disappeared from institutional medicine. The reading's images of bodily parasites (p. 409) and a healing talisman (p. 411) illustrate how bodily, spiritual and therapeutic ideas could coexist.

### 9. Is “Daoist Medicine” a Historical Category?

The modern term **“Daoist medicine” (道教醫學)** is useful for describing the many healing and self-cultivation practices associated with Daoists, but Stanley-Baker warns that it is a **modern analytical category**. Historical Daoists themselves did not use it in this way.

Calling something “Daoist medicine” can falsely separate medicine from ritual, religion or politics, even though historical practitioners did not necessarily recognise these boundaries.

> [!summary]
> **Daoism and medicine shared concepts, techniques and historical origins, but they were never simply the same system. Their relationship changed over time, and modern categories such as “medicine”, “religion” and “science” should be used cautiously.**

# Reading 2 — Stephen Boyanton, *The Canonicity of the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic*

## 10. Was the *Neijing* Always China's Main Medical Classic?

Today the **Huangdi Neijing 黃帝內經 (Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic)** is often presented as the foundation of Chinese medicine. Boyanton argues that this status was **historically constructed**. The *Neijing* was important early on, but it did not always possess the dominant position it later acquired.

The central question is therefore not simply **“What does the Neijing teach?”**, but:

> [!question]
> **How did the *Neijing* become a canonical medical text?**

“Canon” means a body of texts considered particularly authoritative, but this authority is not permanent or automatic. It is produced through historical institutions, technologies and social practices.

## 11. The *Neijing* Was Not Originally One Book

The earliest bibliographic reference to a **Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic** occurs in the *Hanshu*. However, several related works subsequently existed, including the **Suwen 素問, Lingshu 靈樞, Taisu 太素** and other compilations.

Modern research, particularly David Keegan's work, suggests that the early *Neijing* was **not originally a single fixed book**. Instead, it consisted of many short **“primary texts”** that circulated separately. Different people arranged these passages differently, producing the compilations that later became known as versions of the *Neijing*.

The discovery of medical manuscripts at **Mawangdui**, sealed in 168 BCE, supports this interpretation because some contain passages related to later *Lingshu* material.

> [!important]
> There was probably **no single “original Neijing”** identical to the book we know today. It emerged through compilation and rearrangement of earlier textual materials.

## 12. Apprenticeship and Textual Transmission

Early physicians generally learned through **master–student apprenticeship**. Students observed their teacher, received oral explanations and memorised short texts. When ready, they copied texts transmitted by their master.

Because different students received texts in different orders and sometimes from multiple teachers, their personal collections naturally differed. Later editors transformed such collections into more organised books.

This explains why *Neijing* compilations contain similar passages arranged in very different ways.

## 13. When Did the *Neijing* Form?

The Mawangdui manuscripts show that some ideas later incorporated into the *Neijing* existed before **168 BCE**, but the *Shiji*, compiled around 100 BCE, does not mention an Inner Classic.

Boyanton therefore presents roughly **100 BCE** as the earliest likely period when an *Inner Classic* compilation could have formed. Many of its primary texts probably approached their later form during the **first century BCE**, though individual passages may be older or younger.

## 14. Han Dynasty: Important, but Not Yet Supreme

During the Han, medical texts clearly possessed authority, but the *Neijing* was **one important collection among many**. The *Hanshu* lists it alongside other medical classics, while the *Shanghan Lun* also mentions *Neijing*-related works together with several other texts.

Its language also reflects the common analogy between **body, state and cosmos**. Concepts such as **Dao 道, shun 順 (compliance) and ni 逆 (rebellion/counterflow)** could describe both political order and bodily processes.

The Yellow Emperor himself strengthened this political symbolism because he represented ancient rulership and sage knowledge.

However, physical limitations also restricted textual standardisation. Han books written on **bamboo slips** were heavy, difficult to reproduce and vulnerable to damage. Large, identical editions could not easily circulate.

## 15. From Han to Tang: Scholars Reshape the Text

After the Han, elite scholars increasingly edited the *Neijing* into organised books. **Huangfu Mi (215–282)** compiled the *Systematic Classic of Acumoxa*, reorganising *Neijing* material because he considered the existing texts repetitive and disorderly.

Later editors such as **Wang Bing** similarly reorganised and corrected the *Suwen*. They believed the text transmitted the wisdom of the **Yellow Emperor and ancient sages**, giving it authority unavailable to ordinary contemporary physicians.

These editors were also imposing **elite literary expectations** on material that had previously circulated differently among physicians. What looked like textual “disorder” to a scholar may have reflected an older medical culture of flexible transmission.

Even during the Tang, however, *Neijing* texts remained **important works among several authoritative medical texts**, rather than the single foundation of medicine.

## 16. Song Dynasty: Printing Changes Everything

The major transformation occurred during the **Song dynasty (960–1279)**. Woodblock printing created cheaper books, allowed identical copies to circulate widely and dramatically expanded access to medical literature.

The Song government also became directly involved in medicine. Between **1057–1069**, the **Bureau for Editing Medical Texts (校正醫書局)** edited and published important medical works, including the *Suwen*, *Systematic Classic of Acumoxa*, *Shanghan Lun*, materia medica texts and other classics.

Government publication gave these books both **prestige and wide circulation**.

> [!important]
> **Printing + government selection + elite medical culture = major growth of the *Neijing's* canonical authority.**

## 17. Canon Formation Was Not Neutral

The texts selected by the Song Bureau were not necessarily chosen because everyone universally agreed they were the “best”. A **small group of influential editors** played a major role in deciding which works received official publication.

Printing preserved selected texts but could simultaneously cause other versions to disappear. Once an authoritative, inexpensive printed edition existed, there was little reason to continue copying alternative manuscripts.

Therefore:

**canonisation preserves some texts → but also excludes and marginalises others**

The survival of today's medical classics partly reflects these historical decisions rather than simply their intrinsic superiority.

## 18. Medicine Becomes an Elite Scholarly Activity

During the Song, medicine also became increasingly respectable among the **literati elite**. Literati physicians brought scholarly habits of reading, textual criticism and citation into medicine.

Earlier medical authors often collected many different treatments without treating one text as supreme authority. Song physicians increasingly **cited authoritative classics to justify their own arguments**.

As a result, the medical canon became **smaller but more influential**. The *Neijing* changed from being one respected text among many into one of a limited group of essential texts that serious physicians were expected to engage with.

## 19. Canonicity Is a Historical Process

Boyanton's central argument is that **canonicity is not fixed**. The *Neijing* did not automatically become authoritative because it contained timeless medical truth. Its status developed through changes in:

**textual transmission → editing → book technology → printing → government institutions → elite medical culture**

Social, economic and technological change altered both the physical text and the kind of authority people attributed to it.

> [!summary]
> **The *Huangdi Neijing* became canonical through a long historical process. Its modern status as a “foundation of Chinese medicine” was especially strengthened during the Song dynasty through printing, state-sponsored editing and the rise of literati medicine.**

# Connecting the Two Readings

Both readings challenge the idea that Chinese medicine developed as a **single, fixed system**.

Stanley-Baker shows that the boundaries between **medicine, Daoism, ritual, religion and self-cultivation** were historically fluid. Boyanton similarly shows that the authority of the *Neijing* was **constructed gradually**, rather than existing from the beginning.

Together, they suggest that Chinese medicine should be understood historically:

> [!important]
> **Chinese medicine emerged through changing relationships among medical practice, cosmology, religion, politics, texts and institutions. What later appears to be a unified “tradition” was produced through centuries of interaction, selection and reinterpretation.**
