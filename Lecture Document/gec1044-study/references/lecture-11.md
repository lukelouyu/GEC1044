## Lecture 11 — TCM outside China: History and Development in Hong Kong

[Recording](https://mediaweb.ap.panopto.com/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=38e1dfaf-434f-44eb-9c5b-b083009f0bf4)

### 1. Main argument

Chinese medicine's position in Hong Kong is presented as a result of **historical events, public trust, government policy, professional training, research and healthcare organization**. It is not a simple continuous rise or decline. Infectious-disease crises appear at several turning points: the plague reduced confidence in the older service model; SARS stimulated collaboration; post-COVID problems created further research questions.

The lecturer has clinical-research and integrative-medicine experience. The automatic captions are unreliable for the lecturer's name and several institutions, so those have not been guessed.

### 2. From community practice to marginalization

**2:04–8:41.** Chinese medicine initially spread through community practice, herbal businesses and charitable institutions. The lecture discusses early commercial branches and herbal shops, followed by the establishment of a Chinese-medicine hospital service for the Chinese community.

The **1894 plague** is presented as a turning point. The lecturer argues that inadequate results against plague weakened trust in Chinese medicine and pushed patients toward Western medicine. Western medical education, hospitals and registered practitioners subsequently expanded.

During the Japanese occupation, restrictions on free Chinese-medicine services further reduced its institutional role. The lecturer describes the eventual loss of the older inpatient Chinese-medicine service and survival through community clinics and herbal shops.

Migration of experienced practitioners from southern China and later Shanghai sustained practice, private teaching and professional associations. Different regional traditions interacted in Hong Kong.

**Caption caution:** several early business and hospital names, establishment dates and wartime dates are mistranscribed. The causal sequence is clearer than those details. Verify exact names/dates against the slides before using them in an examination answer.

### 3. Post-1997 professionalization

**8:55–13:05.** The lecture identifies the following milestones and changes:

| Date / phase stated | Development | Why it matters to the lecture's argument |
|---|---|---|
| 1998 | Hong Kong Baptist University full-time Chinese medicine/biomedical sciences double degree | Formal university training |
| 1999 | Chinese Medicine Ordinance | Regulatory framework |
| Subsequent years | Degree programmes at CUHK and HKU | Broader professional education |
| 2001 | HKBU Chinese medicine pharmacy undergraduate programme | Training for the medicines/pharmacy component |
| 2003 | First government practitioner qualification examination | Entry standards and professional recognition |
| SARS period | Collaboration between Chinese- and Western-medicine professionals | Catalyst for joint services and research |
| Later service development | Clinics/training/research centres across 18 districts | Community access plus workforce and evidence development |

The lecture describes a **tripartite model** joining the public healthcare/Hospital Authority side, universities and NGOs. These centres are more than dispensing clinics: they support training, research and development of service models.

**Objectives:** improve junior practitioner training; organize the knowledge base; develop evidence-based and standardized practice; manage the interface with Western medicine; and support participation in the wider public healthcare system.

These are historical developments as described in the recording, not an independently updated directory of services in November 2026.

### 4. Workforce development and research infrastructure

**13:27–20:30.** Scholarships and fellowships enable practitioners at different career stages to undertake more advanced clinical training. Examples span acupuncture, oncology, gynecology, stroke, dermatology, respiratory medicine, endocrinology, gastroenterology and cardiovascular conditions.

The lecturer links specialty development to disease burden and service need. Hong Kong's earlier practice categories are contrasted with more differentiated specialist training in mainland China. An e-learning platform supports continuing professional education beyond the university-graduate group.

Research capacity also requires **ethics review and funding structures**. The lecture describes Chinese-medicine input into research ethics arrangements and later commissioning/evaluation support. Institutional infrastructure is necessary for conducting clinical studies and cooperating with mainstream services.

### 5. Integrative medicine: three collaboration models

**20:50–28:29.** Early research examples include acupuncture studies and later professional organizations, international conferences and networks. SARS is again presented as a practical catalyst for joint care.

The most useful organizational distinction is:

| Model | How collaboration works | Main organizational requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-referral | Patients move between services, including consultation during hospitalization | Referral pathway and communication |
| Protocol-driven care | Agreed criteria identify suitable patients and define the intervention | Evidence review, eligibility criteria and shared protocols |
| Case management | Multiple disciplines discuss an individual patient's management together | Shared assessment, records and coordinated decisions |

**Key distinction:** having both systems available is not the same as integrating their decisions. Integration requires agreement about who treats what, when referral is needed, how information is shared and how risks are handled.

### 6. Making the interface workable

**28:44–36:54.** The lecture describes several supporting structures:

- Interface guidance for Chinese and Western medicine.
- Nursing guidance and acupuncture safety guidance.
- Evidence reviews and condition-specific programmes.
- Shared electronic records and access with patient permission.
- Standardized terminology and coding.
- Herb–drug interaction databases/guidance.

The herb–drug work is described as literature searching, critical appraisal, expert consensus and risk classification. Its practical purpose is to guide avoidance, monitoring or dose-related decisions when treatments overlap. The exact herb examples and counts are too garbled in captions to reproduce reliably.

The WHO collaborating-centre discussion connects local service experience with international policy and standards. ICD-11 is presented as an important step toward recording traditional-medicine categories in shared information systems.

**Interpretive distinction:** inclusion in a coding system supports classification and communication; it does not itself demonstrate that a treatment works. Likewise, no reported interaction is not equivalent to proof that an interaction cannot occur.

### 7. The Chinese Medicine Hospital project

**37:07–43:20.** The recording discusses a planned hospital, referring to **2025 as a future opening timeframe**. This wording reflects when the lecture was recorded. It is not a verified statement of the hospital's current operational status.

The hospital is presented as a flagship combining:

- Inpatient, day-care, outpatient and community services.
- Professional training and education.
- Clinical research and innovation.
- Collaboration across public/private and academic partners.
- Implementation of Chinese-medicine development policy.

The governance discussion includes an operator, board, management, Chinese- and Western-medicine leadership, nursing, finance, administration and human resources. Outpatient services range from general care and referrals to selected disease centres and specialist/private consultations.

The lecturer distinguishes **Chinese-medicine-only services, Chinese-medicine-led services and integrated services**. The institutional objective is not to make every encounter identical; different service models can coexist. Bed and room figures appear in the captions but should be checked against the slides before memorizing them.

### 8. Long COVID as a clinical-research example

**43:39–49:16.** Long COVID is introduced as persistent problems after infection, with an approximately three-month reference point in the lecture. It is described as multisystem rather than purely respiratory.

Examples include fatigue, sleep and cognitive problems, psychological symptoms, breathlessness, cough, chest symptoms, gastrointestinal complaints and other organ-system effects. The lecturer emphasizes uncertain mechanisms, heterogeneous presentations and limited established treatment evidence as challenges at the time of recording.

**Research question:** can Chinese medicine improve persistent post-COVID symptoms and recovery measures?

**Study description, 49:22–53:00:** approximately 150 participants, several months of treatment/follow-up, monthly assessments, a retrospective medical-history component and prospective observation. The lecture describes symptom, TCM-pattern/constitution and lung-function-related assessments. The matching paper clarifies that the latter include six-minute walk performance and the Lung Function Questionnaire; these should not be labelled automatically as spirometry.

The captions call the work “randomized” at one point, but the matching published report is explicitly observational. **External research resolves the caption ambiguity: treat the matching 150-person cohort as observational, not as a confirmed randomized trial.** See the additional-research section below for the primary paper and its correction.

The lecturer reports improvement in persistent symptoms, particularly gastrointestinal complaints, lung-function measures and the proportion classified as having a balanced constitution. These are the lecturer's reported findings. Without a verified design, comparator, effect sizes and bias assessment, they do not establish how much improvement was caused by treatment rather than natural recovery or other factors.

### 9. Medicine–food homology and future research

**53:10–1:01:10.** The lecture connects post-COVID research with the idea that some materials serve both food and medicinal roles. It proposes relating ingredients to affected body systems and investigating possible mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and other biological activity.

The development pathway described is:

**Historical texts and clinical experience → select candidate food materials → laboratory screening → identify active constituents/functions → optimize processing → develop and evaluate products.**

Research is presented as ongoing. Mechanistic plausibility or laboratory activity is not the same as demonstrated clinical benefit. The recording also uses broad language suggesting medicinal foods have no side effects; that should be recorded as the lecturer's claim, not accepted as a general safety conclusion. Food-derived products can still have contraindications or interactions.

### Additional research — identifying the study and updating hospital status

**Published study matching the lecture's description.** Zhong and colleagues' 2022 paper is explicitly a **multicentre observational study**, with 150 discharged Hong Kong patients receiving three to six months of individualized treatment. Its combination of retrospective history and prospective observation closely matches the lecture. Reported improvements are associations over follow-up; without a randomized untreated comparison, natural recovery and other influences remain alternative explanations. The study measured six-minute walk performance and the Lung Function Questionnaire, among other outcomes; those are not the same as spirometry or direct evidence of repaired lung tissue. [R16: Zhong et al., 2022, primary report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35996191/)

**Use the corrected publication.** An October 2022 correction changed numerical text and a figure, including a walk-test model coefficient and an LFQ-related statement. It did not convert the study into a randomized trial. This is a useful example of why checking the actual paper and its correction is preferable to relying on automatic captions. [R17: Published correction](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13020-022-00674-9)

**Current hospital status, checked 4 November 2026.** The lecture speaks of a future hospital opening. An October 2026 government update confirms that phased services began on **11 December 2025**, initially emphasizing outpatient and day-patient services. [R18: Hong Kong Government October 2026 update](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202610/21/P2026102100478.htm)

The announced next phase is **24-hour inpatient services from 11 December 2026**, which is still in the future at this check date. The August 2026 plan describes staged expansion rather than immediate use of the full planned 400-bed capacity. Keep the recorded plan, the services already operating and the announced future phase separate in an exam or report. [R19: Official August 2026 expansion plan](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202608/25/P2026082500323.htm)

**Connection to integration:** the study's combination of TCM pattern records and functional/quality-of-life measurements illustrates how systems can share research outcomes. Agreement on what to measure makes collaboration more interpretable; causal effectiveness still depends on an appropriate study design.
