## Lecture 1 — What is medicine? Beginnings of Chinese medicine

**Course sources:** supplied notes S1–S2 and Week 1 reading summary S9; [source index](Course_Source_Index.md#lecture-1-and-week-1). These are summaries of your Markdown files, not a newly checked video transcript. Reading arguments are attributed through those summaries; the original assigned chapters were not supplied.

### 1. The central problem: one biological species, many medical systems

The opening question is why biologically similar people develop different systems for interpreting illness. The course treats medicine as a combination of **observations, explanatory models, practices and institutions**. Patients trust interpretations of bodily experience as well as treatments. Cultural and political history helps explain why one medical account becomes authoritative.

The notes distinguish **healing**, the broad effort to relieve suffering, from **medicine**, a systematic attempt to explain normal and abnormal bodily states, predict their development and intervene. This is the lecture's analytical distinction, not a universal rule excluding all ritual or practical healing from medical history.

The model **living person = body + X** captures historical attempts to explain what makes physical matter alive. X can refer to soul, spirit, psyche or other concepts. The model describes historical reasoning; it does not establish an independently measured life substance or make these terms equivalent.

### 2. Observation requires interpretation

A physician connects signs to a model of bodily processes. The supplied notes use a *Shiji* physician story to illustrate recognizing disease before obvious symptoms appear. The point is expert interpretation and prognosis, rather than proof that the story records a verified diagnostic achievement.

**Observation → theoretical interpretation → diagnosis → prognosis → intervention.** A symptom such as fever and a disease process are different levels of description. Both historical traditions and modern medicine use concepts that go beyond what can be directly seen.

In the supplied account of **Foucault's medical gaze**, hospitals, clinical teaching and pathological anatomy reorganized observation and professional authority. A clinician selects medically relevant features from a person's experience. This explains the relationship between knowledge and institutions; it does not imply that clinical findings are arbitrary or that all clinicians disregard patients' lives.

### 3. Early healing: environment, ancestors and ritual

| Theme | Explanation in the supplied lecture notes | Historical lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Survival and hygiene | Drainage, shelter, ventilation and avoiding harmful environments | Health protection preceded formal medical theory |
| Shang healing | Oracle-bone divination investigated illness and possible ancestral displeasure; the king had ritual authority | Social and ancestral relations informed causation |
| Zhou developments | Heaven, ancestors and other spirits coexisted in explanations of misfortune | New frameworks supplemented older ones |
| Warring States healing | Demons, exorcism and aggressive therapies were described using attack/defence language | Warfare supplied metaphors for bodily disorder |
| Naturalistic accounts | Qi, blood, shen, organs, emotions and environmental influences became linked | Supernatural concepts could be reinterpreted within bodily processes |

Banpo's ditch and Hemudu's raised housing are examples used in the notes to discuss environmental adaptation. Do not infer a complete ancient disease theory or a single purpose for an archaeological structure from these examples alone.

**Research correction:** S2 and the Studocu PDF imply that prehistoric trepanation was absent from China. Lv and colleagues describe a Neolithic Fujia skull interpreted as evidence of trepanation with survival. The blanket absence claim is therefore not retained. The finding does not by itself establish the operator's purpose, so “expelling spirits” should not be assumed for every case. [R30: Lv et al., *World Neurosurgery*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23022649/)

### 4. From external spirits to internal resources

**Zang** are described through longer-term storage of vital resources; **fu** receive, transform and transmit. **Shen** becomes associated with consciousness and mental activity, especially through the Heart system. **Qi and blood** support bodily processes in this framework.

An internal deficiency can be compared with a breach in a city's defences. External influences become pathogenic in relation to bodily vulnerability. **Zheng qi** refers to the body's proper or healthy capacities; **xie qi** to pathogenic or disordering influences. The vocabulary retains political, military and moral associations even when used in naturalistic explanations.

The notes identify emotional excess as a possible internal cause within traditional theory. The conventional medical list includes joy, anger, worry, pensiveness, grief, fear and fright. This differs from the list in Lecture 10's cultivation context. These are historical medical frameworks, not evidence that people are morally responsible for becoming ill.

### 5. Week 1 reading — patterns, yin–yang and society

**Kaptchuk, as summarized in S9:** a patient's signs gain meaning through their configuration. The same biomedical disease can correspond to different TCM patterns, while one pattern can occur in people with different diseases. The stomach-disorder example illustrates the reasoning process rather than supplying a self-diagnosis checklist. The landscape-painting analogy emphasizes relationships among parts; the “web” image describes interacting processes without requiring one controlling thread.

The summary identifies five yin–yang relationships: **co-presence, mutual dependence, mutual restraint, transformation, and inclusion of the opposite within each aspect**. Yin and yang are relative categories, not two isolated substances or a requirement that every healthy variable stay at a fixed 50/50 value.

**Sivin, as summarized in S9:** scientific ideas, employment, patronage, political institutions and intellectual practices develop together. The **cosmos–state–body** relationship cannot be reduced to an apolitical theory later borrowed by government. The China/Greece comparison concerns different settings for argument and intellectual authority; it is not a ranking of civilizations or a claim that every thinker behaved identically.

**Read Week 1 beside Week 4:** Kaptchuk's disease/pattern contrast is a useful teaching model. Unschuld's later critique warns against turning it into an absolute opposition between causal Western medicine and non-causal Chinese medicine. Both traditions have internal diversity.

## Lecture 2 — Natural philosophy, organs and meridians

**Course sources:** S3–S4 and Week 2 reading summary S10; [source index](Course_Source_Index.md#lecture-2-and-week-2).

### 1. Natural order and systematic correspondence

The notes place the growth of natural philosophy amid political upheaval and debate in the late Zhou/Warring States world. Yin–yang, qi and the Five Phases offered ways to explain recurring processes shared by nature, society and the body. These developments coexisted with ancestral and spirit explanations.

**Systematic correspondence** organizes different domains through common relationships: seasons, directions, tastes, bodily systems and climatic qualities. **Microcosm** means a smaller system understood through patterns also attributed to a larger one. A body could therefore be interpreted simultaneously through cosmic cycles and political organization.

### 2. Yin–yang, the Yijing and the Five Phases

Yin and yang originally evoke shade and sunlight and develop into relative aspects such as inward/outward, cold/hot and still/active. Their meaning depends on the relationship being described.

The supplied notes present a cosmological teaching sequence from undifferentiated unity through two forms, four images and eight trigrams. **Broken lines represent yin; unbroken lines represent yang.** Three binary lines give eight trigrams, and two trigrams combine into one of 64 hexagrams. This summarizes a received interpretive scheme; it should not be read as proof that every stage, including the Wuji/Taiji formulation, appeared together in the earliest *Yijing*.

“Phases” or “agents” emphasizes processes rather than five fixed chemical ingredients:

| Phase | Seasonal image | Direction | Climatic association | Taste | Zang / paired fu | Tissue association in the supplied table |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | Spring growth | East | Wind | Sour | Liver / Gallbladder | Sinews |
| Fire | Summer expansion | South | Heat | Bitter | Heart / Small Intestine | Blood |
| Earth | Late-summer ripening | Centre | Dampness | Sweet | Spleen / Stomach | Flesh |
| Metal | Autumn gathering | West | Dryness | Pungent | Lung / Large Intestine | Skin and hair |
| Water | Winter storage | North | Cold | Salty | Kidney / Bladder | Bone marrow |

**Generating cycle:** Wood → Fire → Earth → Metal → Water → Wood. **Restraining cycle:** Wood → Earth → Water → Fire → Metal → Wood. Normal restraint maintains order within the theory; excessive restraint or reversal is a different concept. “Destruction” is not a suitable replacement for all restraint.

### 3. Zang-fu and the bureaucratic body

The *Huangdi Neijing* is a composite textual tradition conventionally discussed through **Suwen, Basic Questions**, and **Lingshu, Spiritual Pivot**, with the latter strongly associated with acupuncture. These are not simply writings personally composed by a historical Yellow Emperor.

The five zang and interior/abdomen are classified as yin relative to the fu and exterior/back classified as yang. Zang-fu name functional systems whose meanings extend beyond anatomical organs.

| Bureaucratic comparison | Systems and functions in the course account |
|---|---|
| Ruler | Heart-mind: shen, consciousness and coordination |
| Senior adviser and general | Lung: order/moderation; Liver: planning |
| Decision-making and envoy | Gallbladder: judgement; Danzhong/Pericardium: joy |
| Grain provision | Spleen and Stomach: food and flavours |
| Receiving and transmitting | Small and Large Intestines: processing and transformation |
| Skilled work and waterways | Kidneys: capacity/expertise; Sanjiao: fluid pathways; Bladder: storage |

**Xin, 心**, spans heart and mind. **Sanjiao, 三焦**, is discussed as a functional organization of upper, middle and lower regions rather than a single identifiable anatomical organ. The supplied table groups respiration/distribution above, digestion/transformation centrally and fluid/waste processes below; these are broad functional regions, not a modern anatomical atlas.

### 4. Six yin and six yang principal channels

The channel name combines **hand/foot + yin/yang division + associated system**:

| Yin channel | Paired yang channel |
|---|---|
| Hand Taiyin Lung | Hand Yangming Large Intestine |
| Foot Taiyin Spleen | Foot Yangming Stomach |
| Hand Shaoyin Heart | Hand Taiyang Small Intestine |
| Foot Shaoyin Kidney | Foot Taiyang Bladder |
| Hand Jueyin Pericardium | Hand Shaoyang Sanjiao |
| Foot Jueyin Liver | Foot Shaoyang Gallbladder |

The Pericardium adds a sixth yin-system channel to the five-zang scheme. The notes distinguish sovereign and ministerial Fire in explaining the expanded correspondences. Avoid treating “weak/strong Fire” as a simple measurement of intensity, or all uses of “six phases,” “six qi” and “six channels” as interchangeable.

Channels link bodily regions in the traditional model, so a selected acupuncture point need not lie directly over the anatomical organ named by its channel. This explains the model of treatment in Lecture 8; it does not independently establish a physical conduit or clinical efficacy.

### 5. Week 2 reading — Daoism, medicine and canonicity

**Stanley-Baker, as summarized in S10:** Daoist traditions and medicine shared techniques and concepts, while also contesting one another. The phrase “medicine and Daoism share a source” should not erase these changing boundaries. **Ming, 命**, connects life, allotted lifespan, destiny and command; political collapse, epidemics and bodily disorder could therefore be understood together.

Healing, prevention, longevity and transcendence could share practices while pursuing different goals. Examples include **daoyin** (guided movement), **fangzhongshu** (bedchamber cultivation), **fushi** (ingesting substances), **bigu** (grain avoidance) and **fuzhou** (talismans/incantations). These circulated beyond exclusively Daoist communities and are described historically, not recommended here. The Celestial Masters and Great Peace movements combined communal organization, moral accounts of illness and healing. An **illness theodicy** explains why suffering may strike people who seem innocent; it differs from identifying an infectious organism.

**Boyanton, as summarized in S10:** the question is how the *Neijing* became canonical. Short textual units, apprenticeship and divergent collections preceded later compilations. Editors such as Huangfu Mi and Wang Bing reordered inherited material; Song state editing and printing then increased particular editions' authority and circulation. Canon formation preserves selected texts and can marginalize alternatives. Its process is **transmission → compilation → editing → publication → citation and authority**, not simply age → automatic supremacy.

Exact composition dates remain contested and layered. A bibliographic reference, surviving passage, later compilation and printed edition are different events; the PDF's broad chronology and the reading's dating argument should not be fused into one exact authorship date.

## Lecture 3 — From Han to Tang, Song and Jin–Yuan; reading extension to Ming–Qing

**Course sources:** S5–S6 and Week 3 reading summary S11, with S10 for related textual history; [source index](Course_Source_Index.md#lecture-3-and-week-3). Ming–Qing and Warm Disease details below come from the reading summary, extending the lecture-note headings.

### 1. Early manuscripts reveal plurality

Mawangdui manuscripts show recipes, ritual techniques, prognostic interests and bodily cultivation alongside early vessel concepts. Their importance is that the fully integrated system familiar from later teaching cannot be assumed for every earlier source.

**Research correction:** do not write that Mawangdui contains no yin–yang or vessel theory. The excavated corpus includes texts conventionally titled *Cauterization Canon of the Eleven Yin and Yang Vessels*. A specialist historical study lists these alongside daoyin, cultivation and recipe texts. The defensible contrast is between diverse early configurations and later systematization, not total absence of relevant ideas. [R31: Li Yunxin, 2022, historical study and manuscript list](https://jas.hkbu.edu.hk/content/dam/jas-assets/publications/bulletin-of-the-jao-tsung-i-academy-of-sinology/issue-9/i9--04.pdf)

### 2. Formula medicine and classical theory

The supplied notes contrast partly distinct early traditions: canonical acupuncture theory associated with the *Neijing*, and collections of **fang, 方**, or prescriptions, including *Prescriptions for Fifty-Two Ailments*. This is a comparison of textual emphases and status, not an absolute rule that all elites rejected drugs.

The tradition attributed to **Zhang Ji/Zhang Zhongjing** links clinical presentations, illness progression and the patient's condition to named formulae. The later transmitted **Shanghan Lun** became particularly influential through Song and subsequent editing and interpretation. A useful distinction is **formula collection** versus **reasoned matching of presentation and formula**; neither ancient title should be treated as a modern clinical trial.

### 3. Religious exchange and Han–Tang synthesis

| Figure or development | Main contribution in the supplied account |
|---|---|
| Religious Daoism | Longevity/transcendence encouraged cultivation and investigation of substances |
| Ge Hong | *Zhouhou Beiji Fang*: accessible emergency and practical formula knowledge |
| Tao Hongjing | Editing and organization of materia medica |
| Buddhist exchange | Four-element/humoral models, healing deities, meditation and breathing practices |
| Sui–Tang institutions | Expanded state medical administration and training |
| Sun Simiao | Formula medicine and cultivation, incorporating several intellectual and religious influences |
| Wang Bing | Reorganized and commented on the Suwen; the notes date his edition to 762 |

Buddhist earth/water/fire/wind and Indian wind/bile/phlegm models are distinct from Chinese Five Phases. Practices were translated and adapted rather than transferred unchanged. Late Tang political persecution affected Buddhist institutions; this should not be converted into a claim that all Buddhist medical influence disappeared.

### 4. The Song transformation: books, scholars and government

The Song's significance lies in expanded institutions and circulation of knowledge. **Woodblock printing** made editions more reproducible and widely available. The **Bureau for Editing Medical Texts**, associated in the notes with 1057–1069, compared versions and published selected works, including the *Suwen*, *Shanghan Lun*, formula collections and materia medica. Selection and state publication helped create a shared canon.

The **ruyi, 儒医**, or scholar-physician ideal connected medical authority with classical learning, ethical responsibility and cultivated judgement. The growth of examination-based elites matters here, but access remained socially unequal; “democratization” means wider circulation, not universal literacy or equal participation.

Official formularies, dispensaries, subsidized medicines, training and epidemic responses expanded government involvement. These improved access and standardization while creating a tension: a formula attached mechanically to a disease label could overlook the patient's actual presentation. State medical institutions predated the Song; the argument concerns expansion and reorganization, not the first government interest in health.

### 5. Pattern differentiation and the four Jin–Yuan masters

Distinguish **symptom**, an individual manifestation; **bing, 病**, a named disorder; and **zheng, 证**, the current configuration of findings. Constitution, locality, stage, qi/blood and organ-system relations can change the pattern of the same disease. Pattern thinking had earlier roots; Jin–Yuan developments intensified and reworked it rather than inventing individualization from nothing.

The notes associate **Zhang Yuansu** with systematizing zang-fu, channels and diagnostic relationships, including **root, ben**, versus **branch, biao**. The eight-principle pairs are yin/yang, exterior/interior, cold/heat and deficiency/excess. Their later standardized formulation should not be attributed as a completed modern package to one earlier author without qualification.

| Master | Central emphasis | Therapeutic direction in the historical model | Memory cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liu Wansu | Pathogenic processes transforming into fire/heat | Cool and drain heat | Fire |
| Zhang Congzheng | Harmful influences accumulating in the body | Expel through sweating, vomiting or purging | Attack |
| Li Gao / Li Dongyuan | Spleen–Stomach damage from diet, exhaustion and emotions | Support the digestive centre and qi | Centre |
| Zhu Zhenheng / Zhu Danxi | Pathological ministerial Fire consuming yin | Nourish yin and regulate Fire | Yin |

These are competing emphases within a shared tradition, not four universal treatment rules. They show how inherited concepts could support different approaches to excess and deficiency. The supplied lecture also mentions **Wang Haogu's Tangye Bencao** as a later example of bringing materia medica into systematic-correspondence theory.

### 6. Week 3 reading — extension through Ming and Qing

The reading summary attributes to **Charles Chace** an account of repeated reinterpretation of classics. Song commentators **Pang Anshi, Zhu Gong and Cheng Wuji** made Cold Damage theory more organized, accessible and connected to classical channels and organs. Later reception shaped the practical meaning of an older text.

**Ming consolidation:** Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* organizes materia medica and natural knowledge; *Binhu Maixue* concerns pulse categories, and *Qijing Bamai Kao* the extraordinary vessels. The reading's 1578 date is a compilation milestone, not the usual date of the first printed edition, 1596. [R35: Métailié, historical publication study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/etchi_0755-5857_2006_num_25_1_892)

**Zhang Jiebin** develops Life Gate, primordial yin/yang and the relation between inherited and postnatal resources. These are historical physiological concepts. **Qing evidential scholarship, kaozheng**, emphasizes textual investigation and returning critically to earlier sources, alongside greater attention to local clinical experience.

**Warm Disease, wenbing**, develops through debate with and reinterpretation of Cold Damage approaches:

| Figure/framework in the reading | Main idea |
|---|---|
| Wu Youxing | Epidemics explained through distinctive pestilential qi; this is not an identification of microbes |
| Ye Gui / Ye Tianshi | Four levels: **wei → qi → ying → xue**, tracking the interpreted depth of pathogenic heat |
| Xue Xue | Damp-heat assessed through its location and relative predominance of dampness/heat |
| Wu Tang / Wu Jutong | Synthesizes and systematizes warm-disease approaches, including Sanjiao differentiation |

The models answer related questions about location, depth and changing presentation. They are not an obligatory linear biomedical disease course. The Week 3 account links Xue's regional framework with Wu's later synthesis; avoid making a simple claim that only one physician invented every use of Sanjiao.

**Historical pattern:** inherited classics → new problems and interpretations → competing approaches → further compilation and revision.

## Lecture 4 — Becoming Traditional Chinese Medicine

**Course sources:** S7 and Week 4 reading summary S12; [source index](Course_Source_Index.md#lecture-4-and-week-4).

### 1. Exchange preceded nineteenth-century confrontation

From the late sixteenth century, missionaries, merchants and scholars moved knowledge in both directions. Jesuit networks, including **Matteo Ricci and Xu Guangqi**, linked scientific translation and court patronage. **Michael Boym** helped present Chinese pulse and medical knowledge systematically in Europe; **John Floyer** adapted pulse ideas through European frameworks. Qing court translations, including Manchu materials, also introduced European anatomy, although court circulation did not mean immediate widespread adoption.

European responses combined admiration, selective borrowing and assumptions of superiority. **Orientalism** is used in the supplied notes to analyze images of an exotic, fundamentally different East. Receiving audiences interpreted medicine through their own expectations.

### 2. Biomedicine, public health and national reconstruction

The supplied account links changing biomedical authority to surgery, anaesthesia, pathological anatomy, laboratory research, germ theory and public-health institutions. These developments were uneven over time; the description does not mean every nineteenth-century intervention was effective or safe.

In late Qing and Republican China, national weakness and reform made health a political issue. **Wu Lien-teh's role in the 1910–1911 Manchurian plague** exemplifies isolation, masks and organized population-level responses in the course account. Public health linked medical expertise to the modern state's ability to protect populations.

**Chen Duxiu** represents New Culture criticism of traditional theories; **Lu Xun's Medicine** uses a failed blood-soaked-bun remedy to criticize suffering and credulity. A literary example is evidence of critique and representation, not a clinical study of an entire system.

Western-trained Chinese physicians and institutions helped make scientific medicine a Chinese national project. **Date clarification:** the notes' 1921 PUMC milestone concerns inauguration; China Medical Board's chronology distinguishes establishment in 1917 from the campus inauguration in 1921. [R32: China Medical Board institutional chronology](https://cmbfound.org/100th-anniversary/centennial-timeline/)

### 3. Abolition, preservation and reform

| Position | Basis of legitimacy | Main tension |
|---|---|---|
| Abolish/restrict | Scientific criticism and concern about harmful practice | Dependence on existing practitioners and limited alternatives |
| Preserve | National culture, accumulated experience and patients' needs | Historical prestige does not by itself demonstrate effectiveness |
| Reform/scientize | Investigate, reorganize and retain practices judged useful | Who decides the standards, and what is excluded? |

The **1929 restriction/abolition controversy** mobilized practitioners; the supplied notes place the Institute of National Medicine in 1931. These are episodes in an extended negotiation, not proof that either side won every debate.

The category **Chinese medicine, zhongyi**, became more sharply bounded through contrast with **Western medicine, xiyi**. Earlier healing traditions had included formulae, acupuncture, bone-setting, cultivation, ritual and local practices; a modern umbrella label should not erase that diversity.

### 4. PRC standardization and integration

After 1949, resource needs, political choices and scientific aspirations shaped support for Chinese medicine. The supplied account emphasizes organized colleges, standardized teaching, research and attempts at integration during the 1950s and later. Some practices were selected and reformulated; others were marginalized. Chinese medicine was neither simply preserved unchanged nor invented entirely from nothing.

**Modern TCM combines inherited concepts with modern institutions.** Competition, borrowing and cooperation with biomedicine can occur together. This prepares the institutional examples in Lecture 6 and the questions about modernity in Lecture 12.

### 5. Week 4 reading — Unschuld's “creative reception”

According to the supplied summary of **Paul U. Unschuld**, Western reception selected, translated and reorganized Chinese medicine through local cultural interests. A claim of uninterrupted antiquity can conceal layers of Chinese state standardization and Western reinterpretation.

| Interpreter or setting | Emphasis in the supplied reading | Unschuld's criticism, as summarized |
|---|---|---|
| Soulié de Morant | Acupuncture terminology, particularly “meridians” and qi as “energy” | Translation can substitute a European model for historically variable Chinese meanings |
| Kaptchuk | Patterns, relationships and holism | Chinese physicians also discussed causes; the contrast can be overstated |
| Porkert | Technical Latin/Greek vocabulary | Abstract precision may obscure political, military and moral imagery |
| Western visitors in the 1970s | Officially presented PRC medicine | Limited language/history access could make a selected contemporary system seem timeless |

**Ying/wei** can carry military imagery; **zheng/xie** can carry meanings of correct/deviant or orderly/disordering. Translation choices help determine whether readers see governance, morality, circulation or abstract energy.

The reading does not require rejecting every clinical claim. It distinguishes historical accuracy from therapeutic effects, and criticizes arguments that make claims immune to testing. Modern Western practice is plural: classical reconstruction, standardized TCM, spiritual interpretations and biomedical approaches coexist.

**Readings in dialogue:** Week 1 introduces Kaptchuk's pattern model; Week 4 asks what that model selects and leaves out. Retain both arguments with attribution. Lecture 12's concern with translation is their direct continuation.

## Lecture 5 — Chinese medicine in Singapore

**Course sources:** S8 and Week 5 reading summary S13; [source index](Course_Source_Index.md#lecture-5-and-week-5). The reading summary attributes its historical argument to Yan Yang.

### 1. The main framework: localization

Singapore Chinese medicine developed through **migration, tropical conditions, local substances, charity, professional organization, multi-ethnic exchange and state policy**. Everyday ideas about heat/cold and food exist alongside formal clinical services. Coconut water after vaccination is an example of a belief mentioned in the source, not an evidence-based requirement for vaccination recovery.

Perceived effectiveness, concern about side effects, family recommendations and dissatisfaction with previous care are reasons reported in the notes for choosing TCM. Survey percentages without a named study, denominator and year are not promoted here as current prevalence or proof of comparative safety. Lecture 12 identifies one historical CAM survey separately.

### 2. Community medicine and professional authority

Migrants and charities supplied care where formal provision was limited. **Thong Chai** exemplifies free treatment and medicines funded through community organization. Its examinations illustrate how institutions could establish reputations and select practitioners before modern state registration.

Professional associations linked **association + clinic + college + research institute**. Cases supported teaching; teaching sustained the profession; research and rules supported legitimacy. The supplied reading highlights **SCPA, founded in 1946**, its training school in 1953, later connections with China and coordination among organizations in 1995. These are historical milestones from your notes, not a claim that present programmes retain every earlier arrangement.

### 3. Week 5 reading — state policy and two directions of modernization

| Period in the supplied historical account | Main development | Analytical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Colonial period, including 1905 framework | Asian therapeutics permitted while biomedicine had official priority | Pluralism with unequal institutional status |
| Japanese Occupation | Registration/examinations and control of herbal supplies | Regulation can serve political and resource-control goals |
| Post-war period | Expanding biomedical services and advertising controls | Increasing oversight of medical claims |
| Professional-association growth | Training, clinics, discipline and research | **Bottom-up modernization** |
| 1978 berberine restriction | Dispute over safety evidence and standards | Biomedical evaluation shaped regulatory authority |
| TCM Practitioners Act 2000 and later implementation | Statutory registration, examinations and professional discipline | **Top-down modernization** |

The contrast between bottom-up and top-down processes is a framework, not a perfectly abrupt before/after division: communities, professions and the state interacted throughout.

Current registration requirements and MOH's TCM Integrative Sandbox are described in Lecture 12. ## Lecture 7 — The Art and Science of TCM Diagnostics

Sources: [Part One, 24:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mh6CxGYLvtQ), [Part Two, 23:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZ6-nS-Tgco).

### 1. Overall purpose: disease diagnosis plus pattern differentiation

**Part One, 3:23–4:32.** TCM diagnostics studies the theories and methods used to identify disease and differentiate syndromes or patterns. The practitioner gathers observations, interprets the patient's current pattern of disharmony, identifies the disease, and combines these to plan individualized care.

The lecturer distinguishes two levels of explanation:

| Concept | Main question | Role in understanding the patient |
|---|---|---|
| Disease | What illness is occurring and how does it develop? | Cause, mechanism, course and likely outcome |
| Syndrome/pattern | What configuration of signs and bodily responses exists now? | Current nature of imbalance, affected systems and treatment priorities |

The same disease can appear with different patterns in different people. A patient's pattern can also change over the course of illness. A disease label alone therefore does not determine the TCM treatment described in this lecture.

The lecturer contrasts this approach with biomedical emphasis on pathogens, structural lesions and biological markers. Treat that contrast as an explanation of TCM's emphasis; it is not evidence that contemporary biomedicine never individualizes treatment.

### 2. The philosophical basis of diagnosis

**Part One, 4:35–8:54.** Ancient practitioners worked without modern imaging or laboratory tools. Their system linked the person to nature and treated body, mind and spirit as interconnected. External manifestations were interpreted as information about internal states.

Three ideas organize this reasoning:

1. **Infer the interior from the exterior:** observable signs are interpreted as expressions of internal change.
2. **Infer the whole from a part:** a localized observation, such as tongue appearance or pulse characteristics, is used to understand the wider condition.
3. **Know the normal to recognize change:** abnormality can only be recognized against a sufficiently familiar range of healthy variation.

The lecturer compares this general logic with biomedical observations such as cyanosis and palmar erythema. The similarity is the use of outward clues; it does not establish that every TCM sign has the same validation as those biomedical signs.

**Two practical challenges:** defining normality in a framework that includes constitution, environment and emotions; and developing the perceptual skill to detect deviations consistently. Pulse taking is used to illustrate the importance of extensive practice and observer subjectivity.

**Gardener analogy, 8:57–9:56:** yellow or wilting leaves can prompt investigation of sunlight, water, soil, roots and pests. Likewise, the TCM practitioner considers the surrounding system before interpreting an isolated symptom.

### 3. Three diagnostic principles

**Part One, 10:00–12:22.**

- **Holistic examination:** relate symptoms to disease history, mechanisms, constitution, qi and blood, yin–yang, family/social circumstances, seasons and climate.
- **Synthesize all four diagnostic methods:** collect broad information, identify the most informative findings and cross-check them against the remainder. One striking sign should not automatically override everything else.
- **Combine disease and syndrome:** disease supplies the longitudinal overview; syndrome describes the current configuration. Both inform the plan.

**Illustrative case, 12:22–13:31:** an older man with hypertension and persistent blood-pressure fluctuation was described as unusually nervous, with a TCM pattern of Liver qi stagnation and blood deficiency. The lecturer reports improvement after an individualized herbal prescription. The educational point is disease–pattern combination. This is a reported individual case, not a controlled demonstration of efficacy or a reason to alter antihypertensive treatment.

**Learning requirements, 13:34–15:30:** learn the foundational theories, obtain repeated clinical exposure, and develop logical reasoning that checks alternatives instead of relying on impressions or one-sided explanations.

### 4. The four diagnostic methods — 望闻问切

|---|---|---|
| Inspection, 望 | Visible presentation | Spirit, colour, form, posture, tongue, excretions |
| Listening and smelling, 闻 | Sounds and odours | Voice, speech, breathing, cough, breath and discharge odours |
| Inquiry, 问 | Reported experience and history | Symptoms, diet, emotions, lifestyle and previous illness |
| Palpation, 切 | Findings obtained through touch | Pulse, temperature, moisture, tenderness and texture |

#### Inspection: whole-body and local information

**Part One, 16:33–23:44.** Five inspection categories are introduced: general appearance; local regions such as face, limbs and skin; tongue; excretions; and pediatric finger inspection. The detailed discussion concentrates on general appearance and tongue diagnosis.

General appearance is organized into **spirit, colour, form and posture**:

- **Spirit (shen):** alertness, eyes, expression, manner, consciousness, emotional presentation and responsiveness. Speech can supplement the visual assessment.
- **Colour and lustre:** complexion and its brightness/dryness are interpreted in relation to qi, blood and organ-system function. The lecturer also describes facial regions associated with different systems.
- **Form:** build, strength, thinness, body proportions and constitutional features. A historical classification uses five basic element types, each subdivided into five, giving 25 constitutions.
- **Posture:** sitting, lying, standing, walking, changes in movement and abnormal positioning; examples include convulsions, imbalance and impaired coordination.

|---|---|---|

The lecture combines colour, facial region and five-element relationships. These associations belong to the traditional interpretive system and are not stand-alone biomedical diagnoses.

### 5. Turning observations into patterns: the eight principles — 八纲

**Part Two, 13:22–19:17.** The lecture names several complementary systems, including eight-principle, disease-nature, organ-system and six-meridian differentiation. One additional system is unclear in the captions and is not reconstructed here.

The eight principles comprise four paired dimensions:

| Pair | Main question | Features/examples in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| Yin / yang | What is the broad nature of the pattern? | Yin: quieter, suppressed, colder/damper, darker; yang: more agitated, active, hotter, brighter/redder |
| Exterior / interior | Where or how deep is the pattern? | Exterior: surface-associated symptom complexes; interior: deeper organ/tissue involvement |
| Cold / heat | What thermal nature characterizes it? | Cold: chills, cold sensation, clear excretions; heat: thirst, flushing, restlessness, increased warmth, yellow/dry excretions |
| Deficiency / excess | What is the relationship between bodily resources and pathogenic factors? | Deficiency emphasizes weakness of healthy qi; excess emphasizes pathogenic predominance |

**Yin–yang is the overarching classification**, incorporating aspects of the other three pairs. These are interacting dimensions, not eight unrelated diagnoses.

Exterior and interior can coexist, and an illness can change from an exterior presentation to deeper involvement. The lecturer illustrates this with an acute respiratory illness and a persistent or more severe lower-respiratory presentation. This is a TCM classification example, not a universal progression of every cold.

Healthy or upright qi is used to describe defensive and regulatory capacity. Pathogenic qi refers to disease-producing influences in the TCM framework. Their changing relationship helps explain progression and recovery within that model.

### 6. Mixed patterns and the worked case

**Part Two, 19:20–21:44.** A menopause-related example illustrates simultaneous heat manifestations and deficiency: hot flushes, irritability and night sweating may be discussed alongside cold sensations or other deficiency signs. The four pairs are not mutually exclusive boxes.

In the final case, the reported findings include nervousness, a reddish tooth-marked tongue, hot flushes/night sweats and a wiry, thin pulse. The lecturer interprets these as an **interior pattern with qi and yin deficiency and heat manifestations**, with further organ-system differentiation suggested by the pulse.

The reasoning sequence matters more than memorizing a single label:

**Gather findings → cross-check the four methods → classify the pattern → combine it with disease history → individualize the plan.**

### Additional research — reproducibility is different from diagnostic validity

**A directly relevant reliability study.** Wang and colleagues developed standardized tongue-coating definitions and a smartphone photography protocol using a quasi-Delphi process involving 18 clinicians. In the selected-image assessment, agreement between clinicians was moderate for coating colour/texture (Gwet AC2 0.49–0.55) and fair for tongue-body colour/features (0.34). Some same-observer comparisons were stronger, but reliability varied by feature. This supports Lecture 7's emphasis on training and standardization while showing why observers cannot be assumed to agree perfectly. [R4: Wang et al., 2020, diagnostic-reliability study](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/7/e16018)

Two different questions must be distinguished:

| Question | Meaning | What would need to be tested? |
|---|---|---|
| Reliability | Do repeated observers or measurements produce similar descriptions? | Agreement under standardized conditions |
| Validity/accuracy | Does the observation identify the intended clinical condition? | Comparison against an appropriate reference standard in a representative population |
| Clinical usefulness | Does using the observation improve decisions or outcomes? | Evaluation of the resulting care pathway |

The table is a methodological explanation. The tongue-image study tests reproducibility; it does **not** establish that a purple tongue diagnoses a particular disease or that an organ-area map predicts treatment benefit. A technology can standardize image capture without validating every interpretation applied to the image.

**Connection to the four methods:** combining history, inspection and other findings is sensible information gathering, but the combined method still needs its own evaluation. More observations do not automatically remove measurement error. This is an inference from the distinction above, not a result reported by the lecturer.

### Additional research — clinical effects, mechanisms and cupping evidence

**Clinical pain evidence.** Vickers and colleagues' 2018 individual-patient-data analysis pooled 39 randomized trials with 20,827 participants with chronic pain conditions. Acupuncture performed better than no-acupuncture controls and sham controls; the average difference was larger against no acupuncture (about 0.5 standard deviations) than against sham (about 0.2). The result concerns the studied pain conditions and treatment packages; it does not validate every condition or point indication listed in the lecture. [R5: Vickers et al., 2018, original pooled trial analysis](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/output/409834)

**Why the comparator matters.** A no-acupuncture comparison estimates the effect of adding the whole treatment encounter, including attention and expectations. A sham comparison asks a narrower question, although sham procedures can themselves stimulate tissues. NCCIH discusses nervous-system, local-tissue and nonspecific effects and notes that the mechanism is not fully understood. These explanations complement, rather than experimentally prove, the lecture's qi/meridian account. [R7: NCCIH mechanism and effectiveness summary](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/acupuncture-effectiveness-and-safety)

**A specific experimental mechanism.** Liu and colleagues investigated electroacupuncture in mice and identified a role for PROKR2-marked sensory neurons in recruiting a vagal–adrenal pathway under particular hindlimb stimulation conditions. This provides a testable neuroanatomical mechanism for a specific experimental intervention. It is **animal mechanistic evidence**, not a human treatment trial or a demonstration that all meridian claims are correct. [R6: Liu et al., Nature, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9178665/)

**Cupping needs a separate evidence assessment.** NCCIH describes possible pain relief but weak evidence and predominantly low-quality research; it does not establish broad benefits for other conditions. The agency also documents burns, infection, scarring and other adverse effects. Nothing in that assessment validates cup-mark colour as a measure of toxins or clot burden. The lecture's cupping-mark chart should therefore remain labelled as traditional interpretation. [R8: NCCIH cupping assessment](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cupping)

**How to read an acupuncture claim:** identify the condition, intervention, comparison, outcome and follow-up. A finding about pain scores is not automatically a finding about disease cure, and a statistically detectable difference may be small. Those are research-reading principles, not additional results from any one trial.

### Additional research — diet evidence and the food–medicine boundary

**Diet can affect disease outcomes, but the tested pattern matters.** The republished PREDIMED analysis included 7,447 adults at high cardiovascular risk. Mediterranean-diet groups supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts had fewer major cardiovascular events than the comparison diet. The republication addressed allocation irregularities using revised analyses. This is evidence about a specified dietary intervention and population; it does **not** test the five-taste/organ correspondence table. [R9: Estruch et al., NEJM, 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)

**A modern comparison with “harmony.”** WHO describes healthy diets through adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity. Its guidance emphasizes whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses; for people older than ten it specifies at least 400 g of fruit/vegetables daily, and for adults less than 5 g of salt daily. This makes an important distinction for the lecture: a traditional “salty” category is not a recommendation for high sodium intake. A range of colourful foods can support variety without establishing that each colour selectively nourishes one anatomical organ. [R10: WHO healthy-diet guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)

**Oldenlandia: what the cancer evidence actually supports.** Memorial Sloan Kettering's specialist monograph distinguishes laboratory/animal anticancer findings from a lack of human evidence sufficient to confirm safe and effective use. It also notes product-quality and substitution concerns. The lecture example illustrates traditional use and a research hypothesis, not an established cancer treatment. [R11: MSK clinical herb monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/oldenlandia-diffusa)

**Cocklebur: historical praise does not establish safety.** Hong Kong Hospital Authority identifies the whole plant, particularly the fruit, as poisonous. This directly qualifies the historical passage on Su Shi's slide describing edibility and lack of poisoning. Preserve the passage as a record of historical belief, not as contemporary food advice. [R12: Hospital Authority clinical-toxicology atlas](https://www3.ha.org.hk/toxicplant/en/xanthium_sibiricum.html)

**How to evaluate a medicinal-food claim:** specify botanical identity, plant part, preparation, amount, duration, patient population and outcomes. “Used traditionally,” “active in cells,” and “improves outcomes in patients” are different evidence levels. This is a methodological synthesis of the sources above. A formula role such as chief or assistant explains intended design; it does not replace testing of the final combination.

## Lecture 10 — Chinese Ideas of Health Preservation & Ageing

[Recording](https://mediaweb.ap.panopto.com/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=620237df-0fd0-4b8d-9264-b37800d0b846)

**Source: screenshots of all 16 sidebar entries.** These notes cover the visible concepts, historical texts and exercise list. They do not reconstruct uncaptioned narration or a complete movement demonstration. The title slide retains the older module code GEH1070.

### 1. What yangsheng means

**0:15–3:06.** **养 yang** means cultivating, preserving or nourishing; **生 sheng** refers to life/health. **养生 yangsheng** is presented as maintaining health, preventing illness and extending life.

The philosophical basis connects humans with heaven, earth and the natural environment. Qi transformation and the changing relationship of yin and yang are used to explain bodily function and change. The slide also notes that the term yangsheng is widely used and can be misused: a commercial label alone does not define the historical concept.

The health-preservation slide distinguishes:

| Dimension | Chinese term | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Cultivating the body | 养身 | Physical condition and bodily practice |
| Cultivating the mind | 养心 | Mental/emotional cultivation |
| Cultivating character | 养性 | Disposition and conduct |
| Cultivating the spirit | 养神 | Spirit/shen |

Note that 养身 and 养神 may look similar in romanization but have different characters and meanings. A second set of concepts is **精 jing (essence), 气 qi (rendered “breath” on this slide), 神 shen (spirit)**. The two lists are related teaching frameworks, not identical categories.

### 2. Why the mind is the starting point

**11:30.** The slide names **joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hate and desire** as seven emotional activities. This is the particular list used in this lecture; do not silently replace it with a different seven-emotion list from another source.

The argument has three steps:

1. Emotional activity is normal and can remain within bodily regulation.
2. Personality, prolonged emotional immersion, sudden shock or violent changes may exceed that capacity; emotions then become excessive passions and potential causes of disease within the framework.
3. TCM links those passions to zang–fu systems and relates a person's tolerance to qi, blood and constitution.

**Key distinction:** emotion itself is not automatically pathological. The lecture focuses on intensity, duration, sudden change and the individual's capacity to regulate and tolerate it.

### 3. Ji Kang and aspirations for longevity

**20:03.** Ji Kang, 嵇康 (220–265 CE), illustrates historical beliefs in the possibility of cultivating exceptional longevity or immortality. The slide associates failure to attain it with inadequate guiding of qi and cultivation of life, 导气养生.

These statements are **historical beliefs being taught**, not evidence that immortality or a lifespan of 120 years can be reliably produced through a practice. Their significance is the older view that lifespan and health could be influenced through disciplined cultivation.

### 4. Daoyin: guiding qi and stretching the body

**22:06.** **导引 daoyin** is explained as guiding qi (导) and stretching the body (引), and as a forerunner of qigong. It brings together:

- Mental awareness.
- Controlled breathing.
- Slow physical movement.

The practice belongs to a worldview involving qi, dao, yin–yang and the five elements. The slide connects bodily development, health and spiritual attainment; it is broader than exercise understood only as muscle work.

**25:30–28:51. Mawangdui and the Daoyin Tu.** The archaeological slides show a tomb/coffin and a chart of people in varied postures. They associate the finds with Mawangdui in Changsha, Hunan, and a Han-dynasty tomb dated 168 BCE. The chart is presented as evidence that breathing and bodily cultivation practices have a long history.

**What the evidence supports:** the existence and historical form of such practices. The age of an illustration does not itself prove the effectiveness of every exercise or later therapeutic claim. The chart has many postures, but the slides do not supply a safe, complete modern instruction sequence for each one.

### 5. Ageing as a problem of maintaining balance

**29:54.** The slide defines good health through holistic equilibrium and emphasizes prevention. It distinguishes **harmony within the individual** from **harmony between the individual and the outside world**.

### Additional research — historical evidence and a modern Singapore trial

**A historical correction worth retaining.** Wellcome Collection's catalogue identifies the exercise chart with **Mawangdui Tomb 3**, sealed in 168 BCE. This is more precise than associating the chart generally with the Lady Dai tomb complex, as the slide does. The source supports the chart's archaeological provenance and historical exercise context, not the efficacy of a modern reconstructed regimen. [R15: Wellcome catalogue record](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/sybqrqu7)

**A local Baduanjin trial.** Tou and colleagues conducted an assessor-blind randomized study at three Singapore senior activity centres. The analysis included 56 older adults; supervised Baduanjin ran for 16 weeks, two to three one-hour sessions weekly, against a wait-list/usual-activity control with monthly health education. No statistically significant overall between-group differences were found at four months. Attendance averaged 81.3%, no major adverse events were reported, and a high-attendance exploratory analysis suggested an exhaustion benefit. The appropriate conclusion is feasibility and acceptability with uncertain effectiveness on the main outcomes—not proven reversal of frailty or life extension. [R13: Tou et al., 2024, randomized trial](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s11556-024-00363-6)

**Why the exploratory result is weaker:** selecting people by attendance after randomization may select for motivation or better health. It is useful for generating hypotheses, but it is not equivalent to the main randomized comparison. This is a research-design interpretation of the reported analysis.

**How the exercise idea connects to current guidance.** WHO recommends regular activity for older adults, including 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly (or an equivalent vigorous combination), strengthening at least twice weekly, and varied activity emphasizing functional balance and strength on at least three days. A Baduanjin session's contribution depends on its actual intensity and content. These guidelines support attention to movement and function; they do not establish immortality or a specific lifespan from the historical texts. [R14: WHO activity recommendations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566046/)

## Lecture 11 — TCM outside China: History and Development in Hong Kong

Chinese medicine's position in Hong Kong is presented as a result of **historical events, public trust, government policy, professional training, research and healthcare organization**. ### 2. From community practice to marginalization

**2:04–8:41.** Chinese medicine initially spread through community practice, herbal businesses and charitable institutions. The lecture discusses early commercial branches and herbal shops, followed by the establishment of a Chinese-medicine hospital service for the Chinese community.

The **1894 plague** is presented as a turning point. The lecturer argues that inadequate results against plague weakened trust in Chinese medicine and pushed patients toward Western medicine. Western medical education, hospitals and registered practitioners subsequently expanded.

### 5. Integrative medicine: three collaboration models

The most useful organizational distinction is:

| Model | How collaboration works | Main organizational requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-referral | Patients move between services, including consultation during hospitalization | Referral pathway and communication |
| Protocol-driven care | Agreed criteria identify suitable patients and define the intervention | Evidence review, eligibility criteria and shared protocols |

### 1. Four questions that frame the course — 2:12

The conclusion asks how Chinese medicine should be understood through four connected issues:

| Issue on the slide | What to explain in an exam | Connections to previous lectures |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine and the state | Who authorizes practitioners, organizes training, funds care and decides which knowledge counts? | Singapore services in Lecture 6; Hong Kong professionalization in Lecture 11 |

**Interpretive point:** Chinese medicine is a changing body of knowledge and practice shaped by institutions, translation, patients and politics. Studying it requires more than deciding whether it is ancient or modern. Likewise, integration can mean coordination of care without agreement on every theoretical concept.

### 2. Comparative history of science — 10:45

The slide challenges a historical habit of describing European knowledge as objective science while treating East Asian knowledge primarily as magic, mysticism or religion. It proposes comparing actual developments in fields such as astronomy, mathematics and the study of substances across regions.

Three distinctions make this argument precise:

1. **Historical understanding:** ask what a concept meant to its users and what problems it addressed.
2. **Historical comparison:** compare institutions, practices and standards of explanation in their own settings, including their exchanges.
3. **Clinical evaluation:** test whether a particular intervention produces meaningful benefits and acceptable harms today.

These are related but different questions. Criticizing Eurocentrism does not establish the effectiveness of a therapy. Conversely, failure of one modern clinical claim does not make a tradition historically unimportant.

**Additional historical research.** Joseph Needham's collaborative project made Chinese contributions visible within world histories of science. Nathan Sivin's introduction to the medicine volume also questions histories that treat every earlier discovery as a step toward a predetermined biomedical endpoint. This supports reading the course as a history of changing knowledge and care, not simply a list of ancient anticipations of modern discoveries. The correct bibliographic identification is *Science and Civilisation in China*, Volume 6, Part VI, *Medicine* (2000), by Needham with Lu Gwei-djen, edited and introduced by Sivin. [R20: Cambridge title pages](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/32621/sample/9780521632621ws.pdf); [R21: Sivin's introduction](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805216/32621/excerpt/9780521632621_excerpt.pdf)

### 3. Translation changes what we think we are comparing — 13:06

The slide identifies a mismatch between modern Chinese biomedical vocabulary and the terminology of historical Chinese medicine. A modern Chinese term for a microbe or hormone can refer to an internationally defined biomedical entity; that does not mean an older medical term had the same reference.

| Term or problem | Useful approach | Mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Qi, 气 | Preserve the term and explain its function in the particular passage | Treating it as automatically identical to energy measured in joules, oxygen or a hormone |
| Wanwu, 万物 | Explain the broad cosmological reference to the multiplicity of phenomena | Assuming its categories reproduce modern biological taxonomy |
| Zang-fu organ systems | Explain the traditional functions and relations | Substituting anatomical organs without qualification |
| Historical religion and philosophy | Examine how these shaped healing and cultivation | Removing them from the account merely because the modern course calls the subject medicine |

The slide raises a problem of translation, rather than proving that translation is impossible. A careful comparison states both the resemblance and the remaining difference. For example, a traditional description of digestive transformation may concern some experiences also studied by gastroenterology, while using a different explanatory structure.

The slide's comment about the absence of an authoritative comprehensive account should be read as the lecturer's assessment in context. It is not presented here as a verified claim that no authoritative reference exists in 2026.

### 4. CAM in multicultural Singapore — 28:24

CAM means complementary and alternative medicine. The slide presents high use in Singapore and distinguishes Chinese, Malay/Jamu and Indian/Ayurvedic traditions. This illustrates **medical pluralism**: people may draw on several systems within one society, and sometimes within the same episode of care.

**Additional research: identify the figures before memorizing them.** Lim, Sadarangani, Chan and Heng's 2005 survey used interviewer-administered questionnaires in one housing estate whose characteristics resembled Singapore's. It reported 76% using CAM over the preceding 12 months (95% CI 73.9–77.9%), with use reported by 84% of Chinese respondents and 69% of both Malay and Indian respondents. Its modality figures were TCM 88%, Jamu 8% and Ayurveda 3%. They describe the reported CAM-use mix, not percentages of all Singapore residents. The abstract alone does not resolve all modality denominator and multiple-use details. These are historical survey findings, not a current national census. The study also reported that 74% did not discuss CAM use with Western-trained doctors. [R22: Lim et al., 2005, primary survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15907674/)

**Additional research: terminology.** NCCIH distinguishes complementary care, used alongside conventional care, from alternative care, used in its place. Integrative care adds deliberate coordination across approaches. Thus, taking two treatments at once does not by itself demonstrate well-integrated care. [R23: NCCIH definitions](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/complementary-alternative-or-integrative-health-whats-in-a-name)

### 6. Why herbal products raise quality and safety questions — 34:12

The final substantive slide organizes distrust of Chinese herbal medicine around variability, uncertainty about constituents, and risks in production and supply.

| Stage or issue | What can vary or go wrong | Why this matters for evaluating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cultivation | Climate, soil and growing conditions | A plant name alone may not describe identical chemical material |
| Harvest and processing | Harvest timing, preparation and storage | Products described by the same traditional name may differ |
| Extraction and manufacture | Solvent, extraction process and formulation | Evidence for one preparation may not transfer to another |
| Identification | Wrong species or wrong plant part | The intended medicine and the supplied material may differ |
| Contamination | Unwanted metals, pesticide residues or microbes | Harm may arise from unwanted material rather than the intended constituent |
| Adulteration | Deliberate addition of undeclared substances | Apparent rapid benefit may have another explanation |
| Intrinsic constituents | Active and potentially toxic chemicals | A natural origin does not establish harmlessness |

**Terminology correction:** the slide lists cadmium, lead, mercury and arsenic together. Arsenic is a metalloid; pesticide residues are a separate contaminant category. Do not equate these categories or infer that every herbal product contains them.

**Additional professional research.** HSA requires dealers and listed Chinese Proprietary Medicines to meet relevant safety and quality requirements, including limits on toxic metals and microbial contamination and restrictions on undeclared synthetic drugs. Finished CPM products are a particular regulatory category; the rules should not be generalized indiscriminately to every raw herb or food. Listing and quality controls should not be read as clinical proof for every claimed indication. [R26: HSA safety and regulatory explanation](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/announcements/safety-precautions-when-buying-chinese-medicinal-pills-and-capsules/)

**Study synthesis:** distinguish the identity of the material, its quality, its potential toxicity, and its demonstrated clinical effectiveness. A product can pass one type of assessment without answering all the others.

## Self-test with brief answer guide

1. **Why combine disease and syndrome?** Disease supplies course/causes; syndrome captures the present pattern, allowing the plan to reflect the individual and the stage of illness.
2. **Why combine all four diagnostic methods?** Findings can be ambiguous, variable or observer-dependent; cross-checking provides a fuller basis for interpretation.
3. **Can heat and deficiency coexist?** Yes. The lecture's yin-deficiency example includes heat manifestations despite underlying deficiency.
4. **What are the eight principles?** Yin/yang, exterior/interior, cold/heat, deficiency/excess.
5. **How many channels are in Lecture 8's main teaching scheme?** Fourteen: twelve principal channels plus Ren and Du.
6. **How do back-shu and front-mu points differ?** Posterior versus anterior organ-associated point groups in the lecture's framework.
7. **What distinguishes wet from dry cupping?** Deliberate skin puncture/blood removal in wet cupping.
8. **What distinguishes referral from joint case management?** Referral transfers or requests care; joint case management coordinates decisions for the same patient across disciplines.
9. **Does ICD inclusion validate a treatment?** No. Classification and effectiveness are separate questions.
10. **Does an uncontrolled improvement establish treatment causation?** No. Natural recovery, concurrent care and other biases must be considered.

11. **What does Lecture 12 add to the clinical lectures?** A framework for examining state authority, modernity, translation, pluralism and trust.
12. **Does the 76% CAM figure measure current national prevalence?** No. It is a 12-month estimate reported in a 2005 housing-estate survey.
13. **Is standardization equivalent to proven effectiveness?** No. Identity, consistency, safety and clinical outcomes require different evidence.
14. **What is the difference between a historical herb grade and a formula role?** One categorizes a substance in a historical scheme; the other describes its function within a prescription.

15. **Why can Week 1 and Week 4 readings differ?** They make different interpretive arguments; a useful teaching contrast can become misleading when universalized.
16. **How does a text become canonical?** Transmission, editing, patronage, publication, teaching and citation help establish authority.
17. **What unites the four Jin–Yuan masters?** Reinterpretation of inherited theory for particular patterns, despite different clinical emphases.
18. **What distinguishes Singapore's modernization processes?** Community/professional organization from below and statutory regulation from above interacted.
19. **Does a modern institution make a tradition historically new in every respect?** No. Older concepts can be reorganized into new curricula and institutions.
20. **What did the full Studocu comparison add?** Visual details and revision links, with erroneous or overbroad claims corrected rather than copied.
